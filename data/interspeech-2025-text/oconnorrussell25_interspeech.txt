Visual Cues Support Robust Turn-taking Prediction in Noise
Sam O’Connor Russell, Naomi Harte
ADAPT Centre, School of Engineering, Trinity College Dublin, Ireland
russelsa@tcd.ie, nharte@tcd.ie
Abstract
Accurate predictive turn-taking models (PTTMs) are essen-
tial for naturalistic human-robot interaction.
However, little
is known about their performance in noise. This study there-
fore explores PTTM performance in types of noise likely to be
encountered once deployed. Our analyses reveal PTTMs are
highly sensitive to noise. Hold/shift accuracy drops from 84%
in clean speech to just 52% in 10 dB music noise. Training
with noisy data enables a multimodal PTTM, which includes
visual features to better exploit visual cues, with 72% accuracy
in 10 dB music noise. The multimodal PTTM outperforms the
audio-only PTTM across all noise types and SNRs, highlighting
its ability to exploit visual cues; however, this does not always
generalise to new types of noise. Analysis also reveals that suc-
cessful training relies on accurate transcription, limiting the use
of ASR-derived transcriptions to clean conditions. We make
code publicly available for future research.
Index Terms:
multimodal interaction, turn-taking, human-
robot interaction, noise
1. Introduction
Human turn-taking is a remarkable process. There is only 200
milliseconds of silence on average between speaking turns [1],
yet language production takes over 600 milliseconds [2]. Lis-
teners must therefore plan what they are about to say whilst their
conversational partner is still speaking [3]. Although effortless
for humans, turn-taking is highly challenging for robots. Most
turn-taking algorithms in use today are based on a robot react-
ing to silence after a turn [4], resulting in interactions that are
less fluid than those between humans [5, 6]. Predictive turn-
taking models (PTTMs) have been proposed to overcome these
limitations [7, 4]. PTTMs learn human-like turn-taking from
large corpora of human interaction, e.g. continuously predict-
ing whether a shift between speakers will occur [7, 8, 9, 5], akin
to human turn-taking [3].
Most PTTMs use speech features [4]. Yet in human-human
interaction, listeners make faster and more accurate turn-taking
decisions when they can see and hear a speaker [10].
Re-
cent work shows multimodal PTTMs, which also use gaze,
head pose and facial expression, outperform audio-only PTTMs
[11, 12], illustrating the known role of visual cues in turn-taking
[13, 10]. This reflects the many communicative advantages of
multimodality [14], which include boosting the intelligibility of
speech in noise [15]. MacLeod and Summerfield found that the
intelligibility gain from being able to see a speaker was equiva-
lent to an 11 dB increase in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) [15].
This motivates audio-visual speech recognition (AVSR), which
exploits visual cues to overcome the performance degradation
of automatic speech recognition (ASR) in noise [16].
This paper asks if multimodality confers similar perfor-
mance benefits to PTTMs in noisy environments. Surprisingly,
PTTMs have to date only been tested on interactions free from
background noise interference [4, 11].
The impact of noise
on PTTM performance is therefore unknown and unquantified.
This presents a major oversight as PTTMs will be deployed in-
the-wild, where they will inevitably encounter diverse sources
and levels of noise. We therefore present the first exploration of
PTTM performance in background noise, asking:
1. How is the performance of predictive turn-taking models af-
fected by background noise? and
2. Does the inclusion of visual features make multimodal
PTTMs robust to background noise?
This paper explores the crucial capability of a PTTM to
distinguish holds (no speaker change) from shifts (changes be-
tween speakers). In noise-free conditions, a multimodal PTTM
which incorporates visual features outperforms an audio-only
model (84% and 80% accuracy, Table 1). When we add ar-
tificial music, babble, and speech noise, the accuracy of both
PTTMs is poor (51% and 52% in music). Including noise dur-
ing training increases accuracy (61% and 72% in music). It also
‘primes’ the multimodal PTTM to better exploit visual infor-
mation (10 dB effective SNR gain over the audio-only PTTM).
We find this effect generalises to types of noises unseen by the
model during training. The inclusion of visual cues therefore
makes PTTM performance more robust to noise. We also in-
vestigate the reliance of PTTMs training on accurate transcrip-
tions. When noisy data is re-transcribed with ASR PTTM per-
formance collapses (50-54%). PTTMs training therefore relies
heavily on accurately aligned transcripts collected from noise-
free recordings, complicating the use of ASR in noisy data.
PTTMs are essential to facilitating interaction between hu-
mans and robots, but there is little understanding of how well
they could work in-the-wild. Part of the puzzle is understand-
ing how they handle noise and our work therefore represents a
significant step forward in PTTMs development.
2. Predictive turn-taking models
PTTMs continually predict future speaking activity, from which
turn-taking predictions can be made [7, 8, 17]. For example, if
speaker 0 is currently talking, but the model prediction favours
speaker 1, a shift is predicted. PTTMs are developed on corpora
of human-human interaction with the long-term eventual goal
of deployment to human-robot interaction. Our work considers
two-party interaction, where most work has been conducted [4].
We evaluate two PTTMs based on the state-of-the-art voice-
activity projection (VAP) approach introduced by Ekstedt et al.
[17] as it has been thoroughly explored [18, 9, 11]. VAP models
predict future speaking activity in 8 bins (4 per speaker). At

VAP models are neural networks consisting of stacked

The first model is an
audio-only VAP model which we re-implement from [19]. The
initial VAP model used mono audio and ground-truth knowl-
edge of the current speaker [17]; i.e. it knew when each turn
ended. The updated model used here takes stereo audio with
1 channel per speaker and removes the dependency on ground-
truth knowledge, reflecting a real-world deployment. We also
use a multimodal VAP (MM-VAP) model that incorporates fa-
cial action unit, facial landmark, gaze and head pose features
from OpenFace [20, 11, 12]. As the initial MM-VAP model also
depended on the ground-truth knowledge of the current speaker
[11], we similarly extended the model to support stereo audio
and remove this dependency [12]. For comparison, we also
train a video-only VAP model, identical to VAP with the audio
encoder removed [19]. Our implementations, along with full
architectural details are publicly available in our repository1.
PTTMs require labels of the start and end of each utterance
for training (the alignment). Manual transcriptions are typically
used e.g. the Switchboard telephone corpus [21, 17, 22, 5].
ASR more closely represents real-world deployments and has
been shown to work well in PTTM training [23, 24].
3. Data preparation
This work uses the Candor corpus [25], an 850 hr corpus of
1657 unscripted two-party videoconferencing interactions be-
tween speakers in US English. The average session duration is
34 min. Recordings are 60 fps video, 32 kHz stereo audio with
one channel per speaker. There is no controlled background
noise. Recordings take place at a location of the participant’s
choosing e.g. home offices. We transcribe with Amazon Tran-
scribe (EN-US model) which outputs word-level timings and
preserves most disfluencies [23]. We extract gaze, head pose,
facial action units, and facial landmarks with OpenFace [20].
We follow the AVSR literature by artificially simulating
noisy conditions, adding noise separately to each channel at
controlled SNRs [16]. Babble noise consists of 65,000 unique
30 speaker babbles which is synthesised from LRS-3 [26, 16]
(46 hr train, 14 hr test). Music noise is sourced from MUSAN
[27] (15 hr train from the Jamendo genre, 70 hr test). Interfer-
ence from other speakers (speech noise) is simulated by over-
laying another speaker from Candor, ensuring train/test separa-
tion.
In AVSR research, noise is typically added to isolated
speech segments. However, PTTMs are trained using conver-
sational speech which includes long pauses. A standard method
of adding controlled noise to conversational speech has not been
reported. We propose to add noise at a given SNR by consider-
ing signal energy during speaking times and then adding scaled
noise to the complete recording. Natural variation in amplitude
means this yields an average SNR over all utterances.
The clean test audio comprises 70 sessions withheld for
evaluation. The test audio is also corrupted with -10 to +10 dB
SNRs noise in 2.5 dB increments (noisy test audio). Shi et
al. found that adding noise in training increased AVSR perfor-
mance. Following their method, in each training session, with
probability 0.25 we add either babble, music or speech noise at
0 dB [16], yielding the augmented training audio. Note that

4. Model training and evaluation
First, we train with the clean audio + clean alignment, as is
standard in the turn-taking literature [17, 24]. Then we train on
the augmented audio + clean alignment, where noise is added
but the transcription remains unchanged [16]. To test the re-
liance on accurate ASR, we train using the augmented audio +
augmented alignment. This represents a situation where there
is noisy data transcribed with ASR. Training batches consist
of 20-second windows with 2 second overlap randomly sam-
pled from training sessions. The training procedure is a 5-fold
cross-validation with 10 epochs per fold on an NVidia RTX
6000 GPU. A grid search sets the batch size to 16 and the learn-
ing rate to 1e-4. The loss function is the sum of cross-entropy
(future speaking prediction) and binary cross-entropy (current
speaker prediction) losses minimised with AdamW [28].
We report the hold/shift (H/S) prediction accuracy: the abil-
ity of the model to distinguish holds (no speaker change) from
shifts (speaker change), a common metric in the turn-taking lit-
erature [17, 19]. First, all silences > 250 ms are identified.
Those with speaker changes are shifts (83,158) and those with-
out are holds (206,830) [17]. Then, the probability of speech in
the latter half of the VAP bins is computed [17]. If speaker 0
has finished speaking and the probability of speaker 1 exceeds
a binary threshold, a shift is predicted. We set the threshold to
maximise balanced accuracy on validation sessions and report a
5-fold average on test sessions.
We report effective SNR gain i.e. the SNR at which MM-
VAP achieves the same accuracy as VAP at 0 dB, following the
AVSR literature [29]. We compare VAP and MM-VAP model
performance with two-tailed independent t-tests [30].
5. Turn-taking model performance in noise
5.1. Baseline performance on clean speech
We begin our analysis by training VAP and MM-VAP models
using the clean audio and clean alignment, establishing a base-
line H/S prediction accuracy without added noise (clean test au-
dio, Table 1, first two rows). The 5-fold average H/S prediction
accuracy of MM-VAP model is 84%, greater than the audio-
only VAP at 80% (p < 0.001). The accuracy and the superior
performance of MM-VAP are in agreement with the PTTM lit-
erature [19, 24].

5.3. Training with noisy examples
We re-train both models on the augmented training set and show
H/S prediction accuracy by noise type in blue in Figure 1. There
is a clear performance increase, e.g. average accuracy in music
is 61% for VAP and 72% for MM-VAP, up from 51-52% (p <
0.001). A large gap in model accuracy has also emerged.
The blue lines in Figure 2 show performance at each SNR.
At -10 dB SNR, the average accuracy of VAP is 50% whereas
MM-VAP is 65% (p < 0.01). The effective SNR gain of MM-
VAP has increased to +10 dB and the extreme sensitivity to
speech and music noise is reduced. At +10 dB SNR of speech
noise, the average accuracy is 64% for VAP and 75% for MM-
VAP (second two rows of Table 1), up from 51% and 52% pre-
viously (p < 0.01). At -10 dB SNR, VAP has a 50% accu-
racy. On the other hand, MM-VAP backs off to the accuracy
of a PTTM model trained exclusively on video features (67%
vs 66% in -10 dB music noise). The only difference in input is
MM-VAP receives visual features. However, it achieves supe-
rior performance in all types of noise and across the full range
of SNRs, and performance is similar to a video-only model in
extreme (-10 dB) noise. This demonstrates that it exploits visual
information to improve accuracy in acoustic interference.

5.6. The critical role of the alignment in training
Finally, we re-train both models using the augmented alignment
obtained from Amazon ASR. The orange bars in Figure 1 reveal
a stark fall in accuracy to 50 −58% and Figure 2 shows that
this is consistent across all SNRs. The drop in performance to
the 50% range indicates models are comparable to a random
hold/shift prediction. This is due to alignment and transcription
errors that ASR experiences in noise [31]. PTTM training is
therefore dependent on accurate transcriptions which are either
manual [17] or obtained from ASR and clean recordings [24].
6. Discussion
Our results show PTTMs do not maintain high accuracy in types
of noises they are likely to encounter in-the-wild (Section 5.2).
We found slight (10 dB SNR) interference from music and
other speakers caused accuracy to plummet to 50%. Includ-
ing noise during training dramatically increased performance
(Section 5.3). This is a common technique in AVSR [16] that
has not previously been considered in the turn-taking literature.
Returning to our first research question, our findings therefore
show that the PTTMs in this work are not robust to noise with-
out the prior inclusion of noise in training.
The initial poor performance shows MM-VAP does not ex-
ploit visual cues to their fullest extent (Section 5.2) without ap-
propriate training. Training on augmented data led to consider-
ably more improvement in the accuracy of MM-VAP than VAP
across the SNR range. At extreme levels of noise interference
(e.g. -10 dB music noise) VAP fails (50%, i.e. chance) but MM-
VAP backs off to the accuracy of the video-only model (67% vs
66%, Section 5.3). This highlights the role visual cues play in
achieving robust H/S accuracy in noise.
If MM-VAP is truly able to utilise visual cues to overcome
noise, this should generalise to new sources of noise. However,
we found that this was only true for the MM-VAP model trained
on speech-noise (Section 5.5). Our working hypothesis is that
mixing in random speakers over the speech signal training pro-
cess ‘forces’ MM-VAP to use visual information, rather than
relying on the acoustic properties of the noise. Thus, returning
to our second research question, PTTMs can achieve robust per-
formance in noise, but this is intimately related to the training
process and does not inherently generalise to new noise types.
It is difficult to interpret H/S prediction accuracy numbers
as no studies have determined a minimum accuracy needed to
support fluid interaction in a human-robot deployment. Taking
our 80-84% accuracy on clean speech as a baseline and ignor-
ing the many complexities of a real-world deployment includ-
ing hardware, real-time performance and human-machine dia-
logue, a robot would make a correct H/S decision 8/10 times.
Both MM-VAP and VAP would only be correct 5/10 times in
10 dB SNR music interference when trained using conventional
methods, significantly complicating interaction. This rises to
6.5/10 for VAP and 7.5/10 for MM-VAP when noise is included
in training; close to baseline performance, illustrating the power
of visual cues in achieving robust performance in noise.
A limitation of our work is we artificially add noise to
speech recorded in noise-free settings. Although this is com-
monplace [31, 16, 29], it means we do not capture changes to
verbal and non-verbal [32] communication and turn-taking [33]
in noise. Another limitation is our use of videoconferencing.
However, no in-person corpora match the size and quality of
Candor. Finally, our mixing of noise at a constant energy level
means the audio-only VAP model may be partly capitalising on
the natural variations in SNR in long recordings of speech.
Training techniques for multimodal PTTMs should be fur-
ther explored to ensure their ability to exploit visual cues gener-
alises to new noise types. As AVSR can exploit visual informa-
tion in datasets unseen in training [34], AVSR forms a natural
starting point for future multimodal PTTMs architectures [16].
Turn-taking in noise is quite similar to the channel separation
problem of speaker diarisation [35] and our findings support
further exploration and the extension to the multimodal setting.
There are many other turn-taking events (e.g. backchannels)
which require consideration in future work.
7. Conclusion
Accurate turn-taking models are an important milestone on the
path to viable human-robot interaction. We have conducted the
first exploration of PTTM performance in noise, showing that
PTTMs are not robust to typical noises a PTTM may encounter
once deployed. Training with noise improves overall accuracy
and enables multimodal PTTM to leverage visual features to
overcome acoustic interference. However, this capability does
not necessarily generalise to new types of noise. This issue must
be solved prior to their deployment in real-world settings. Our
work therefore constitutes a significant step forward in our un-
derstanding of PTTMs. Future work in PTTM architectures,
training techniques and in-the-wild deployment are therefore
imperative before deployment to human-robot interaction.
