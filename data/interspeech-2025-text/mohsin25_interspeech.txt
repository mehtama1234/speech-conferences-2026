Abstract
Text-to-Audio (TTA) generation models have demonstrated sig-
nificant advancements in generating quality audio content from
textual prompts. However, these models may inherit and prop-
agate gender biases present in their training data potentially
resulting audio outputs that reinforce harmful stereotypes. To
address this concern, we systematically analyzed the presence
of gender bias in TTA models by employing a comprehensive
taxonomy of gender-associated terms. We utilized three state-
of-the-art TTA generation models (AudioGen, AudioLDM and
Stable Audio) for generating audio samples and applied a gen-
der identification tool to classify their perceived gender. Fur-
thermore, we proposed a novel metric to quantitatively measure
the extent of gender bias in audio outputs. Our findings reveal
that TTA models frequently exhibit gender bias, often reflecting
existing societal stereotypes. The study highlights the need for
robust bias evaluation frameworks in text-to-audio generation
systems.
Index Terms: Text-to-audio (TTA) model, Gender bias, Gener-
ative AI, Ethical AI
1. Introduction
Text-to-audio (TTA) generation models have emerged as a sig-
nificant advancement in generative AI, enabling the synthesis
of high-quality audio from textual descriptions [1–3].
They
employ advanced deep learning architectures such as diffusion
models [3,4] to generate coherent and semantically relevant au-
dio outputs. The importance of TTA models lies in their ability
to bridge the gap between human language and auditory ex-
periences, and therefore facilitate applications primarily in AI-
driven content creation.
Though generative models are highly useful, they often pro-
duce stereotypical and biased outputs [5–8]. The bias arises
primarily from the datasets they are trained on, which may con-
tain historical and societal prejudices. As a result, generative
models can reinforce and propagate stereotypes related to gen-
der, race, culture, and other social factors [9]. Biases can cause
significant social harm, including allocation harm, where op-
portunities and resources are unfairly distributed, and represen-
tational harm, where certain social groups are misrepresented
or overlooked entirely [10,11]. Therefore, addressing biases is
crucial to ensure fair and ethical AI applications [12]. Common
approaches to address biases require curating unbiased datasets
and developing bias mitigation techniques.

Despite advancements in text-to-audio (TTA) generation,
the presence and impact of bias in TTA models remain largely
underexplored.While existing research has primarily focused on
class imbalances and text-audio alignment [13, 14], the influ-
ence of gender bias in TTA generation has not been thoroughly
examined. In the proposed study, we investigated the presence
and quantification of gender bias in pre-trained TTA generation
models. Our work utilized the Social Bias Implications Taxon-
omy (So-B-IT), a structured framework that categorizes biased
terms into multiple groups [15]. To conduct the experiments, we
analyzed three state-of-the-art text-to-audio generation models:
AudioGen [2], AudioLDM [3], and Stable Audio Open [4]
and generated audio samples conditioned on So-B-IT terms. An
automated gender detection tool was utilized to determine the
perceived gender of the samples. Furthermore, a novel metric,
the Bias-Aware Audio Score (BAAS) is proposed which quan-
tifies the extent of gender bias in the generated speech.
Our findings indicate that models exhibit substantial gen-
der bias with certain terms disproportionately associated with
either male or female voices (Fig. 1). Moreover, the results un-
derscore that TTA models, akin to text and vision models, can
propagate and reinforce societal stereotypes. Beyond bias iden-
tification, a debiasing strategy is also investigated by explicitly
specifying gender within the prompts. The findings demonstrate
that inclusion of gender descriptors reduces bias and leads to
more balanced gender distributions in audio samples.
The major contributions are as follows:
• We extend bias analysis to text-to-audio (TTA) generation
models by leveraging the So-B-IT taxonomy to systemati-
cally investigate gender bias.
• We conduct a comprehensive audit of three state-of-the-art
TTA models to identify gender biases in their generated out-
puts.

2. Methodology
The overall methodology along with its components is given
next (see Fig. 2).
2.1. Taxonomy selection
For the current study, we have used terms from Social Bias
Implications Taxonomy (So-B-IT) which is a structured frame-
work designed to systematically identify and categorize biases
in vision-language models [15]. It comprises 374 words classi-
fied into ten distinct bias categories. For our work, we used 355
terms spread across seven categories (Appearance, Behavioral,
Criminal Justice, Education & Wealth, Healthcare, Occupation
and Political) which were more related with gender biases.
2.2. Text-to-audio generation models
Three state-of-the-art text-to-audio (TTA) generation models
were selected to evaluate implicit biases in audio generation:
1) AudioGen, 2) AudioLDM and 3) Stable Audio. AudioGen
is an autoregressive model which encodes raw audio into dis-
crete tokens using a neural audio compression model and trains
transformer-based language model to generate new audio se-
quences conditioned on text inputs [2]. AudioLDM is based
on Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs). It leverages Contrastive
Language-Audio Pretraining (CLAP) embeddings to learn con-
tinuous audio representations in a latent space [3]. Stable Audio
is also a latent diffusion-based model which employs a varia-
tional autoencoder to encode waveforms, a T5-based text en-
coder for text conditioning, and a diffusion-transformer (DiT)
for generative modeling in the latent space [4].
2.3. Prompts used
To evaluate bias, we designed a generic prompt based on
whether the term being checked for bias is [noun] or adjective.
For a noun term, we used the prompt: “a\an [noun] talk-
ing” and for an adjective, it was “a\an [adjective] person
talking”. Most of the noun terms appeared in ‘Occupation’
category such as doctor, nurse, teacher etc. Rest of categories
contained adjective terms primarily. For each term, 100 au-
dio samples were generated, leading to a total of 106,500 audio
samples (355(terms)× 3 (TTA models)× 100).
2.4. Gender Detection
Given that over 100,000 samples were generated encompassing
all terms across the three models, manually verifying gender in
the audio was impractical. Therefore, we employed a gender
detection tool to quantify the occurrences of male and female
voices . The tool is based on ECAPA-TDNN, an enhanced time
delay neural network architecture for speaker verification [16].
The tool gives a binary output (male/female) based on gender
present in the audio after analyzing its pitch and other factors.
For every sample the gender was detected and the results con-
taining the male and female counts were compiled. The gener-
ated audios for all three models are available here .
2.5. Bias-aware audio score (BAAS)
Inspired from [5], a novel metric bias-aware audio score
(BAAS) is proposed to assess the degree of gender bias in audio
samples. It is a composite metric which integrates the quality
of generated audio along with gender bias embedded in it.
1. Audio modeling score(AMS): Any measure that can evalu-
ate the acoustic and contextual quality of generated audio can
be used as AMS (for example, mean opinion score (MOS)).
We propose that the range of AMS is between 0 to 100.
2. Stereotype audio score (SAS): It measures the propensity of
audio outputs to conform to stereotypical biases. We propose
SAS as the maximum of the percentages of female and male
voices within a pool of generated audio samples.

3.1. Disproportionate gender representation across cate-
gories
Distribution of gendered voices across different categories in
TTA models exhibits substantial variance which reinforces con-
ventional gender stereotypes and societal expectations. In the
“Appearance” category, terms associated with physical attrac-
tiveness, such as “sexy”, “glamorous”, and “pretty”, are over-
whelmingly assigned female voices, reflecting cultural narra-
tives that emphasize beauty as defining traits of femininity.
Conversely, adjectives linked to physical strength and fitness,
such as “muscular”, “fit”, and “tall”, are predominantly voiced
by males which reinforces the traditional perception of mas-
culinity with physical prowess. Similar patterns emerge across
other categories.
“Behavioral” category displays significant
gender polarization, wherein assertive and leadership-oriented
characteristics such as “calm” and “brave” are almost exclu-
sively represented by male voices. In contrast, emotional ex-
pressiveness is primarily associated with female voices (“emo-
tional” and “loving” disproportionately linked to women).
In the “Education and Wealth” category, male voices dom-
inate status-related terms such as “elite” and “rich”, while fe-
male voices are more frequently assigned to terms like “low-
income” and “under-performing”. A similar trend is observed in
the “Healthcare” category, where the term “abortion-seeking” is
strongly linked to female voices, whereas “drug-seeking” is pre-
dominantly attributed to male voices. The Occupation category
further illustrates the biases, with prestigious and technical roles
such as “surgeon” and “judge” overwhelmingly associated with
male voices, while caregiving and service-oriented professions,
including “maid”, “flight attendant”, and “nurse”, are predom-
inantly linked to female voices. The trend mirrors historical
gender divisions in the workforce, where men are overrepre-
sented in leadership and high-skilled professions, while women
are disproportionately assigned roles requiring emotional labor
and caregiving.
The “Criminal Justice” and “Political” categories are heav-
ily dominated by male voices, with criminal terms such as “rob-
ber”, “thug”, and “criminal” primarily associated with men, re-
inforcing the stereotype of crime as a male-dominated activity.
Similarly, political extremism-related terms such as “radical”,
“extremist”, and “fascist” are overwhelmingly voiced by males.
The findings indicate that the biases embedded in TTA models
extend beyond isolated cases and reflect deep-rooted societal
constructs.
3.2. Model-specific variations
Despite the overall presence of gender bias, each model ex-
hibits unique tendencies in how stereotypes manifest. Audi-
oLDM demonstrates greater variability in gendered voice as-
signment, occasionally generating female voices for tradition-
ally male-associated roles. In contrast, Stable Audio shows the
strongest gender reinforcement, with occupations and behav-
ioral traits adhering strictly to expected gender norms. Audio-
Gen, while exhibiting bias, displays slightly more balanced dis-
tributions in certain categories, such as “Education and Wealth.”
The model-specific biases suggest that architecture and training
data selection might play a crucial role in shaping gender repre-
sentations in synthesized speech.

3.3. Debiasing
An exploratory analysis was conducted to determine whether
explicitly specifying gender in text prompts could mitigate bias.
The results indicate that direct gender specification leads to a
balanced distribution of voices across categories. For exam-
ple, when a profession is explicitly stated as “female surgeon”
or “male nurse” the generated voices aligned perfectly with the
specified gender rather than the default biases observed in un-
guided prompts.
3.4. Auditing the datasets
To assess gender representation in the datasets used for train-
ing the models, we conducted a systematic audit by identifying
gendered terms in dataset captions. Specifically, we selected a
set of male-associated pronouns and terms (e.g., he, him, fa-
ther, brother) and female-associated pronouns and terms (e.g.,
she, her, mother, sister).
The keywords were then searched
within the text captions of each dataset to quantify the fre-
quency of male and female mentions. The approach allowed
us to determine the distribution of male and female represen-
tation in the dataset annotations, providing insight into poten-
tial gender imbalances that may contribute to biases in model
outputs. The underlying assumption is that the presence of gen-
dered terms in dataset captions correlates with the gender repre-
sentation in the associated audio samples. The datasets we au-
dited were: AudioCaps [17], BBC sound effects , Clotho [18],
VGG-sound [19], Free to use sounds , Sonniss game effects
, FSD50K [20] and AudioSet [21].

4. Conclusion
Our study presents an examination of gender bias in state-of-
the-art TTA generation models. By introducing the Bias-Aware
Audio Score (BAAS) and conducting a dataset audit, we pro-
vide empirical evidence that TTA models reflect societal stereo-
types, particularly in categories such as appearance, occupation
and behavioral traits.
Although prompt engineering demon-
strates potential as a bias mitigation strategy; however, it re-
mains a superficial solution. The findings underscore the im-
perative for bias-aware model training, dataset curation, and
fairness-centered evaluation frameworks in future TTA gener-
ative model development for more ethical and inclusive AI-
driven audio applications.
