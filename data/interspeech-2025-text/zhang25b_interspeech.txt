Tonal Perception in Changde Mandarin 
Zhenrui Zhang1, Fang Hu2 
1University of Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, China 
2Institute of Linguistics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, China

Abstract 
This paper explores perceptual cues of the four tones in 
Changde Mandarin. Results show that T1 is perceived as a high-
level tone, T2 is a low-rising tone in both production and 
perception, T3 is not phonologically level but falling, and the 
perception of T4 is influenced by various factors. And results 
also show that tonal perception might not be categorical, since 
neither the T2-T3 continuum nor the T2-T4 continuum fulfills 
the standards for typical categorical perception. 
Index Terms: tone, perceptual cues, categorical perception, 
Changde Mandarin 
1. Introduction 
Many languages in the world are tonal [1]. Chinese is an 
exemplar that uses pitch or fundamental frequency (F0) to 
distinguish lexical meanings [2] [3]. The four lexical tones in 
Standard Chinese are transcribed as /55 35 214 51/ in Chao's 
tone letters [4]. Chinese tones have attracted abundance of 
research interest for decades. And there are two issues essential 
to tonal perception. The first one concerns perceptual cues of 
tone. In general, pitch, intensity, duration, and phonation all 
contribute to tonal perception [5]. And pitch could be further 
differentiated into relative height or register and the dynamic 
movement or contour. There is a long debate on the difference 
between T2 and T 3 in Standard Chinese. Both tones have a 
phonetically concave shape and are easily misperceived 
possibly due to their similar pitch onset, contour shape, and 
duration [6] [7] [8] [9]. Some research proposed that T2 andT3 
differ in concavity, with T2 having an earlier turning point than 
T3 [10] [11], while others treated T2 as rising and T3 as a 
phonological low tone [12] [13]. However, there are still others 
argue against the low tone account, because they found that 
native speakers favor a low concave pitch contour as an 
appropriate representation of T3 [11] [14] [15]. 
The second issue concerns the debate whether tones are 
perceived categorically. Typically, in categorical perception, (1) 
the peak in discrimination accuracy should align with the 
category boundary where rapid changes in identification 
probability occur, and (2) the discrimination accuracy should be 
predictable from the identification results [16] [17] [18] [19]). 
It is well documented that consonants are perceived 
categorically [20] [21] [22], whereas vowels are perceived in a 
continuous manner [23] [24] [25]. But there is no consensus 
regarding tone perception [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]. 
This paper pursues these two theoretical issues by 
investigating Changde Mandarin, a regional variety of the 
south-western Mandarin dialects [32]. Changde is situated in 
the northwest of Hunan Province. Similar to Standard Chinese, 
Changde Mandarin also has four tones, but with different 
phonetic tonal values in Chao’s tone letters: /45 23 21 24/. 
Figure 1 shows pitch contours of the four tones and their 2-
sigma ellipses in the pitch-slope plot. A typologically marked 
phenomenon is that Changde Mandarin has no level tone: T1 is 
high rising and T3 is low falling. It is thus essential to 
investigate whether the dynamics of F0 plays a role in the 
perception of T1 and T3, or they mainly distinguish by high 
versus low pitch. Another marked tonal phenomenon is that T2 
and T4 have similar pitch onset and contour shape. 
Identification and discrimination experiments are designed to 
explore perceptual cues of the four tones in general and to 
examine the particular differences between tonal pairs with 
similar or different contours. The findings would shed light on 
the controversy as to whether tonal perception is categorical or 
not, and contribute to a better understanding on tonal contrasts 
in a tonal language with a contour tone system.

2. Methodology 
2.1. Subjects 
31 native speakers (10 males and 21 females) were recruited. 
None of them reported any speech or hearing disorders. All 
subjects completed the experiments except that one male did 
not participate in Experiment 2. 
2.2. Stimuli 
2.2.1. Experiment 1 
Target syllables /mi/ were provided by a male speaker with four 
tones: /mi45/ “squint”, /mi23/ “puzzle”, /mi21/ “rice”, /mi24/ 
“honey”. Each syllable was embedded in a carrier sentence [mi, 
təu23 mi kɛ21 ni21 tʰiŋ45] “mi, read mi for you to listen.” Four 
repetitions in a randomized order were recorded. The rhyme 
was labeled as the tone-bearing unit and annotated in Praat 
6.0.29 [34]. Ten equidistant sampling points of F0 across the 
entire duration were extracted and measured. A typical sample 
for each tone was selected from the recordings as the model of 
the synthetic [mi] syllable. Table 1 shows the reference pitch 
values for each tone. Based on the reference pitch range, pitch

To encapsulate the diverse shapes of tonal contours, our design 
manipulates the offset of each pitch curve by one semitone 
increment, enabling transitions from slightly descending to flat, 
then to low-rising, and ultimately to high-rising contours. Given 
that T4 exhibits the greatest pitch increase (approximately 7 
semitones) and T3 the greatest decrease (around 4 semitones), 
we incorporated seven rising steps (S7 to S1) and four falling 
steps (S-1 to S-4) relative to each base pitch curve. As shown 
in Figure 2, we totally have a set of 60 stimuli (5 registers × 12 
steps), with a normalized time of 300 milliseconds. 
2.2.2. Experiment 2 
The second experiment is designed to explore the differences 
between the two tonal pairs, namely T2-T3 and T2-T4. As 
shown in Figure 3, two 10-step pitch continua with equivalent 
step length in semitone distance are constructed through 
PSOLA re-synthesis in Praat: one is ranging from T2 to T3 
(left), the other is ranging from T2 to T4 (right).

2.3. Procedure 
2.3.1. Experiment 1 
An identification task was conducted. Four Chinese characters, 
[mi] syllables with the four tones, were displayed on the screen. 
Upon hearing a stimulus [miX] (X represents one of the four 
tones), the participant was required to identify it as one of the 
four choices by pressing the corresponding key on the keyboard. 
A total of 240 stimuli (60 stimuli repeated four times) were 
presented in ten blocks with a randomized order. Participants' 
responses and reaction times were automatically recorded. 
2.3.2. Experiment 2 
Participants are required to complete an identification task first, 
and then a discrimination task. The procedure of the 
identification task is similar to Experiment 1. For each 
continuum, 40 stimuli (10 steps * 4 repetitions) are played in a 
random order for subjects to identify in 2 blocks. 
Either ABX or AX paradigm is used in discrimination task. 
Of the 30 participants, 15 participants each completed both the 
AX and ABX tasks. For AX paradigm, stimuli were presented 
in pairs with a 500-ms interstimulus interval (ISI) to maximize 
differences in the performance of between- versus within-
category discrimination ([36]). The whole 416 pairs (26 pairs * 
4 repetitions) were presented in a random order. In each set, 16 
pairs consist of two different stimuli separated by two steps in 
either forward 1-3, 2-4, 3-5...7-9, 8-10 or reverse order 3-1, 4-
2, 5-3, etc; 10 pairs consist of two identical stimuli, like 1-1, 2-
2, 3-3, etc. After hearing each pair, participants need to judge 
whether the two stimuli were the same or different, and to 
respond by pressing the key “g” (same) or “h” (different). 
For ABX paradigm, three stimuli A, B, and X are played 
successively in each trial, where A and B are two stimuli one 
step apart on the continuum, such as S1-S3, S2-S4, S3-S5... The 
third stimulus, X, is either identical to A (e.g., S1-S3-S1) or to 
B (e.g., S1-S3-S3). After listening to the three stimuli, 
participants need to respond accordingly by pressing "g" for A 
or "h" for B as soon as possible. To counteract any sequence 
effects, all possible permutations of ABX are considered: ABA 
(e.g., S1-S3-S1), ABB (e.g., S1-S3-S3), BAA (e.g., S3-S1-S1), 
BAB (e.g., S3-S1-S3). Each sequence is repeated four times, 
resulting in a total of 128 trials (4 sequences × 8 pairs × 4 
repetitions). The stimuli are presented in a random order. 
3. Results 
3.1. Experiment 1 
3.1.1. Mean identification rate 
Figure 4 shows the average response percentages of the 12-step 
tonal continuums from falling to rising at 5 register groups. 
 
 
 
 
Figure 4: Mean identification rate at 12 steps in 5 
register groups. 
At the L register, the rising contours S1 to S7 are 
predominantly identified as T2 and T4. As the rising slope 
decreases, the recognition rate of T4 decreases and the rate of 
T2 steadily rises, peaking at 0.9 at S2. When the contour is level 
S0, the recognition of T2 drops sharply to the bottom, and there 
715

is a 0.5 chance of it being identified as T3. And the falling 
contours S-1 to S-4 are predominantly recognized as T3, with a 
recognition rate ranging from 0.9 to 1.0. And the hL register has 
a similar perceptual pattern to the L register. In summary, low 
pitch contours are identified as T3 when the contour is falling, 
are identified as T2 when the contour is slightly rising, and are 
identified as T2 or T4 the contour is sharp rising. That is, it is 
difficult to identify T4 in the low pitch registers. 
The M register also demonstrates a three-way perceptual 
pattern. When the contour is falling, it is again identified as T3. 
When the contour is sharp rising, T4 has the highest 
identification rate, but the competitive tone is T1 rather than T2. 
When the contour is slightly rising, the identification rates of 
T1, T2 and T4 are all below the chance level. 
The two high registers hH and H exhibit a similar pattern, 
which is different from the low and mid registers. When the 
contour is rising or level, it is predominantly identified as T1, 
except that T4 is a competitor when the contour is sharp rising. 
When the contour is falling, it is likely identified as T3, except 
that when the falling is least at -1 step the identification of T1 
could challenge or even prevail. And there is a systematic 
difference between the hH and H registers. That is, a higher 
pitch of H favors the identification of T1, and a relatively lower 
pitch of hH favors the identification of T3. 
3.1.2. Statistical analysis 
A generalized linear model was employed for each tonal 
category to fit the identification rate, setting Slope (pitch 
contour) and Register (pitch height) as independent variables. 
Results indicate that both pitch register and slope have 
significant influences on the recognition rate for all the four 
tones (T1-Register: wald chi-square=1367.5, p<0.00; T1-Slope: 
wald chi-square=865.24; p<0.00; T2-Register: wald chi-
square=1091.61, p<0.00; T2-Slope: wald chi-square=1049.13; 
p<0.00; T3-Register: wald chi-square=280.80, p<0.00; T3-
Slope: wald chi-square=5739.36; p<0.00; T4-Register: wald 
chi-square= 24.39, p<0.00; T4-Slope: wald chi-square= 528.66; 
p<0.00). And there exists an interaction between the two factors 
(T1: wald chi-square= 361.30, p<0.00; T2: wald chi-square= 
886.63, p<0.00; T3: wald chi-square= 346.65, p<0.00; T4: wald 
chi-square= 130.93, p<0.00;).

tones by Glm models. 
Figure 5 shows the predicted identification rate for each 
tone based on generalized linear models. First, T1 is sensitive 
to both pitch register and slope. When the pitch register is 
below 160 Hz, T1 has a low rate of identification. Additionally, 
when the pitch contour is falling (S-2 to S-4), T1 has a low rate 
of identification regardless of the pitch register. However, when 
the pitch contour is rising, level, or slightly falling, the 
identification rate of T1 shows a strong positive correlation with 
the pitch register: higher pitch register is associated with a 
higher rate of identification. 
Second, T2 has an identification pattern mirrored to T1. T2 
has a low rate of identification when the pitch register is high 
or when the pitch contour is falling or level. And when the pitch 
contour is rising, the identification rate of T2 shows a negative 
correlation with the pitch register in general: higher pitch 
register is associated with a lower rate of identification. But it 
should be noted that the slope also plays a role: the 
identification of T2 prefers a slight rising contour, rather than a 
sharp rise. 
Third, slope prevails register in the identification of T3. 
Only a falling contour has a high identification rate of T3. When 
the pitch contour is rising or even level, the identification rate 
is low regardless of pitch register. When the pitch contour is 
falling, the identification rate shows a negative correlation with 
the pitch register: higher pitch register is associated with a 
lower rate of identification. But the lowest identification rate is 
above the chance level except for the least falling contour (S-1). 
Finally, T4 has the worst rate of identification, with a 
highest rate below 0.7. Only the pitch contour is sharp rising 
(S6 and S7) and the register is lower than 170 Hz, would T4 be 
the favored choice.

3.2.2. T2-T4 continuum 
As depicted in Figure 7, the identification results for the T2-T4 
continuum do not show a typical category pattern. Only the 
identification curve on the left side of the continuum in the AX 
group has an ideal identification rate for T4, spanning from 0.9 
to 1.0. By contrast, the identification results for T2 are relatively 
poor, particularly in the AX group, with a lower rate of 
approximately 0.8 at the right side of the continuum. 
Also, the discrimination curves for the T2-T4 continuum 
are around or below 0.6 and exhibit no discernible peak, 
regardless of the group or calculation method employed. 
Furthermore, the two discrimination curves overlap heavily 
with each other in the ABX group, whereas the estimated curve 
is marginally higher than the predicted curve in the AX group. 
Both indicate a low discrimination accuracy for the T2-T4 
continuum.

4. Discussion and Conclusions 
What is essential in tonal phonology is to explain how tones 
distinguish from each other. Among the four tones /45 23 21 
24/ in Changde Mandarin, T1 is high, T2 and T3 are low, but 
T4 is ambiguous. How these tones are differentiated from each 
other in perception? Is tonal perception categorical? This paper 
employed two experiments to address these two issues in tonal 
perception in Changde Mandarin. 
Experiment 1 aims to investigate perceptual cues of the four 
tones in Changde Mandarin. Results show that both pitch 
height/register and pitch contour/slope are critical for the 
identification of the tones in general on the one hand. On the 
other hand, register and slope play different roles in the 
perception of individual tones. 
First, register is more important than slope in the perception 
of T1. As long as the register is sufficiently high, T1 is tolerant 
of slope. For instance, the sharpest rising contour S7 has an 
identification rate above 0.6, as shown in Figure 4. However, 
the slope is statistically significant, too. T1 prefers a slightly 
rising or level contour, and the least rising contour (S1) and the 
level pitch curve (S0) have the highest rate of identification in 
the H and hH groups respectively in Figure 4. To conclude, T1 
could phonologically be characterized as [+High] [+Level], 
with a phonetic realization as high level or high (slightly) rising.  
 Second, T3 is a low falling tone. But it could not be treated 
as a low tone phonologically, because slope is more important 
than register in perception. As evidenced in Figure 5, all falling 
contours have identification rates higher than chance level 
except the least falling slope (S-1), whereas all the rising 
contours have identification rates lower than 0.2. And even the 
level contours under the condition of low pitch register also 
have identification rates lower than chance level in general. 
Therefore, T3 should phonologically be characterized as 
[+Falling] [+Low].  
Third, the two rising tones, T2 and T4, could not be 
differentiated perceptually by a different rising slope, as 
suggested by their production as well as their transcriptions [23] 
versus [24]. Rather, high rising pitch contours are likely to be 
identified as T2, no matter whether the rising slope is sharp or 
not. In short, T2 could phonologically be characterized as 
[+Low] [+rising]. But as for T4, the experiments did not yield 
satisfactory results. The results suggest that T4 prefers a low 
pitch register below 170Hz and a sharp rising contour (S6 and 
S7). But it should be admitted that further research is required 
to explore how T4 is distinguished from T2. 
Experiment 2 employed both the ABX and AX paradigms 
to examine whether the homomorphic tonal continuum T2-T4 
and the heterotonic tonal continuum T2-T3 are perceived 
categorically or not. Results show that the T2-T3 continuum 
exhibited an ideal identification pattern in the AX group: T2 
and T3 achieved nearly 100% recognition rates within their 
respective 
perceived 
advantage 
intervals, 
and 
their 
identification scores were highly sensitive at the categorical 
boundary, showing sharp rises or falls and quickly transitioning 
to another categorical region. Although the identification 
performance in the ABX group was not as good as that of the 
AX group, it still presented two distinguishable categorical 
regions on both sides of the continuum. However, neither the 
ABX group nor the AX group demonstrated a corresponding 
discrimination peak at the category boundary, failing to meet 
the strictly defined criteria for categorical perception. 
Regarding the T2-T4 continuum, the results did not align with 
the criteria for categorical perception, regardless of whether the 
ABX or AX paradigm was adopted, due to poor performances 
in both the identification and discrimination tasks. Therefore, 
T2 and T4, which share the same contour shape, are actually 
perceived in a continuous manner. 
It should be noted that although the heterotonic tonal 
continuum T2-T3 did not achieve the category perception mode 
in the strict sense, our results suggest that the perception of the 
heterotonic tonal continuum T2-T3 is more categorical than that 
of the homomorphic tonal continuum T2-T4. Additionally, our 
results revealed that there is no significant difference between 
ABX and AX paradigms.
