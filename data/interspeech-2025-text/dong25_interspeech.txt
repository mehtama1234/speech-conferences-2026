Multitalker Babble in English Vowel Perception Training:
A Comparison between Humans and Neural Models
Wenwei Dong1, Alif Silpachai1, Catia Cucchiarini 1, Helmer Strik1,2
1Centre for Language and Speech Technology, Radboud University Nijmegen, The Netherlands
2Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour, Radboud University Nijmegen, The
Netherlands
{wenwei.dong, alif.silpachai, catia.cucchiarini, helmer.strik}@ru.nl
Abstract
Perceptual training with multitalker babble can benefit first lan-
guage listeners; however, it is unclear whether such training
is beneficial for second language (L2) listeners and whether
there is an optimal number of talkers for creating babble. Since
perception experiments with humans are complex and time-
consuming and neural models are inspired by the human brain,
we explore the use of neural models and study how well their
performance aligns with the results for human listeners. In this
study, we first investigated how babble produced by 2 and 6
talkers affected the perceptual learning of English vowels by
L2 listeners. We then fine-tuned Automatic Speech Recogni-
tion (ASR) models using the same babble datasets. The results
showed that babble regardless of the number of talkers benefited
listeners in speech-shaped noise, and the Wav2Vec2.0 model
also improved the accuracy with babble training and exhibited
trends more similarly to humans than the TDNN model.
Index Terms: perception, L2 listeners, ASR models, babble
training
1. Introduction
Perception training is crucial for enhancing listeners’ abilities
to distinguish, categorize, and process sounds, particularly in
challenging listening environments [1] or when acquiring a sec-
ond language (L2) [2]. Research shows that computer-assisted
perceptual training of L2 sounds is highly effective, especially
when high variability pronunciation training (HVPT) [3] is ap-
plied. Corrective feedback during HVPT can improve listen-
ers’ perception of L2 sounds, and can especially help them
generalize to new words produced by novel talkers [4]. First-
language studies involving subjects with hearing deficits sug-
gest that perceptual training in noise, particularly in multitalker
babble (competing voices in the background), can be beneficial
[5][6]. However, it is unclear whether training using babble is
also beneficial for L2 listeners and whether there is an optimal
number of talkers in babble that is the most beneficial to listen-
ers.
Considering that perception studies with L2 listeners are
complex and time-consuming and that neural models are in-
spired by the structure and function of the human brain [7][8],
previous research explored the potential of such models for
perception studies [9], for instance by investigating Hidden
Markov models (HMMs) and Kaldi-based deep belief network
acoustic models. Results showed that the Kaldi-based neural
model was able to model the difficulty of Japanese native lis-
teners in American English /ô/-/l/, and also model the vowel
length contrasts in Japanese, which are easier to perceive for
Japanese natives than for American English native listeners. In-
vestigating perceptual training with neural models could pro-
vide useful insights and possibly alternative solutions. It may
allow researchers to find effective perceptual training conditions
and directly apply them to human listeners. In a separate on-
going study, we [10] applied a pretest-training-posttest design
to L2 listeners, the pretest and posttest were under quiet (also
known as clean speech for ASR models) and speech-shaped
noise (SSN) [11] conditions, and the training was under 2- and
6-talker babble conditions. In the current study, we further ap-
plied the same procedure to neural models to compare them to
L2 listeners. Such a comparison may provide valuable insights
into how neural models can be used to develop effective and ef-
ficient perceptual training methods. The research questions we
address are:
1. Is multitalker babble training beneficial for native Dutch lis-
teners learning to perceive English vowels?
2. If so, can neural models (TDNN and Wav2Vec2.0) also ben-
efit from multitalker babble training like L2 listeners? Ad-
ditionally, which model shows improvement most similar to
that of L2 listeners?
3. Does training neural models with background noise that
matches the test set yield greater improvement than training
with multitalker babble?
To address these research questions, we first compared the
effects of babble produced by two talkers (2-talker babble) in
an online study using HVPT with babble with six talkers (6-
talker babble) on Dutch listeners’ perception of American En-
glish vowels (/E/–/æ/ and /eI/–/aI/ ) produced in monosyllabic
words.
To investigate whether the ASR models also benefit
from fine-tuning with the babble training set, we compared the
accuracy improvement of two ASR models, including the pre-
trained Time-delay Neural Network (TDNN) [12] model and
the pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 [13], which were fine-tuned under
similar conditions as those used for L2 listeners.
Consider-
ing that the test sets were in quiet and SSN, we further com-
pared the improvement of the two ASR models under quiet
(clean speech) and SSN training conditions to study whether
they could achieve greater improvement than with babble train-
ing.
2. Methodology
2.1. Datasets
The datasets we used in our experiments were divided into
pretest, training and posttest sets. The pre- and posttests had
64 trials (32 in each of two smaller tests, made up of 8 words
× 4 vowels), and the training phase had 100 trials (25 words ×
4 vowels). Two speakers (one female, one male) produced the
stimuli for the pretest, and two different speakers (one female,
one male) produced the posttest stimuli. English monosyllabic

American talkers served as the stimuli.
The babble in the training conditions was created from ei-
ther two (one female and one male) or six (three females and
three males) American English speakers reading a passage from
the Wonderful Wizard of Oz [14]. Their readings were randomly
taken from different parts of the passage before being mixed
to create the babble. These talkers did not produce the test or
training stimuli. Immediately before the pretest and again right
before training, 12 trials (3 words × 4 vowels) with a quiet back-
ground containing words sharing the same characteristics as the
stimuli were used in a familiarization task. These words were
unique to the familiarization task and were produced by two
talkers (one female and one male) who did not produce the test
or training stimuli.

SSN [11] is white noise whose spectrum is shaped like the
long-term spectrum of speech. SSN in our study was created
from babble based on six American English talkers (three fe-
males and three males) reading a passage from White Fang[15].
These talkers did not produce other materials in the study.
For the L2 listener experiment, we tested the 2-talker and
6-talker babble backgrounds, while for the ASR models experi-
ment, we compared the quiet and SSN backgrounds in addition
to the babble conditions.
2.2. L2 listeners perception experiment
Seventy native Dutch listeners were trained in an online study
to perceive the four target vowels in a two-alternative forced
choice using HVPT in a pretest-training-posttest design in
which corrective feedback was provided only during the train-
ing phase. Half of the listeners (n = 35) were randomly as-
signed to be trained with babble produced by two talkers, and
the other half, with babble produced by six talkers. Based on
self-reported data, the participants rated their English fluency as
3.76 (SD 0.81) out of 5, with 1 being “helemaal niet vloeiend”
(not fluent at all) and 5 “zeer vloeiend” (very fluent), and all
were adults. Each listener received a unique randomized order
of the stimuli. During the pre- and posttests, both groups iden-
tified the sounds in quiet and SSN.
2.3. ASR models experiment
To better compare ASR models with L2 listeners, we applied
two ASR models: the TDNN and the Wav2Vec2.0 models. The
following procedures were applied to both ASR models:
• Test pre-trained models with pre- and posttest sets.
• Fine-tune models with four training conditions (2-talker and
6-talker babble, quiet and SSN).
• Test fine-tuned models with pre- and posttest sets.
We used the accuracy in selecting the correct words as our per-
formance measure to compare all the experiments. As in the L2
listeners’ perceptual experiment, every audio had two options.
We calculated the Goodness of Pronunciation (GOP) score [16]
or alignment confidence score [17] for the two options sepa-
rately, and the option with the higher score was considered the
model’s choice.
2.3.1. GOP algorithm based on TDNN model
A TDNN model was chosen because it is widely used in ASR
tasks and its time-delayed connections capture temporal de-
pendencies of the feature sequences. This model was devel-
oped from Deep Neural Networks (DNN) and has been ap-
plied to many pronunciation assessment tasks [16, 18, 19]. In
such tasks, the pre-trained TDNN model was used to extract
phoneme posterior probabilities, and the phoneme level pro-
nunciation accuracy can be measured using Equation 1. The
pre-trained TDNN [20] model we used here was trained by the
Librispeech [21] dataset with 960 hours of training set.

In measuring phoneme pronunciation accuracy tasks, the
GOP algorithm was calculated using prompts and L2 speech,
providing an indication of how well the speech matches the
prompt. To better mimic the human perception experiment and
consider two options, we calculated the GOP score for audio
and two options separately and averaged the phoneme GOP to
the word GOP score. If the GOP score of the correct word was
higher than the GOP score of the incorrect word, we concluded
that the ASR model chose the correct option. For fine-tuning,
we used the Kaldi script with a lower learning rate and 4 epochs.
2.3.2. Alignment confidence score based on Wav2Vec2.0 model
The pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 model we used in this study
was ”wav2vec2-base-960h” from Huggingface [22], which was
trained on the Librispeech dataset (960 hours) and has achieved
competitive performance on several speech tasks [23, 24, 25,
26]. We used the forced alignment confidence score to repre-
sent the matching degree of the audio with two options. As in
the TDNN model experiment, if the confidence score of the cor-
rect word was higher than the score of the incorrect word, we
concluded that the model chose the correct option. The align-
ment confidence score was obtained through the following pro-

The Wav2Vec2.0 model was fine-tuned with Connectionist

2 epochs.

3.1. L2 listeners versus ASR models on babble sets
For the comparison between L2 listeners’ and the ASR model
results, it is important to note that the L2 listeners took the
pretest on the pretest set and the posttest on the posttest set
to avoid memory effects. For the ASR models, both pre- and
posttests could be used for testing, so we used the average ac-
curacy of the two test sets for the comparison.
3.1.1. L2 listeners’ perception experiment
The results from a linear mixed-effects model [27] showed sig-
nificant effects of vowel (/E/–/æ/, /eI/–/aI/) (p <.001) and the in-
teraction between test (pretest, posttest) and background (quiet,
SSN) (p <.05). There was no significant effect of training con-
dition (2-talker babble, 6-talker babble). Post hoc analyses [28]
suggested that accuracy was significantly higher for the per-
ception of /eI/–/aI/ than /E/–/æ/ (p <.001). In both pre- and
posttests, accuracy was significantly higher when the stimuli
were presented in quiet than SSN (p <.001). However, the accu-
racy was significantly higher in the posttest than in the pretest,
but only when the stimuli were presented in SSN (p <.001), not
in quiet, with the listeners improving their accuracy by 3.44%
(pretest: 82. 32% versus posttest: 85. 76%). This suggested
that training was more beneficial for vowel perception with SSN
than in quiet regardless of whether the babble had two or six
talkers during training.
3.1.2. Comparison between L2 listeners and ASR models
When we compare the results of pre-trained L2 listeners to those
of pre-trained ASR models on the quiet test set (Table 2), it ap-
peared that the pre-trained TDNN model had lower discrimina-
tion accuracy in the /E/–/æ/ pair than in the /eI/–/aI/ pair, with
accuracies of 81.25% and 87.50%, respectively. L2 listeners
showed the same pattern but had higher accuracy in both pairs
than the TDNN model, with accuracies of 83.30% and 97.86%.
The pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 model also had lower average ac-
curacy in the /E/–/æ/ pair than in the /eI/–/aI/ pair, the accuracies
were 84.37% and 96.87%, respectively. For the /E/–/æ/ pair,
the accuracy was 1.07% higher than that of L2 listeners, and
for the /eI/–/aI/ pair, the accuracy was 0.99% lower than that of
the L2 listeners’ performance, which was almost at the same
level. In the comparison between the pre-trained TDNN and
the pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 models, we observed that the pre-
trained Wav2Vec2.0 had better phoneme pairs discrimination
ability than the pre-trained TDNN model, in the /E/–/æ/ pair,
the accuracy was 3.12% higher, and especially in the /eI/–/aI/
pair, it was 9.37% higher.
When we compare trained L2 listeners with the trained
ASR models on the quiet test set (TAVI in Table 2), L2 listeners
only benefited from 6-talker talker babble training with a total
average improvement of 2.59%, which was mostly contributed
by the /E/–/æ/ pair. However, the TDNN model could gain im-
provement from both babble conditions, and the 6-talker bab-
ble training condition was able to improve accuracy more than
the 2-talker babble, the improvements were 7.81% and 3.12%.
The Wav2Vec2.0 model could achieve more improvement in the
2-talker babble training condition than in the 6-talker babble
condition, the improvement was also mostly contributed by the
/E/–/æ/. The TDNN model results showed improvement in both
babble conditions, but more improvement was obtained in the
6-talker babble condition. However, the Wav2Vec2.0 model re-
sults only showed improvement in the 2-talker babble condition.
In the comparison between pre-trained L2 listeners and the
pre-trained ASR models on the SSN test set (Table 2), the
TDNN model showed slightly better accuracy for the /E/–/æ/
pair than for the /eI/–/aI/ pair, with the values of 56.25%
and 50%, respectively.
We can see from Table 2 that the
Wav2Vec2.0 model did not perform well in the SSN test set
either, the pre-trained model accuracy on the /E/–/æ/ pair was
46.87%, and the accuracy on the /eI/–/aI/ pair was 56.25%. L2
listeners had lower accuracy on the /E/–/æ/ pair than on the
/eI/–/aI/ pair too, with accuracy of 75.44% and 89.19%, respec-
tively. However, both accuracies were higher than those of the
ASR models.
When we compare trained L2 listeners with the trained
ASR models on the SSN test set, L2 listeners after training with
different babble conditions obtained improvement from both
babble conditions; however, the accuracy of the TDNN model
became lower in both babble conditions, with a total average
improvement on 2-talker babble of -9.37% and on 6-talker bab-
ble of -6.25%. The negative improvement mostly came from
the /E/–/æ/ pair. The Wav2Vec2.0 model showed improvement
in both babble conditions, which was in line with the L2 lis-
teners’ results. The total average improvement in the 2-talker
babble was 3.12%, and in the 6-talker babble, it was 9.37%.

4. Discussion and Conclusions
In the current research, we first conducted a perception experi-
ment on L2 listeners, training them with 2- and 6-talker babble
conditions to study whether the L2 listeners could benefit from
babble training and which one could benefit more. Then, we ap-
plied the same babble conditions to ASR models to investigate
whether ASR models could also benefit from babble training
conditions like L2 listeners do, or if it would be better to fine-
tune ASR models using the same background noise as the test
set. The results from the experiment with L2 listeners suggested
that perceptual training with babble was more effective for help-
ing learners perceive nonnative vowels in SSN than in quiet,
and listeners could gain accuracy improvement from both bab-
ble training conditions, but no significant difference was found
between 2-and 6-talker groups which answers our first research
question.
In our comparison of L2 listeners and ASR models experi-
ment, the overall accuracy in the SSN test set was lower than in
the quiet test set for both ASR models and L2 listeners, which
indicated that phoneme discrimination is more difficult in SSN
than quiet for both L2 listeners and ASR models. For the re-
sults in the quiet test set, as can be seen in Table 2, the results
from both L2 listeners and ASR models showed that the ac-
curacy of /eI/–/aI/ pair was higher than the /E/–/æ/ pair both in
before and after training, which suggests that the discrimina-
tion of the /eI/–/aI/ pair was easier than the /E/–/æ/ pair for both
ASR models and L2 listeners. When comparing L2 listeners
with the TDNN model under the 6-talker babble training, the
results showed that L2 listeners, before training, had better dis-
crimination ability than the pre-trained TDNN model. However,
after training with the same training set, the difference became
smaller, and the average accuracies of the pretest and posttest
sets of the two pairs were close to the accuracies of L2 listen-
ers. When comparing L2 listeners with the Wav2Vec2.0 model,
the pre-trained Wav2Vec2.0 model already achieved average ac-
curacies for the pretest and posttest sets that were close to the
accuracies of L2 listeners, for both phoneme pairs. The total av-
erage accuracy in the Wav2Vec2.0 model improved more under
the 2-talker babble than 6-talker babble training condition, and
after training with 2-talker babble, the average accuracy of two
pairs was higher than that of trained L2 listeners.
For the results in the SSN test set, when comparing L2
listeners with the TDNN model, we observed that L2 listen-
ers could benefit from both babble training conditions. How-
ever, for the TDNN model, both babble training conditions did
not improve the accuracy. When comparing L2 listeners with
the Wav2Vec2.0 model, we found that, although the pre-trained
Wav2Vec2.0 model performed worse than L2 listeners in the
accuracies of both pairs, it could still benefit from both bab-
ble training conditions, similar to L2 listeners. When compar-
ing the TDNN model with the Wav2Vec2.0 model under the 6-
talker group, the pre-trained TDNN model and the Wav2Vec2.0
model showed similar phoneme discrimination ability. How-
ever, after training with 6-talker babble, the average accuracy
of both phoneme pairs decreased for the TDNN model and in-
creased for the Wav2Vec2.0 model. Based on the above re-
sults, we can answer our second research question: Only the
Wav2Vecw2.0 model can benefit from babble training in both
quiet and SSN test sets, and its improvement trend is closer to
L2 listeners compared to the TDNN model.
After training ASR models with babble conditions, we fur-
ther studied training models with quiet and SSN conditions.
When it comes to the quiet test set, training the TDNN model
with the quiet set did not improve the accuracy, and the to-
tal average improvement decreased.
However, training the
Wav2Vec2.0 model with the quiet set increased the total aver-
age improvement. We believe that the TDNN model was more
fitting to the training set in our experiment, but Wav2Vec2.0
was underfitting to the training set, and has better generaliza-
tion ability to the test set. When training ASR models with the
SSN set, we found no improvement in the total average accu-
racy of the Wav2Vec2.0 model, and it was worse in the TDNN
model. With regard to the SSN test set, training ASR models
with the quiet set made the models’ accuracy decreased. When
the training and test sets have the same SSN background noise,
both the TDNN and Wav2Vec2.0 models obtained improve-
ment. Thus, we can conclude that when ASR models are trained
with the same background noise for both training and test sets,
the TDNN model shows more improvement on SSN sets but
no improvement on the quiet set. Conversely, the Wav2Vec2.0
model exhibits consistent performance: training with the quiet
set improves accuracy on the quiet test set, while training with
the SSN set improves accuracy on the SSN test set. The answer
to our third research question is as follows: the Wav2Vec2.0
model benefits more from 6-talker babble training, with a total
average improvement of 9.37% (Table 2), higher than the 7.81%
improvement from SSN training (Table 3), but the TDNN model
shows more improvement (18.74%) from SSN training.
There are several limitations in our experiment. First, due
to time constraints, we conducted our experiment only under 2-
and 6-talker babble conditions, without comparing additional
training settings like more talkers, quiet, or SSN backgrounds.
Second, in our experiment with ASR models, we used ASR
models trained only in English. However, L2 listeners can of-
ten have multiple language backgrounds. For future research,
we will conduct more experiments with L2 listeners with other
