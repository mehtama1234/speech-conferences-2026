Abstract 
Speech inversion holds much potential to describe speech errors 
in childhood speech sound disorders. However, the clinical 
interpretability of speech inversion tract variables is unknown. 
This study is the first to show, through linear mixed modeling, 
that acoustic-to-articulatory speech inversion can quantify 
statistically significant articulatory differences between several 
perceptually salient subtypes of /ɹ/ and /s/ speech sound errors, 
and correct /ɹ/ and /s/ targets, in American English. 
Index Terms: acoustic-to-articulatory speech inversion, child 
speech science, speech sound disorders 
1. Introduction and Motivation 
1.1. Articulatory feedback for speech sound disorders 
Childhood speech sound disorders reflect neurodevelopmental 
impairments in speech sound production [1]. Errors persisting 
past age 8 are thought to reflect an articulatory difficulty with 
underlying somatosensory or auditory-perceptual targets, most 
frequently impacting rhotics (i.e., /ɹ/) and sibilants (e.g., /s/) in 
American English. During speech therapy, clinicians often 
provide two types of motor learning feedback [2]. Knowledge 
of results motor learning feedback reflects a binary “correct”/ 
“not quite ” summary of the movement outcome . In contrast, 
knowledge of performance feedback provides corrective 
articulatory instructions (e.g., “raise your tongue blade” for /ɹ/). 
Knowledge of performance feedback is important for 
articulatory speech sound errors, in which subtle articulatory 
differences may result in perceptually distinct speech sound 
error subtypes. This study explores if acoustic -to-articulatory 
speech inversion can quantify clinically interpretable 
information about speech sound error subtypes produced by 
children and adolescents with speech sound disorders. 
1.2. Acoustic-to-articulatory speech inversion 
Speech inversion is the process of inferring articulatory 
movements from the speech signal, allowing for kinematic 
speech analyses in datasets that do not have a kinematic ground 
truth [3]. The Espy-Wilson Speech Inversion System [4, 5] is 
designed to predict the degree and location of vocal tract 
constrictions in the Articulatory Phonology framework [6], 
which normalizes speaker differences in vocal tract size [7] to 
promote speaker independent systems. Vocal tract constrictions 
in the Articulatory Phonology framework are modeled by six 
vocal tract variables : lip aperture (LA), lip protrusion (LP), 
tongue tip constriction location (TTCL), tongue tip constriction 
degree (TTCD), tongue body constriction location (TBCL), and 
tongue body constriction degree (TBCD ). Research has 
demonstrated that incorporating source information 
(fundamental frequency, F0; periodicity, PER; and aperiodicity, 
APER) improves speech inversion inference [5, 8]. 
2. Related Works and Contributions 
2.1. Speech inversion tract variable constrictions 
Geometric transformations convert the ground truth kinematic 
pellet locations to Articulatory Phonology tract variables [9, 
10]. For this study, we considered only tongue tract variables 
and also inverted tract variable signs relative to [10], so positive 
values reflected more anterior and tighter constrictions. TTCD 
is the minimum distance between pellet T1 and the maxilla 
trace. TTCL is the horizontal offset of pellet T1 from the incisor 
origin at the point of tightest TTCD. TBCD is the minimum 
distance between an arc through pellets T2-T4 and the trace of 
the maxilla/velum/pharynx. TBCL is the horizontal offset of the 
T2-T4 arc from the incisor origin at the point of tightest TBCD. 
2.2. Speech sound error subtypes 
2.2.1. American English /ɹ/ 
American English / ɹ/ involves a complex vocal tract 
configuration that can be categorized as "bunched" or 
"retroflexed." Both variations share features like pharyngeal 
narrowing and lateral bracing of the tongue against the upper 
molars, but differ in tongue tip elevation relative to the tongue 
body. For "derhotic" /ɹ/ productions, midsagittal imaging may 
show a too-low tongue tip or blade, a too-high posterior tongue 
dorsum, and/or limited tongue root retraction [11]. Coronal 
imaging may reveal insufficient lateral bracing. Perceptually, 
derhotic / ɹ/ can have characteristics of other approximant 
sounds (i.e., /w/, /l/, or /ɰ/), fricatives (i.e., /ɣ/ or /ʁ/), or vowels 
(e.g., /ʌ/) [e.g., 12]. 
2.2.2. American English /s/ 
American English /s/ is articulated near the alveolar ridge, 
where coronal constriction creates a narrow passage for central 
airflow that is reinforced by lateral bracing of the tongue against 
the upper molars. For /s/ distortions, midsagittal imaging may 
show anterior/posterior tongue placement while coronal 
imaging may show insufficient lateral bracing. Distorted /s/ can 
have perceptual characteristics of /θ/, /ɬ/, or /ʃ/ [13].

2.3. Speech inversion for speech sound disorders 
Two prior studies show that adult -trained speech inversion 
neural networks (as in this study) , can be sensitive to clinical 
child speech, despite a current lack of child speech inversion 
training data . Benway et al. [14] showed t hat adult -trained 
speech inversion inference can be highly sensitive to vocal tract 
gesture coordination differences for two subtypes of speech 
sound disorder, with and without childhood apraxia of speech, 
in 96 children as young as 7 (AUROC = .90). Source variable 
ablation significantly lowered performance compared to lip 
ablation, tongue ablation, and full models (ΔAUROC = -.19, p 
< .001), implicating source-filter coordination in distinguishing 
speech sound disorder subtypes . Benway et al. [15] showed 
speech inversion feature sets (median AUROC = .90) increase 
accuracy of binary classifiers over formants alone (median 
AUROC = .86) for every speaker in the [16] clinical trial. 
2.4. Hypotheses and contributions 
It is unknown if speech inversion estimates are sensitive to 
vocal tract gesture error subtypes for /ɹ/ and /s/ , so this study 
explored if tract variable values differ between error subtypes 
and perceptually correct sounds . For example, articulatory 
phonetics [17] posits that dentalized /s/ should have a more 
anterior TTCL than correct /s/. For this preliminary report, one 
a priori articulatory hypothesis (Table 1) was selected for each 
speech sound. A paired phoneme whose articulation was 
theoretically related to the speech error subtype was chosen to 
aid interpretation. The rationale was that if a hypothesis was not 
supported (i.e., if dentalized /s/ was found to have more 
posterior TTCL values than correct /s/ ), examining patterns in 
interdental /θ/ tract variables relative to dentalized /s/ might 
clarify if unexpected results reflect speech inversion validity for 
the task . Hypotheses were constructed based on articulatory 
phonetics conventions and our team’s familiarity with clinical 
ultrasound tongue imaging for /ɹ/ and /s/ biofeedback. These 
hypotheses were analyzed through estimated marginal means 
from linear mixed-effects models. Our contribution shows that 
speech inversion, trained on adult ground-truth kinematic data, 
quantifies clinically interpretable information about tongue 
constriction and location for the investigated articulator and 
speech error subtype . The rest of this preliminary report is 
organized as follows: Section 3 describes the child speech 
dataset and perceptual labeling process , then adult speech 
inversion training and testing . Section 4 analyzes the child 
speech research questions and hypotheses using the inferences 
from the adult speech inversion model described in Section 3.

3. Methods 
3.1. Dataset 
Data come from several IRB-approved clinical trials , 
longitudinal studies, and cross-sectional studies [18-26]. These 
studies included 118 children and 3 adults aged 3.5– 45 years, 
with most speakers between the ages of 6-17 years (𝑥𝑥̅ = 10.5,
𝜎𝜎 = 4.0). Speakers contributed an average of 134 audio files 
each. Speech audio (words and phrases) were collected through 
wordlist recordings or during speech therapy activities . Audio 
was encoded as 44.1 kHz lossless PCM 16-bit audio in .wav 
containers, and was downsampled to 16 kHz during the study 
as required by the speech processing tools. 
3.1.1. Ground Truth Labels 
Error subtype ground truth is taken from expert clinician 
perceptual ratings with a custom Likert rating scale (Figure 1). 
Raters were masked to participant information and timepoint of 
data collection (e.g., pre-treatment, post-treatment). Because of 
non-perfect agreement among clinician raters, several raters 
rated each file. Not visible in Figure 1 are options to rate the 
subtype of deletion or distortion. For / ɹ/, possible subtypes 
included: /w/-error, /l/-error, vowel error, omitted, and other . 
For /s/, possible subtypes include: dentalized , lateralized, 
palatalized, affricated, stopped, omitted, and other. 
 
 
Figure 1: Sample rating scale (for / ɹ/). Levels scored as 1 -5. 
 
A total of 56 ,929 existing ratings for 17,083 candidate audio 
files from 137 speakers were analyzed for this study. 1,972 files 
were excluded from the analysis because raters indicated issues 
that would impact forced alignment validity (e.g., unusable 
audio, speech other than the target utterance in the recording, or 
omitted error subtypes). 5,961 audio files were retained in the 
analysis: 3,256 for / ɹ/ and 2,431 for /s/. These utterances met 
one of the following criteria: (1) /ɹ/ or /s/ was unanimously rated 
as correct, or (2) a majority (> 2 ) of raters agreed on an error 
subtype. Error subtypes with fewer than 15 files were excluded 
from the analysis (/ɹ/ → /l/, “other” /r/ errors, stopped /s/ errors, 
affricated /s/ errors, and “other” /s/ errors). The study’s 17,083 
candidate audio files were also searched for paired phonemes 
/w/, /l/, /θ/, /ʃ/, and /ʌ/ (Table 1, Table 2), which were assumed 
to be correctly articulated. With these paired phonemes, a total 
of 8,608 audio files were analyzed (Table 2).

3.2. Speech Inversion Model and Tract Variable Inference 
3.2.1 Speech Inversion Training Data 
 
No child data is currently available to train child speech 
inversion models, so t he model in this study was based on the 
University of Wisconsin X-ray Microbeam (XRMB) dataset 
[9], containing speech audio and associated lip/tongue pellet 
data from 21 adult males and 25 adult females). We employed 
data reconstruction techniques [27] to increase the total dataset 
size from 4 to 5.3 hours. Speakers were partitioned into the 
same training set (36 speakers), development set (5 speakers), 
and test set (5 speakers) as [10]. Pellet X-Y coordinates were 
transformed into the tract variables described prior . Tract 
variable values were scaled between [-1, 1] using speaker-level 
min-max normalization. Ground truths for the source variables 
were obtained using the APP detector algorithm [8]. 
3.2.2 Speech Inversion Architecture 
This study’s speech inversion system (Figure 2) uses the self-
supervised WavLM pretrained model [28] to extract speech 
features. The 25 -layer WavLM output was processed through 
two convolutional layers, first reducing it to 16 layers then a 
single-layer representation. This wa s followed by two gated 
recurrent unit layers with 256 and 128 units. To match the target 
100 Hz sampling rate, we upsampled the WavLM 50 Hz output 
by a factor of two. The final feature set was passed through two 
dense layers (128 and 9 units), mapping to six tract and three 
source variables. We optimized the model using Adam with a 
plateau learning rate scheduler, starting at 5e-4, and determined 
an optimal batch size of 8 via grid search. To prevent 
overfitting, we applied early stopping with a patience of 10 
epochs. A weighted combination of Pearson Product Moment 
Correlation (PPMC) and root mean squared error served as the 
loss function.

3.2.3 Speech Inversion Performance on XRMB Test Set 
We utilized the same XRMB test set as [10] to evaluate the 
PPMC performance of the proposed speech inversion system 
for adult speech. As presented in Table 3 , the average PPMC 
correlation across all six tract variables was 0.85, representing 
a 3.93% relative improvement for the same speakers compared 
to the speech inversion system proposed by [10]. Key 
differences in th e present model are the use of WavLM (vs . 
HuBERT) and the estimation of glottal source tract variables 
PER (.93), APER (.88), and F0 (.76), which were not examined 
further in this study. These results demonstrate the efficacy of 
the proposed speech inversion system in enhancing speaker -
independent performance for tract variable estimation.

3.2.4 Tract Variable Inference 
The adult-trained speech inversion model was used to infer the 
tract variable values for the child speech data set . We only 
examined tongue tract variables for the purposes of this report, 
by calculating the average of each tract variable time series 
within the force-aligned interval. 
3.3. Statistical Analysis of Tract Variable Estimates 
Linear mixed model s examined if tract variable values for 
speech error subtypes differed from correct speech sound in 
interpretable ways according to our a priori hypotheses. Two 
separate linear mixed models (one / ɹ/, one /s/) were fit to 
examine the interaction of tract variable channel and speech 
sound on the outcome, tract variable value. A random intercept 
was included to model speaker specific variation, with repeated 
audio files clustered within speakers. Models were fit with lme4 
and estimated marginal means were calculated with emmeans, 
both in R. Reverse pairwise contrasts were applied to compare 
the tract variable values for error subtypes and paired phonemes 
against the tract variable values for correct productions. Both 
linear mixed models converged without fit warnings.

5. Discussion 
The goal of this study was to explore if acoustic-to-articulatory 
speech inversion quantified clinically interpretable information 
about speech sounds produced by children and adolescents with 
speech sound disorders. The long-term goal of this line of work 
is to motivate speech inversion tract variable inference as 
clinically interpretable speech measures for machine learning. 
Our study shows that, for four of our five a priori 
hypotheses, adult -trained speech inversion data provides 
clinically intuitive articulatory information about child speech 
that aligns with articulatory phonetic conventions [17]. Speech 
inversion estimates indicated that /ɹ / → /w/ errors had a more 
posterior tongue body than correct /ɹ/, /ɹ/ → vowel errors had a 
lower tongue tip than correct / ɹ/, dentalized /s/ had a more 
anterior tongue tip than correct /s/, and palatalized /s/ had a 
more posterior tongue tip than correct /s/. Furthermore, 
evidence from paired phoneme tract variables supports the 
common clinical notion that /ɹ/ → /w/ errors reflect 
intermediary gestural and perceptual elements between / ɹ/ and 
/w/, /ɹ/ → vowel errors are intermediary to /ɹ/ and /ʌ/, dentalized 
/s/ errors are intermediary to /ɹ/ and /θ/, and palatalized /s/ errors 
are intermediary to /s/ and /ʃ/. It is notable that these articulatory 
differences were noted even using speech signal processing 
methods that downsample audio to 16 kHz, given that child 
fricatives can exceed 8 kHz [29]. 
While TBCD was significantly different in lateralized /s/ 
versus correct /s/, the effect was in the opposite direction than 
we hypothesized. The intuition driving our hypothesis was that 
lateral bracing supports central airflow in correct /s/, lateral /s/ 
airflow reflects insufficient lateral bracing, and insufficient 
lateral bracing would be reflected by a lower tongue body along 
the midsagittal plane than a tongue with sufficient lateral 
bracing. Without a phonemic /ɬ/ in American English, /l/ was 
selected as a paired phoneme. Given that speech inversion 
suitably captured the articulation of other phonemes, the results 
for lateral /s/ may indicate that there are more complex tongue 
dynamics at work for lateralized /s/ , that midsagittal speech 
inversion might not sufficiently capture lateral tongue gestures, 
and/or that /l/ is not a suitable analogy for lateralized /s/. Our 
ongoing work investigates each of these possibilities. 
Our ongoing work also expands upon the study reported 
here. We are examining additional tract variable hypotheses for 
each speech error subtype . The analysis is also expanding to 
account for the simultaneous impact of constriction and degree. 
The need to examine both simultaneously is highlighted by the 
simultaneous difference in constriction location and degree 
evident in the Tongue Tip panel of Figure 3 . This same figure 
panel also appears to show two clusters in correct / ɹ/, possibly 
representing bunched and retroflexed / ɹ/, and our future work 
aims to explore this further. It is also possible that using time -
averaged tract variables has obscured important time series 
information, which our future will address. Finally, this study 
leaves an open question of how speech inversion tract variable 
inference would differ if they were measured by a speech 
inversion system validated for child speech, which is a direct 
goal of our current research. 
6. Conclusions 
Acoustic-to-articulatory speech inversion, trained on adult 
ground-truth kinematic data, quantifies clinically interpretable 
information about tongue constriction and location for /ɹ/ → 
/w/, /ɹ/ → vowel, dentalized /s/, and palatalized /s/ speech sound 
errors in children and adolescents with speech sound disorders.
