Band-Split Self-supervised Mamba for Infant-centered Audio Analysis
Xulin Fan1,2, Jialu Li2, Mark Hasegawa-Johnson1,2, Nancy L. McElwain2,3
1Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, University of Illinois, USA
2Beckman Institute for Advanced Science and Technology, University of Illinois, USA

xulinf2@illinois.edu, jialuli3@illinois.edu, jhasegaw@illinois.edu, mcelwn@illinois.edu
Abstract
Infant-worn audio recorders provide a valuable means to
analyze an infant’s home environment and vocal interactions
with family members.
Recent advances in self-supervised
learning on large unlabeled datasets and supervised training on
limited annotated data have improved performance in this do-
main. However, data scarcity remains a challenge. We intro-
duce Band-Split SSAMBA (BS-SSAMBA), a self-supervised
representation learning method that incorporates band-specific
projections and a band-agnostic Mamba encoder to model tem-
poral relationships across frequency bands. Designed for data-
efficient learning, BS-SSAMBA effectively leverages both un-
labeled and labeled in-domain data. Through extensive experi-
ments on family audio recordings, we show that BS-SSAMBA
outperforms vanilla SSAMBA and wav2vec2-based models,
demonstrating its effectiveness for infant-centered audio tag-
ging.
Index Terms: infant-centered audio analysis, speaker diariza-
tion, vocalization classification, self-supervised learning
1. Introduction
Infants communicate a wide range of needs and emotions
through vocalizations, such as cries, fusses, and babbles. Care-
givers’ timely and appropriate responses to infants’ vocal
cues [1] and infant-caregiver vocal turn-taking [2] [3] have im-
portant implications for social-emotional [4] [5], cognitive [6],
and language [7] development. Because infants and mothers
show meaningful differences in behavior, including vocaliza-
tions, across lab and home contexts [8] [9] and because father-
infant [10] and sibling-infant interactions [11] also play impor-
tant roles in infant development, automated diarization of in-
fants’ and family members’ vocalizations across daylong home
recordings has the potential to transform scientific understand-
ing of these early yet critical developmental processes. To ac-
complish such diarization tasks, audio tagging has emerged as
a powerful tool for labeling audio segments or frames with in-
formation about who is speaking (e.g., infant, adult) and the
type of vocalization (e.g., crying, babbling). Previous works on
infant-centered audio have largely relied on fully supervised ap-
proaches, such as infant vocalization classification [12, 13, 14],
cry classification [15], and speaker diarization [16], but these
methods often suffer from limited performance due to the
scarcity of labeled data.
Recent advances in self-supervised learning (SSL) are
transforming how we tackle audio tagging, particularly in do-
mains with limited labeled data. By leveraging large-scale unla-
beled audio, models such as wav2vec2.0 [17], the Audio Spec-
trogram Transformer (AST) [18], and Self-Supervised Audio
Mamba (SSAMBA) [19] learn high-level representations before
fine-tuning on small labeled datasets. Compared with purely
supervised approaches, these SSL methods substantially reduce
the amount of labeled data needed and often yield better per-
formance. Furthermore, spectrogram-based methods like AST
(and its self-supervised variant SSAST [20]) capture frequency-
time structures effectively, yet often require large-scale data
and can be computationally expensive when modeling long se-
quences.
On the other hand, SSAMBA uses a state space
model [21] that scales linearly with sequence length, address-
ing the complexity and memory challenges associated with long
audio contexts.
Despite these advances, audio tagging for infant-centered
home audio recordings remains under-explored due to the
scarcity of in-domain data. Some recent works using infant-
centered recordings have begun to bridge this gap. For instance,
Khan et al. [22] employed a pretrained AST model for environ-
mental sound detection in the home, whereas Li et al. [23] lever-
aged wav2vec2.0 pretraining and finetuning schemes to solve
speaker diarization and vocalization classification for infants
and parents. Both studies demonstrated the potential benefits
of SSL in infant-centered tasks but faced challenges like model
complexity and limited domain-specific data.
In this paper, we propose a new method, Band-Split
SSAMBA (BS-SSAMBA), to further advance SSL of audio rep-
resentation for infant-centered family audio tagging. Below we
outline our major contributions:
1. We introduce Band-Split SSAMBA, a state space model
(SSM) that disentangles spectrogram modeling into frequency
modeling via band-specific projections and temporal modeling
via a Mamba encoder. This design reduces the sequence length
and difficulty of spectrogram modeling for the mamba encoder
and facilitates better representation learning with limited in-
domain data.
2. We incorporate framewise feature conditioning, mak-
ing important cross-band information easily accessible to the
model during fine-tuning, improving efficiency while minimiz-
ing computational overhead.
3. We conduct extensive experiments on unlabeled and la-
beled infant-centered audio recordings in the home. Through
comparison with existing SSL methods, we validate the effec-
tiveness of our band-split mechanism and framewise feature
conditioning.

To mitigate the effects of limited data and improve per-
formance, we propose a hybrid approach that combines band-
specific convolutional kernels and a unified temporal Mamba
encoder, which is shared across frequency bands. This design
enhances data efficiency and strengthens feature learning, mak-
ing the model more robust in the case of limited in-domain data.
Furthermore, we propose to incorporate framewise feature con-
ditioning to mitigate the model’s lack of cross-band modeling
ability.
2.1. Spectrogram Representation
The input audio waveform is first transformed into a 128-
dimensional mel-spectrogram, providing a time-frequency rep-
resentation of the signal. This transformation is performed us-
ing the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a 25 ms
window and a 10 ms stride, producing a spectrogram matrix
X of dimensions F × T, where F represents the number of
frequency bins and T denotes the number of time frames. The
spectrogram is then partitioned into overlapping subbands, re-
sulting in a final structure of dimensions B×K×T, where B is
the number of subbands and K is the number of frequency bins
per subband. Finally, each subband spectrogram is further di-
vided into 2D patches, forming patch sequences for downstream
processing.
2.2. Band-specific Projection
To ensure that the Mamba encoder processes patch sequences
from different subbands consistently, we employ linear projec-
tion layers with independent weights for each band. These lay-
ers map patches from different subbands into a unified embed-
ding space, enabling a coherent representation across all sub-
bands.
2.3. Mamba Encoder
To prepare the patch sequences for input into the Mamba en-
coder, we prepend band-specific tokens to each sequence to in-
dicate the corresponding band and apply a shared positional
embedding. Specifically, for a patch sequence from band b,
denoted as [Eb,1, Eb,2, Eb,3, . . . , Eb,T ], we prepend a band-
specific token Bb and add a learnable positional embedding
[P0, P1, P2, . . . , PT ] to the sequence. The final input sequence
to the Mamba encoder is then represented as:
[Bb + P0, Eb,1 + P1, Eb,2 + P2, . . . , Eb,T + PT ]
The band token Bb allows Mamba to learn band-specific in-
formation, while the positional embedding Pt helps Mamba to
learn temporal relationships. Since temporal structures are con-
sistent across different bands, we use a shared positional em-
bedding sequence for all bands.
The Mamba encoder consists of stacked bidirectional SSMs
as proposed in Vision Mamba [24]. The bidirectional SSM pro-
cesses patch sequences in both forward and backward direc-
tions, effectively capturing temporal dependencies from both
perspectives.
2.4. Training Objectives
During pretraining, we apply both generative and discriminative
losses to patch sequences from each of the B frequency bands.
We randomly select N indices from the total S patch positions
in each band, denoted by the set M ⊂{1, . . . , S}, where S is
the number of patches per band and N = |M|. The patches
at these selected positions are masked across all bands. The
masked tokens’ contextualized embeddings from the mamba
encoder are passed to two prediction heads: a generative head
and a classification head. The output of these heads are opti-
mized via Mean Squared Error (MSE) (Lg) and InfoNCE losses
(Ld), respectively.

2.5. Framewise Feature Conditioning
Although we successfully reduce the sequence length through
band splitting, thereby decreasing the complexity of modeling
long sequences, this approach also limits the model’s ability to
capture cross-band relationships. For the audio tagging task of
interest, certain framewise characteristics play a crucial role in
distinguishing different classes in the home audio recordings.
For example, because the recording device is worn by the in-
fant, the distance to adult caregivers—and consequently, the
volume of their voices—serves as a strong cue for distinguish-
ing whether an adult is speaking to another adult or to the infant.
Because Band-Split SSAMBA has a straightforward mech-
anism for cross-band modeling, it may struggle to efficiently
extract framewise features across all bands. To address this, we
explicitly extract the framewise energy of the spectrogram and
use it as an additional conditioning cue for the final classifier
during fine-tuning. We adopt Featurewise Linear Modulation
(FiLM, [25]) as our conditioning mechanism, which can be for-
mulated as:

3.2. Experimental Configuration
We pretrain the models for 15 epochs using the Adam optimizer
with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of 32. We set
λ = 10 as the weighting term for the pretraining loss and N =
192 as the number of masked timesteps. Training was done on
4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs where pretraining takes around 3 days.
After pretraining, we fine-tune the model using binary cross-
entropy loss with mixup training [27] (mixup ratio = 0.6) and
SpecAugment (maximum frequency mask length = 48 frames).
The same optimizer is used for fine-tuning. The model is fine-
tuned for five epochs, and we select the checkpoint with the best
kappa score for evaluation. We use the base configuration from
SSAMBA [19] for our mamba encoder, consisting of 24 layers
with an embedding dimension of 768.

3.3. Main Results
We present our main results and comparisons with baseline
methods in Table 2.
As baselines, we include Wav2Vec2-
LittleBeats (W2V-LB) [23] and SSAMBA [19]. All the models
we compare have similar size: W2V-LB has 95M parameters,
SSAMBA 101M parameters, BS-SSAMBA 102M parameters.
For W2V-LB, we use the official released checkpoint,
which was pretrained on the same unlabeled dataset, and then
fine-tuned using our labeled data. The SSAMBA models are
pretrained and finetuned with most of the hyperparameters as
released in [19], except number of masked patches and data
augmentation techniques. We evaluate variants with different
patch sizes while keeping the percentage of masked patches
fixed at 75% and use the same specaugment and mixup train-
ing technique as used by BS-SSAMBA. We find that the de-
fault 16×16 patch size yields suboptimal performance for both
SSAMBA and BS-SSAMBA. The primary reason for this per-
formance drop appears to be the lack of temporal resolution,
which is crucial for our downstream task requiring label resolu-
tion of 10 frames (0.1s).
From the main results, we observe that BS-SSAMBA out-
performs both W2V-LB and all tested SSAMBA variants across
different patch sizes. Specifically, vanilla SSAMBA lags sig-
nificantly behind W2V-LB, whereas BS-SSAMBA successfully
closes this gap through the advantages introduced by the band-
split method.

3.4. Ablation Studies
In this section, we present several key ablation studies regarding
BS-SSAMBA to evaluate the effect of different components and
various hyperparameters.
Impact of Framewise Feature Conditioning: Table 3 presents
our ablation study on framewise feature conditioning. We test
BS-SSAMBA with and without framewise feature conditioning
during finetuning with other configurations fixed. The results
reflect the effectiveness of incorporating our proposed condi-
tioning methods into the BS-SSAMBA framework.
Impact of Model Size: Table 4 presents our ablation study on
model size. In general, larger BS-SSAMBA models with more
layers and larger embeddings yield better results. However, per-
formance differences across model sizes are small, and even the
smallest 6M-parameter model achieves competitive results.
Impact of Number of Masked Patches: Table 5 presents our
ablation study on different N, the number of masked patches in
each band, ranging from masking 25% to 75% of patches on
the temporal axis. In general, we observe an increase on the
downstream infant-centered audio tagging performance when
we mask out a larger portion of patches with our optimal setting
at 75%.
4. Conclusion
We propose Band-Split SSAMBA, which integrates band-
specific projections and a band-agnostic Mamba encoder into
the Self-Supervised Audio Mamba framework. These modifica-
tions incorporate human knowledge of the spectrogram into the
model, reducing sequence length for seq-to-seq modeling and
facilitating efficient spectrogram representation learning with
limited in-domain data. We evaluate our approach on unlabeled
and labeled audio recordings from infant-centered home audio
recordings. Our experimental results advance audio SSL tech-
niques with limited in-domain data. Such advances are central
to achieving a fuller understanding of this key development pro-
cess: infants’ everyday vocal interactions with family members.
