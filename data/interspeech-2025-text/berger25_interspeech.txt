Non-Standard Accent TTS Support via Large Multi-Accent Frontend
Pronunciation Knowledge Transfer
Noe Berger, Siqi Sun, Korin Richmond
The University of Edinburgh, Scotland, UK
noe@alum.vassar.edu, Siqi.Sun@ed.ac.uk, Korin.Richmond@ed.ac.uk
Abstract
Mainstream text-to-speech (TTS) applications rarely offer
non-standard accented voice options, perhaps in part due to
practical challenges associated with the acquisition of prerequi-
site training data. Building on prior research, this work demon-
strates that a large multi-accent neural frontend model can re-
duce pronunciation training data requirements by 95% for ro-
bust performance in low-resource accents. We further show
that pronunciation knowledge transfer is weakly influenced
by accent similarity, quantified using a Levenshtein distance-
based metric. The large multi-accent paradigm thus emerges as
an effective strategy for improving non-standard accent voice-
building accessibility, provided source accents are selected to
maximize similarity with target accents where possible.
Index Terms: TTS, multi-accent, low-resource, linguistic fron-
tend, transfer learning
1. Introduction
Written text can correspond to strikingly different pronuncia-
tions among speakers of a common language. Commercial text-
to-speech (TTS) applications, however, generally model only
those pronunciations associated with a small subset of highly
standard accents. This lack of non-standard accent represen-
tation might lead some to hesitate to interact with voice tech-
nologies altogether, because they fail to deliver natural experi-
ences, and might exacerbate negative attitudes towards speakers
of such varieties [1, 2].
While a lack of non-standard TTS accent options might
cause material representational harm, building systems to ac-
commodate them is non-trivial. High-quality, state-of-the-art
(SOTA) speech synthesis typically requires copious data, but
the socio-pragmatic status of non-standard accents makes them
necessarily low-resource. Previous work shows the use of a dis-
tinct neural frontend module reduces the amount of audio data
needed to produce adequate pronunciation performance com-
pared to fully end-to-end systems, by maximizing lexical cov-
erage in its associated pronunciation datasets [3]. However,
this strategy relies instead on the availability of large hand-
transcribed pronunciation datasets, which are difficult to acquire
for many non-standard accents because there are often few, if
any, expert users of the relevant language varieties able to pro-
duce those transcriptions.
Sun and Richmond [4] present a way to reduce the volume
of pronunciation data needed to train neural frontends for novel
accents, given existing data associated with other accents. Their
“multi-accent” strategy lays the groundwork for keeping both
text and audio data to a minimum, which is essential to facilitate
greater non-standard accent voice-building accessibility.
Sun and Richmond [4] demonstrate that a multi-accent
frontend can compensate for the sparsity of low-resource ac-
cent pronunciation data with strong lexical coverage in an alter-
nate one. They show that by “augmenting” the training data of
one source accent with extra sentences, the correct pronuncia-
tions for those sentences in a separate target accent (for which
the sentences are unseen) are “transferred” at impressive rates.
Specifically, the researchers report as few as 20k target accent
training sentences (roughly 25.5k word types) yield pronuncia-
tion transfer success commensurate with the accuracy of a base-
line model trained on 206k target accent sentences.
Unfortunately, 20k lines of hand-transcribed pronunciation
data is still far too many for low-resource voice-building con-
texts. Reducing the number to one a single developer could
manage, for example, would be a great next step. Addition-
ally, Sun and Richmond’s reported effect is supported by only
one configuration of source/target accent pair whose degree of
similarity was not quantified. It is possible, however, that the
multi-accent model performs better when source/target accent
sequences have a greater overlap of phonetic characters, which
would be consistent with findings associated with multilingual
large language models [5, 6, 7, 8]. This uncertainty prompts the
need for an extension of Sun and Richmond’s experiments with
new pairs whose degrees of similarity are quantified objectively.
The present work thus expands on Sun and Richmond’s
[4] efforts to reduce the training data needed to support novel-
accent TTS development via multi-accent pronunciation knowl-
edge transfer. We present two novel contributions. First, we find
ceiling-level word transcription accuracy rates can be achieved
via a new record-low number of training data sentences—a
95% decrease from Sun and Richmond’s demonstrated reduc-
tion—by increasing the number of accents in the multi-accent
model. Second, we find that the observed pronunciation knowl-
edge transfer effect that enables a reduction in training data vol-
ume is indeed weakly mediated by the similarity of source and
target accent pairs, as quantified via Levenshtein distance. The
large multi-accent paradigm therefore emerges as a robust tech-
nique for adding non-standard accent frontend coverage, pro-
vided developers seek to maximize pronunciation knowledge
transfer by using a maximally similar source accent.
2. Model Architecture
All models presented in this work are neural Sequence-to-
Sequence (Seq2Seq) frontend models whose structures and hy-
perparamters adhere to Sun and Richmond’s [4] design. For a
full account, the reader is referred to their work.
The frontend model translates a text sentence character se-
quence x1:T = [x1,x2, ...,xT ] to a linguistic specification charac-
ter sequence y1:L = [y1,y2, ...,yL] (where T and L are lengths)
via a bidirectional LSTM encoder and unidirectional LSTM de-

3. Data
3.1. Source Text & Target Linguistic Specification
Training and validation datasets are derived from the Libri-TTS
corpus and test datasets are from the HiFi-TTS corpus [10, 11].
Pronunciation training data comprise 14 sets of 199,612 sen-
tences of paired English text/linguistic specification sequences.
Validation and test data comprise 14 sets of 2181 and 4431 sen-
tences of paired sequences, respectively.
Target linguistic specifications are “bootstrapped” from the
Festival (pipeline-based) frontend [12]. A sample sentence is
given in Table 1. The “accent-independent” phoneset system
known asUnilex is utilized to enable the simultaneous modeling
of many unique accents with a single shared phone superset.1

3.2. Accents & Bootstrapping Procedure
The creators of Unilex maintain a rule-system for mapping
baseline phone sequences to accent-specific ones, for 14 dis-
tinct English accents. All 14 accents, displayed in Table 2, are
included in this study.
Festival’s frontend pipeline involves a lexicon look-up and
statistical grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) back-off. To minimize
error associated with ground-truth pronunciation data, we only
bootstrap sentences for which each word is contained in Festi-
val’s lexicon, consistent with Sun and Richmond [4]. We also
repeat their exclusion of text normalization from the definition
of the present frontend task. Frontend pronunciation data is thus
attained by removing “out-of-vocabulary” (OOV) tokens from
the Libri-TTS and Hifi-TTS corpora, and then passing them,
line by line, through 14 unique configurations of Festival’s fron-
tend, each initialized with a different accent-specific translation
of the master lexicon (made possible by the Unilex accent trans-
lation rules).

Overall, our results show a large number of accents can be suc-
cessfully covered by a single model.
4.2. Scaling of Pronunciation Knowledge Transfer with
Large Multi-Accent Models
Next, we evaluate the performances of multi-accent models
trained on increasingly smaller datasets but augmented for one
source accent only with a separate target accent’s test data sen-
tences, to determine the smallest size that yields ceiling-level
knowledge transfer results. EDI is designated as target accent
for all experiments. The augmented source accent is selected
such that it and EDI form a high-similarity pair: ABD1 (simi-
larity score = 0.99). In these experimental configurations, target
accent training data is sampled randomly without replacement
from the Libri-TTS data such that none of those sentences are
also used as training data for the other accents (but all other ac-
cents share the same training sentences). 3 The lack of overlap
between target accent and other accent training data replicates
Sun and Richmond’s [4] design and reflects the fact that newly
acquired data for novel accent development may be unique. The
reduced-data experimental models are compared to a ceiling-
baseline model in which the full Libri-TTS dataset is used as
training data for all 14 accents (but one source is still augmented
as in the experimental models), as depicted in Figure 3.

While the moderate-similarity accent pair models appear to
behave similarly to the high-similarity pair ones, there is evi-
dence of a reduction in the strength of pronunciation knowledge
transfer upon substituting these with low-similarity pair mod-
els. Specifically, the lowest successful training data size of 1k
sentences illustrated with {ABD1/EDI} does not replicate with
{GNZ/EDI}—a significant decrease in unseen word accuracy
rate occurs at this threshold compared to its ceiling-baseline
(p < 0.05). This finding suggests the similarity of source and
target accent is indeed a relevant factor to consider when devel-
oping multi-accent strategies for low-resource accent frontend
support, although even moderate similarity appears to facilitate
success.
5. Discussion
The evidence presented in this work is limited by two design
choices. First, the finding that accent similarity affects pronun-
ciation knowledge transfer is based on only one example of each
similarity type (low, moderate, high), whereas replication with
additional pairs would provide stronger evidence. Second, the
use of bootstrapped pronunciation data might limit the external
validity of these experiments, as the “real-world” annotations it
seeks to simulate may be error-prone to some degree. Future re-
search should consider data noising techniques to more closely
model human data [15]. Nevertheless, we find compelling pre-
liminary evidence that the use of a large multi-accent frontend
model enables strong linguistic specification transfer, provided
the similarity between source and target accents is maximized.
A compelling next step would be to develop a single pre-
trained multi-accent model that could be fine-tuned for any new
low-resource accent desired. While additional experimentation
would be needed to establish the optimal model calibration that
avoids so-called catastrophic forgetting, or over-application of
the target accent’s phonology at the cost of knowledge con-
tributed by the other accents’ pronunciations, such work could
lead to even further advances [16].
6. Conclusion
In this work, we show a large neural multi-accent frontend
model can facilitate strong linguistic specification transfer with
a record low number of novel accent training examples—1k for
high and moderate-similarity pair configurations. This reduc-
tion in training data volume needed to produce robust frontend
behavior suggests the large neural multi-accent frontend can im-
prove non-standard accent voice-building accessibility by sub-
stantially reducing its seemingly insurmountable data sourcing
obstacle. The failure to replicate this new record with a low-
similarity accent pair configuration, on the other hand, indicates
that careful planning should go into the selection of the source
accent to maximize pronunciation knowledge transfer success
where possible.
Finally, while we believe the improvement of non-standard
accent representation is important as we strive for greater inclu-
sivity in science and technology, a word of caution is raised. It
is possible that the availability of some non-standard accents in
certain mainstream TTS applications could lead to situations re-
garded as appropriation and/or mockery [17]. System develop-
ers must therefore carefully consider the ethical implications of
their particular application before seeking to introduce new ac-
cents, and establish safeguards to help mitigate potential harms
[18].
