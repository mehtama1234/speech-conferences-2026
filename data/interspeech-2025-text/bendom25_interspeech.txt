Introducing EMOPARKNZ: the Emotional Speech Database from New
Zealand English Speakers with Parkinson’s Disease
Itay Ben-Dom1, Catherine I. Watson1, Clare M. McCann1
1University of Auckland, New Zealand
iben350@aucklanduni.ac.nz, c.watson@auckland.ac.nz, c.mccann@auckland.ac.nz
Abstract
This paper introduces EMOPARKNZ, a new speech
database designed to study emotional speech production in
New Zealand English speakers with Parkinson’s disease. The
database features 13 speakers (7 male, 6 female) and 1,950
speech recordings expressing five primary emotions: excited,
angry, happy, sad, and neutral.
The design considerations,
participant selection, and recording procedures are detailed.
Acoustic analysis of the database revealed significant differ-
ences in fundamental frequency, intensity, and speech rate
across emotional states. A preliminary perception test with 22
New Zealand English listeners achieved 63% classification ac-
curacy across the five emotions, aligning with the performance
reported for similar emotional speech databases. The results
indicate EMOPARKNZ is a valuable resource for advancing re-
search in emotion communication for individuals with Parkin-
son’s disease and speech emotion classification.
Index Terms: Emotional Speech Corpus, New Zealand En-
glish, Parkinson’s Disease, Acoustic Analysis, Perception Test
1. Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative
movement disorder that significantly impacts motor function
and quality of life [1]. Speech disorders are among the most
prevalent symptoms, affecting an estimated 70–90% of individ-
uals with PD over the course of the disease, with hypokinetic
dysarthria being the most common [2]. Hypokinetic dysarthria
impairs the phonatory, articulatory, respiratory, and prosodic
components of speech.
Emotions play a central role in human communication,
shaping how individuals express themselves and connect with
others [3]. Inability to express emotions not only hinders so-
cial interactions but also exacerbate feelings of isolation and
emotional distress, further diminishing quality of life [4]. PD
significantly affects emotional speech production, where peo-
ple with PD often exhibit reduced modulation of pitch and in-
tensity, altered speech rate, and imprecise articulation, resulting
in monotonous or hypophonic speech [4]. Despite the impor-
tance of understanding how PD affects emotional speech pro-
duction, research on this topic remains limited, particularly for
New Zealand English speakers.
Epidemiological studies indicate that age is the primary
risk factor for PD, with prevalence increasing significantly in
older populations [5]. In Western countries like New Zealand,
increasing life expectancy has led to a rapidly ageing demo-
graphic. Projections from the 2023 New Zealand census sug-
gest that individuals aged 65 years and older will comprise 32%
of the population by 2073 [6]. As PD incidence rises alongside
this demographic shift, there is an urgent need to better under-
stand the disease impact on communication [7], particularly in
the context of New Zealand English.
Existing emotional speech databases primarily focus on
healthy speakers or populations outside of New Zealand, over-
looking the linguistic and prosodic features unique to New
Zealand English. The scarcity of PD-focused corpora is un-
derstandable, as clinical studies face challenges in recruiting
participants. However, the quality of emotion recognition re-
search is heavily influenced by the design of the speech corpus,
which must consider naturalness, linguistic context, and scope
[8]. To address these gaps, we developed EMOPARKNZ, a
novel emotional speech database specifically designed to cap-
ture the vocal characteristics of New Zealand English speakers
with PD. By analysing fundamental frequency, intensity, and
speech rate, this research aims to delineate the alterations in
speech attributable to PD and provide insights into the interplay
between physiological changes and vocal expression in affected
individuals.
While the acoustic analysis provides valuable insights into
the speech characteristics of individuals with PD, perceptual
validation is crucial for understanding how these alterations
affect listeners’ ability to recognise emotions. We conducted
a human perception test of EMOPARKNZ, in which listen-
ers classified emotional expressions in speech samples from
the database. This study offers essential insights into how ac-
curately humans can identify and interpret emotional cues in
Parkinsonian speech, complementing the acoustic analysis with
real-world listener responses [9]. The perceptual test serves as a
validation measure for the accuracy, authenticity and reliability
of the dataset [10].
2. Corpus Design
2.1. Scope
A widely adopted design approach in emotion recognition re-
search is to focus on a concise set of primary emotions, facil-
itating structured and comparable analyses [8]. This study ex-
amines five primary emotions: angry, happy, sad, excited, and
neutral. The inclusion of a neutral or “no emotion” control con-
dition as a baseline is common in studies of emotional speech
[11]. The selection of these specific emotions was guided by
current research trends in emotional speech analysis, ensuring
the database is both relevant and comparable to existing re-
sources [12].

2.3. Stimuli
Emotions were elicited using a variation of the Stanislavski
method [14], in which specific situations and corresponding im-
ages were presented to speakers to evoke and sustain the desired
emotional state. The approach aligned each speaker’s mental
state with the intended emotions throughout the recording ses-
sion. Emotions were recorded in sequence from high to low
arousal: excited, angry, happy, neutral, sad. To ensure consis-
tency, the same leading phrases and images were provided to all
speakers, who were instructed to maintain consistent emotional
intensity. The sentences and prompts used in this study were
adapted from those in the JL corpus [15], a related NZ English
speech dataset developed for strictly-guided emotions. Fifteen
emotionally neutral sentences were chosen (e.g. “Jack views
an art piece”), each consisting of 4-7 syllables to minimise po-
tential emotional drift that may occur in longer phrases. The
complete list of sentences used is available in [16].
2.4. Recording procedure
Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Univer-
sity of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee (UAH-
PEC23603). The recording setup involved an omnidirectional
condenser microphone (AKG, HC 577L) positioned 7 cm at
a 45-degree angle to the right of the speaker’s mouth. This
microphone was connected to a Microsoft Surface Laptop 3
via a pre-amplifier (M-Audio MobilePre [MK II]), with au-
dio capture at a 44.1kHz sample rate with a 16-bit quantisa-
tion. Recordings took place in a sound-treated room within the
Speech Science laboratory at the University of Auckland, New
Zealand. Participants were seated comfortably and faced an
iPad displaying both the sentence prompts and corresponding
emotional images. This arrangement ensured participants were
well-acquainted with the recording environment. Before start-
ing, participants were given five minutes to familiarise them-
selves with the setup and ask questions.
The session began
with a warm-up task, in which participants read the sentence
“Tom beats that farmer” at high intensity. This exercise allowed
for the adjustment of amplification gain levels to prevent clip-
ping, with gain settings fixed for the duration of the session.
The main recording task followed, where participants read sen-
tences displayed on the monitor, each paired with an image rep-
resenting the intended emotion. The first author monitored the
session in real-time to ensure technical quality. Each sentence
prompt was changed upon completion of each emotion expres-
sion. Recording sessions lasted up to one hour, and the entire
process was repeated to ensure two variations of each sentence
for each emotion, resulting in a comprehensive corpus. In to-
tal, the study produced 1950 primary emotion sentences, encap-
sulating responses from 13 participants across 5 primary emo-
tions, each repeated twice with 15 different sentences, all part
of the EMOPARKNZ corpus.
2.5. Methodological challenges
Emotional speech databases are typically categorised into three
types: natural, acted (simulated), and induced (elicited). Nat-
ural emotional speech samples are ideal because they capture
real interactions. However, the databases usually have a lim-
ited number of speakers and suffer from reduced control, short
voice samples, and poor audio quality [17]. In contrast, acted
speech databases involve professional actors portraying emo-
tions, providing greater control, repeatability, and simplicity.
However, they are frequently criticised for being exaggerated
or artificial [18, 19]. A third approach elicits emotions through
stimuli such as images, music, or memory recall. While widely
used, these methods can be passive, failing to engage partici-
pants as real-life emotions do, and may not trigger the physio-
logical responses that influence speech [20]. Ethical concerns
also restrict the use of techniques that induce high-intensity
emotions [9]. Additionally, individual differences in percep-
tion and mood introduce variability, limiting consistency across
recordings. These challenge complicate comparisons of vocal
expressions across different database types [21]. The trade-off
between spontaneity and experimental control remains a key
challenge. Spontaneous emotions offer authenticity but are dif-
ficult to capture, while acted portrayals provide control at the
expense of naturalness [9]. Addressing these methodological
issues is essential for creating reliable speech corpora that ad-
vance emotion recognition research.
3. Emotional Corpus Acoustic Analysis
3.1. Feature extraction
To investigate the impact of PD on vocal expression, six acous-
tic features were analysed: fundamental frequency, intensity,
speech rate, zero-crossing rate, shimmer, and jitter. This paper
focuses on fundamental frequency, intensity, and speech rate,
as these features demonstrated the strongest differentiation be-
tween emotions. The audio files were imported into Python
(v3.12.0) for initial processing, where leading and trailing si-
lences were removed using the pydub package [22]. Funda-
mental frequency values were extracted using the summation of
residual harmonics method, implemented in the covarep pack-
age [23] in MATLAB [24]. Intensity measurements (in dB)
were obtained using the intensity function in the phonetic soft-
ware Praat [25]. Speech rate, measured in syllables per second,
was determined by dividing the total number of syllables in a
sentence by its duration, following the methodology in [26]. For
each acoustic feature, mean values were calculated per sentence
and aggregated per speaker for each primary emotion.

3.2. Results
The mean fundamental frequency, intensity, and speech rate for
each emotion, averaged across all speakers, are shown in Ta-
ble 2. Table 3 presents statistical comparisons between emo-
tion pairs across three acoustic parameters. Statistical analy-
sis using a linear mixed-effects model revealed significant in-
teractions for most emotion pairs with respect to fundamental
frequency, except between angry and happy. The model also
identified a significant two-way interaction between emotion
and gender (χ2
(4) = 1195, p < 0.0001), where females dis-
played higher F0 values across all emotions. For intensity, the
model found significant interactions for all emotion pairs and
a significant two-way interaction between emotion and gender
(χ2
(4) = 1195, p < 0.0001). Males exhibited higher inten-
sity values than females for all emotions except angry. The lin-
ear mixed-effects model also showed that speech rate is signif-
icantly affected by emotion (χ2
(4) = 226, p < 0.001). Speech
for angry was significantly slower than for all other emotions
(p < 0.0001). While gender did not significantly affect speech
rate, a trend suggested that males tended to speak faster than
females across all emotions.
4. Emotional Corpus Evaluation
4.1. Listeners
A human emotion perception test was completed by 22 listeners
(8 male, 14 female), all New Zealand English speakers aged 18
years or older. Among the male listeners, 1 was aged 20-29,
6 were aged 30-39, and 1 was aged 40-49. Among the female
listeners, 5 were aged 20-29, 4 were aged 30-39, 3 were aged
40-49, and 2 were aged 50-59. Only 2 female listeners reported
having experience with voice changes in individuals with PD.
None of the listeners reported any hearing deficits.
4.2. Listening Tasks
The preliminary human perception test was conducted on a sub-
set of the data. Three speakers were selected to represent each
PD severity level. The perception test consisted of 97 ques-
tions and took approximately 15 to 25 minutes to complete. It
began with an introductory slide outlining the purpose of the
test and informed participants that they would be listening to
speech samples from individuals diagnosed with PD. The first
seven questions gathered demographic information, including
age and gender. Participants were also asked about any history
of hearing loss and prior experience interacting with individu-
als with PD. The remaining 90 questions were divided into three
sets of 30 questions, each featuring audio samples from a differ-
ent speaker with varying degrees of disease progression (mild,
mild-moderate, and severe). Prior to the emotion classification
task, participants listened to a neutral sentence spoken by each
speaker to familiarise themselves with the speaker’s vocal style.
This step was not a training phase, as participants were not
asked to classify the neutral sentence into emotion categories
but simply listened to a sentence categorised as neutral. The in-
clusion of a neutral sentence before the perception test, as per
T´oth et al. [9], was found not to bias emotion recognition ac-
curacy in listeners. In total, 90 stimulus sentences (6 sentences
× 5 emotions × 3 speakers; specifically, sentences 1, 5, 6, 9,
11, and 15 as described in [16]) were presented to the listen-
ers.The test was completed using a computer or mobile device
with headphones. The speech stimuli were randomised to mit-
igate potential effects of presentation order. The process was
self-paced, allowing listeners to replay the recordings as many
times as necessary.
4.3. Results
The results presented in Table 4 summarise the human percep-
tion test of emotions involving three distinct speakers. Each
column represents the classification accuracy for one of these
speakers, while the overall column reflects the aggregated re-
sults across all three speakers, providing a broader view of emo-
tion recognition performance across varying PD severity levels.
Negative emotions were generally well recognised across all
PD severity levels, with sad being the most accurately classified
emotion, achieving an overall accuracy of 82%. Angry was also
recognised well, with an overall accuracy of 71%. In contrast,
positive emotions such as happy and excited were more chal-
lenging for listeners to identify, with accuracy of 50% each. The
neutral emotion was classified with moderate accuracy at 60%,
suggesting that listeners found neutral expressions moderately
distinguishable across the speakers. The aggregated confusion
matrix for all three speakers in Figure 1 has an overall classifica-
tion accuracy of 63%. The matrix illustrates the distribution of
predicted emotion labels against the true labels. Each cell rep-
resents the number of instances classified into a given category,
with darker shades indicating higher counts. Frequently con-
fused emotion pairs include sad-neutral, angry-excited, happy-
excited, and happy-neutral.

5. Discussion
The acoustic analysis demonstrates that fundamental frequency,
intensity, and speech rate can be utilised to differentiate emo-
tional states in speech produced by individuals with PD. As
shown in Table 3, significant differences in fundamental fre-
quency were found between most emotion pairs, particularly
between high-arousal emotions (angry, excited, happy) and
lower-arousal ones (sad). These results align with previous re-
search, which suggests that heightened emotional arousal leads
to an increase in fundamental frequency. For instance, the com-
parison between angry and neutral speech reveals a substan-
tial increase in pitch for anger, supporting the idea that anger
raises pitch due to increased vocal tension [29, 30]. In contrast,
sad speech exhibited a lower pitch, consistent with the reduced
vibratory capacity of the vocal folds observed in PD speakers
[31]. Regarding intensity, our analysis shows that high-arousal
emotions tend to be associated with higher mean intensity lev-
els. This trend supports the notion that intensity, like funda-
mental frequency, serves as an arousal-differentiating feature,
reflecting the greater energy demands these emotions place on
speakers. For speech rate, the post-hoc comparisons confirmed
that angry speech was significantly slower than all other emo-
tions (p < 0.0001), suggesting that anger has the most pro-
nounced impact on speech rate. While sad speech was also
slower than happy, excited, and neutral speech, the difference
was less pronounced. No significant differences were found be-
tween happy, excited, and neutral speech rates, implying that
these emotions do not notably affect speech rate relative to each
other.
The results of the perception survey provide strong prelim-
inary evidence validating the EMOPARKNZ corpus. Listen-
ers achieved an overall emotion recognition accuracy of 63%
across five primary emotions, a result consistent with the 69%
accuracy reported for the JL corpus with 60 listeners [15] and
similar to recognition rates around 60% reported in prior stud-
ies [9, 32, 33]. These findings highlight that, despite the vari-
ability introduced by speakers with differing degrees of PD, lis-
teners were able to decode emotions with relatively high ac-
curacy, supporting the EMOPARKNZ corpus as a reliable re-
source for future research on speech emotion perception in PD
populations. The confusion matrix in Figure 1 revealed notable
variations in recognition accuracy across specific emotions. Sad
and angry emotions were among the most accurately identified,
aligning with previous findings [9], whereas happy emotions
posed the greatest challenge. Common misclassifications in-
cluded sad as neutral, angry as excited or neutral, and happy as
excited or neutral. Excited emotions were frequently mistaken
for happy, reflecting the inherent difficulty in distinguishing
emotions with overlapping arousal characteristics. There was
minimal confusion between low-arousal emotions (sad, neutral)
and high-arousal emotions (excited). This finding underscores
the importance of arousal in emotion perception, as suggested
by the Valence-Arousal model, which posits that emotions dif-
fering in arousal are easier to distinguish than those differing
primarily in valence [34]. These results indicate that arousal
may play a more critical role than valence in listeners’ emotion
recognition, particularly when considering the effects of PD-
related changes in speech. A notable methodological consider-
ation is the gender bias in the speaker sample, as this perception
test exclusively featured male speakers. This decision was made
to represent all three levels of PD severity, with the only avail-
able severe PD speaker being male. Consequently, the results
are limited by gender bias. However, this choice is partially jus-
tified by the higher incidence of PD among males, as men are
twice as likely to develop the disease [35]. Thus, the male-only
sample aligns with the demographics of the PD population.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduced the EMOPARKNZ corpus, a unique
database of induced emotion speech featuring dynamic emo-
tional expressions from speakers diagnosed with PD. With 1950
high-quality speech recordings, it offers a valuable resource
for further exploration of emotional expression in PD popula-
tions. Acoustic analysis revealed significant variability in the
prosodic features fundamental frequency, intensity, and speech
rate across different emotions, confirming that these parame-
ters effectively capture emotional content despite the vocal im-
pairments associated with PD. The results from the human per-
ception test involving untrained listeners demonstrated the va-
lidity of the database, providing strong preliminary evidence
of its usefulness in speech emotion recognition research. Fu-
ture research will focus on analysing time- and frequency-
domain acoustic features to train machine learning models in
emotion classification tasks. Additionally, the collected speech
data will be used in a large-scale perceptual study involving a
broader pool of listeners and incorporating all sentences from
the database. Upon the completion of the first author’s doctoral
thesis, the EMOPARKNZ corpus will be made publicly avail-
able. This lays the foundation for future studies aimed at im-
proving emotion recognition technologies and communication
aids for people with PD.
