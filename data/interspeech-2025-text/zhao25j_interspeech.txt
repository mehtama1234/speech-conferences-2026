The Role of Contextual Variation
in Learning Cantonese Tones from Naturalistic Speech
Fengyue Lisa Zhao1, Jennifer Kuo1
1Department of Linguistics, Cornell University, USA
fz227@cornell.edu, jk2767@cornell.edu
Abstract
A central question in speech acquisition is how infants learn
contrastive sound categories from variable acoustic cues. The
Distributional Learning Across Contexts (DLAC) proposal sug-
gests that infants may identify contrastive sound dimensions by
tracking distributions across contexts, with greater contextual
variation signaling contrastive categories. Most research on ac-
quisition of sound categories has focused on simple two-way
vowel and consonant contrasts, leaving open how infants learn
more complex or tonal contrasts. This study addresses these
gaps by analyzing naturalistic speech to examine whether the
ease of learning Cantonese’s six tonal contrasts is linked to their
variation across different contexts. By comparing our findings
with existing acquisition data, we show that this proposal could
predict which contrasts are easier or harder to learn, suggesting
that in absence of invariant acoustic cues, infants may rely on
contextual variation to acquire complex sound categories.
Index Terms: Cantonese, tone, sound category learning, distri-
butional learning, acquisition, contextual variation
1. Introduction
Linguistically, two sounds are contrastive if they differentiate
meaning—for example, English [l] and [r] distinguish words
like “led” and “red”. However, even before infants have ac-
cess to top-down information such as word meaning, they be-
gin learning the sound categories of their language [1, 2].
This bottom-up learning process involves mapping continu-
ous acoustic signals onto discrete sound categories, a process
known as sound category learning. This raises a fundamental
question: how do infants identify sound categories that are con-
trastive in their native language?
1.1. Distributional Learning (DL)
The Distributional Learning (DL) account proposes that in-
fants act as “statisticians,” tracking speech sound distributions
to infer contrastive categories [3].
If a probability density
function along an acoustic dimension (e.g., duration) exhibits
distinct peaks—indicating a bimodal or multimodal distribu-
tion—infants could identify this acoustic dimension as linguis-
tically important, and interpret these peaks as separate sound
categories. This mechanism has been supported by controlled
experiments, where exposing infants to speech with bimodal
distributions leads to formation of two categories [3, 4, 5, 6].
These studies demonstrate that infants are sensitive to distribu-
tional patterns when clear multimodality is present in the in-
put. However, multimodality is rarely observed in naturalistic
speech—the input infants receive in daily life—which is highly
variable with significant acoustic overlap between sound cate-

gories [7]. This lack of clear-cut multimodal distributions chal-
lenges the reliability of DL as a universal learning cue.
The Distributional Learning Across Contexts (DLAC) pro-
posal [8], as an extension to the DL model, emphasizes contex-
tual variation in addition to static distributional peaks. Figure 1
schematically demonstrates the concept of contextual variation:
even when the overall frequency distribution is unimodal, dif-
ferent contexts (e.g. prosodic position, neighboring sounds, etc)
reveal distinct distributional shapes. Hitczenko and Feldman [8]
suggests that infants identify contrastive acoustic dimensions
by tracking how distributional shapes vary across different con-
texts. By comparing vowel length in naturalistic speech cor-
pora from Japanese (a language with a vowel length contrast)
and French (a language without one), the authors found that
contrastive dimensions exhibit greater contextual variation. In
other words, when an acoustic dimension is contrastive, its dis-
tributional shape changes more drastically across contexts.
Most research on distributional learning has focused on
simple two-way sound contrasts in vowels and consonants. For
example, studies have examined English /t/ vs. /d/ [3], En-
glish /l/vs. /r/ [9], Dutch /A/ vs. /a/ [10], and Japanese long
vs. short vowels [8]. However, two key gaps remain. First, lit-
tle is known about how infants acquire multiple contrasts that
are cued by a single acoustic feature. Within this dimension,
contrasts may also vary in learnability, with some contrasts be-
ing relatively easy or hard to acquire. There is limited under-
standing of what contributes to this difference in learnability.
This gap limits our ability to test a key prediction of the DLAC
framework: within the same acoustic dimension, contrasts with
greater contextual variation should be easier to learn. Second,
despite the prevalence of tone languages, especially in Asia
and Africa, the role of DL in lexical tone acquisition remains
largely unexplored. Cantonese, with its complex tone system
and well-documented learning asymmetries, provides an ideal
test case. The following section explores how these asymme-
tries make Cantonese tones particularly suitable for investigat-
ing the DLAC.

1.2. Cantonese tones: a learning puzzle
Cantonese is a variety of Chinese primarily spoken in Guang-
dong, southern Guangxi, Hong Kong, Macau, and beyond. It
is characterized by a complex lexical tone system with six dis-
tinct tones: high-level (T1), high-rising (T2), mid-level (T3),
low-falling (T4), low-rising (T5), and low-level (T6). Each tone
distinguishes word meaning; for example, si can mean “poetry”
(si1), “history” (si2), “to try” (si3), “time” (si4), “market” (si5),
or “be” (si6). Learning Cantonese tones can be challenging be-
cause children must distinguish between six tones that differ
primarily in a single acoustic cue—pitch (measured with funda-
mental frequency, F0). In controlled experiments, tones could
be differentiated by their pitch height and pitch contour [13].
However, naturalistic speech often blurs these distinctions. As
illustrated in Figure 2, which shows a speaker’s tones in natu-
ralistic speech, most tones are highly overlapping.
Despite these challenges, children eventually master Can-
tonese tones, though some tone pairs are harder to learn than
others [12, 14, 15]. Fig. 2E illustrates children’s accuracy in
distinguishing three different tone pairs at various ages [12].
The T1 vs. T4 contrast is learned more quickly and accurately
than other pairs, whereas the T2 vs. T5 contrast is acquired the
latest. The T2 vs. T5 distinction is particularly noteworthy as
it is undergoing a sound change and merging into one category,
similar to T3 vs. T6 and T4 vs. T6 [16, 17]. These develop-
mental patterns, along with acoustic salience and sound change
status, allow tone pairs to be categorized into three groups:
• Easy pairs – Pairs involving T1 (e.g., T1T4).
These are
acoustically distinct and learned early. T1 is the most acous-
tically salient, making it the easiest to distinguish from other
tones, a pattern observed in both perception experiments and
machine classification studies [13].
• Merger pairs – Pairs undergoing merger (e.g., T2T5, T3T6,
T4T6). These exhibit extensive acoustic overlap and are not
fully learned until adulthood.
• Overlapping pairs – All other pairs (e.g., T3T5). These ex-
hibit acoustic overlap but remain learnable with experience.
Crucially, while both Overlapping and Merger pairs exhibit
acoustic overlap, Overlapping pairs are more learnable. This
raises a key question: Why are some acoustically overlapped
tone pairs easier to learn than others?
This study takes an initial step toward addressing this puz-
zle by testing whether the DLAC framework could explain
these learning asymmetries. Specifically, this study investigates
whether the ease of acquiring different tonal contrasts is linked
to their distributional variation across contexts. By analyzing
three selected tone pairs and comparing our findings with ex-
isting age-of-acquisition and perception data, we show that the
DLAC framework could predict which contrasts should be eas-
ier or harder to learn. These findings suggest that, in the absence
of invariant acoustic cues, infants may rely on contextual varia-
tion to acquire complex sound categories.
Given that Cantonese tone discrimination involves 15 bi-
nary contrasts, we selected three representative tone pairs from
each group for detailed analysis: T1T4 as an Easy pair, T3T5 as
an Overlapping pair, and T2T5 as a Merger pair. The T1T4 pair
was chosen because it consists of the highest and lowest tones
in Cantonese, making it the most distinct and easily separable
contrast. The T2T5 pair was selected because it represents the
most studied and well-documented tone merger in Cantonese
[16]. The T3T5 pair was chosen due to its acoustic overlap and
its shared T5 tone with the T2T5 pair, making it an ideal case
for comparison with the T2T5 merger.
2. Method
2.1. Data
We analyzed the Multi-ethnic Hong Kong Cantonese Corpus
(MeHKCC) [11], a corpus of annotated child-directed speech
(CDS) and adult-directed speech (ADS) produced by mothers in
Hong Kong with diverse linguistic backgrounds. The data were
originally collected between 2019 and 2021 and include record-
ings from various speech tasks, such as film retelling, one-on-
one interviews, map descriptions, story retelling, word identi-
fication, and in-home recordings. For this study, we selected
only native Cantonese-speaking mothers, resulting in a dataset
of naturalistic speech from 25 female speakers. We extracted
monosyllabic and disyllabic words with unambiguous tonal an-
notations while excluding function words. Although the corpus
includes both CDS and ADS, we do not distinguish between
them in the current analysis; however, future work may explore
potential differences between these speech registers. We ex-
tracted the following information about each syllable produced
by the mothers.
2.1.1. Acoustic cues
We extracted nine F0 descriptors per syllable to capture both
pitch height and the temporal dynamics of the F0 contour: mean
F0, median F0, variance of the F0 contour, F0 range (maxi-
mum–minimum), onset F0, 25% F0, 75% F0, offset F0, and F0
contour duration. To minimize pitch perturbations at syllable

2.1.2. Contexts
Each syllable was categorized based on four contextual fac-
tors known to influence the realization of Cantonese tones
[18, 17, 19]: (1) the class of onset sounds, which includes 8 cat-
egories (e.g., unaspirated stops, fricatives, nasals); (2) the class
of coda sounds, which is divided into 3 categories (empty coda,
nasals, stops); (3) the syllable’s position within a word (mono-
syllabic words, first syllable of disyllabic words, second sylla-
ble of disyllabic words); and (4) its prosodic position (utterance-
initial, utterance-medial, or utterance-final). These factors were
also used in [8].
2.2. Distributional analysis
Unlike previous studies that rely on a single acoustic measure
(e.g. duration, VOT), our approach captures the dynamic use of
F0 with nine descriptors. Given the high dimensionality, we em-
ployed t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)
via the sklearn package in Python to reduce the data to a two-
dimensional space for visualization and analysis [20]. t-SNE is
an unsupervised nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique
that embeds high-dimensional data in a low-dimensional space
in such a way that similar objects are modeled by nearby points
and dissimilar objects are modeled by distant points in the
space. In the visualization of the t-SNE results, we overlaid
ellipses representing one and two standard deviations around
the mean of each tonal distribution to highlight the spread and
variation of the data. Additionally, we included marginal den-
sity plots along the x- and y-axes to illustrate the distributional
shapes in each dimension.
Following [8], we also measured the differences between
tonal distributions using Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) [21],
also known as the Wasserstein distance, using the scipy pack-
age in Python. EMD quantifies the effort required to transform
one distribution into another by computing the optimal transport
cost between them. This metric is particularly well-suited for
comparing probability distributions that may differ in both loca-
tion and shape. We computed EMD separately for the marginal
densities along the x and y axes of the t-SNE results, summing
them to obtain the final EMD measure: EMDtotal = EMDx
+ EMDy. Higher EMD values indicate greater distributional
separation. This process is repeated for each specific context.
We report the mean, median, maximum, and variance of EMD
across contexts for each tone pair. Higher mean EMD values
indicate generally greater distributional separation between two
tones, while higher variance reflects greater contextual varia-
tion.
We predict that the separability and degree of contextual
variation in distributional shapes will align with developmental
acquisition patterns. Specifically, Easy contrasts (e.g., T1T4)
will exhibit the highest overall separation and the greatest vari-
ation across contexts, as reflected in the highest EMD mean and
variance. Overlapping contrasts (e.g., T3T5) will show moder-
ate separation and variation. Merger contrasts (e.g., T2T5) will
have the least distributional separation and the lowest variation,
reflecting in the lowest EMD mean and variance.
3. Results
Figure 3 visualizes the frequency distribution of the Overlap-
ping tone pair, T3T5, after dimensionality reduction. Panel A
reveals an overall unimodal distribution for the pair, despite
the fact that T3 and T5 are contrastive tones. This suggests
that, when aggregated across contexts, the tonal distinction may
not always be easily perceptible. However, Panel B presents a
more nuanced view by illustrating the distributional shapes of
the T3T5 pair in two specific contexts. In Context 2—charac-
terized by nasal onsets without codas, second-syllable position
within a word, and utterance-medial position—the tonal distri-
butions exhibit greater separability, as reflected in a higher Earth
Mover’s Distance (EMD = 78.3). In contrast, in Context 1—de-
fined by the absence of onsets and codas, with the same syllable
and prosodic position—the distributions show much lower sep-
arability (EMD = 12.2). This comparison suggests that certain
contexts, such as nasal onsets, enhance the distinctiveness of
tone contrasts.

sound category learning, we examined whether the degree of
contextual variation in the distributional shapes aligns with de-

These results support the hypothesis that greater contextual
distributional variation facilitates tone learning, as predicted by
the DLAC framework. The Easy tone pair (T1T4) exhibits the
most distinct separation, suggesting it should be easier for in-
fants to learn. The Overlapping pair (T3T5) shows less sepa-
rability but relatively high contextual variation, implying that
while this contrast may be more challenging, it remains learn-
able with experience. Finally, the Merger pair (T2T5) exhibits
the least separation and least contextual variation, suggesting
the greatest difficulty. These patterns align with the develop-
mental data presented in Figure 2E.
4. Discussion
This study investigated the DLAC with the specific question:
whether the ease of learning sound contrasts is influenced by
the degree of contextual variation in the input.
Using three
Cantonese tone pairs in naturalistic speech as a test case, our
findings revealed that tone pairs that are acquired more readily
exhibit greater distributional separation and contextual variation
than those that are more challenging to learn. These current re-
sults provide empirical support for the generalizability of the
DLAC framework to both complex sound contrasts and tonal
systems, underscoring the framework’s potential as a mecha-
nism for learning in the absence of invariance in speech signals.
An underlying assumption of this work is that infants learn
tones in a pairwise manner. Our focus on pairwise compar-
isons was driven by two reasons. First, we want to maintain
consistency with prior DL research which primarily examines
two-way contrasts.
Second, our study aligns its predictions
with developmental data, which rely on pairwise discrimination
paradigms. However, our choice does not imply that infants
necessarily learn tones exclusively through pairwise contrasts.
It remains possible that learners acquire all six tones simultane-
ously or that they first establish a reference tone (e.g., T1) before
differentiating others. Future research could employ computa-
tional modeling to simulate different learning mechanisms and
compare their predictions to empirical data.
This study extends prior research by examining a differ-
ent stage of sound category learning than was studied in prior
work. [8] applied the DLAC framework to explain the initial
step—identifying the relevant acoustic dimension that differen-
tiates contrastive sounds (e.g. duration, F0). We focus on the
next step: how learners identify contrastive categories along an
already-identified relevant acoustic dimension. In other words,
we assume that infants have already recognized F0 as the key
acoustic cue but have yet to learn the tone categories. By in-
vestigating this later stage, our study strengthens the general-
izability of the DLAC proposal, demonstrating that it serves as
a comprehensive learning account that explains both stages of
sound category acquisition. Future research could focus on the
earlier stage of acquisition, and study how Cantonese-learning
children identify F0 as a relevant acoustic cue. One way to do
this would be to compare the distribution of F0 against dimen-
sions that are non-contrastive in Cantonese, such as duration.
Alternatively, a cross-linguistic comparison between Cantonese
and a non-tonal language like English—where F0 does not en-
code lexical contrasts—could also provide further insights.
In summary, our results suggest that rather than hindering
sound category learning, contextual variation in distributional
shapes may serve as a cue that helps infants distinguish mean-
ingful contrasts, even in the absence of clearly separable multi-
modal distributions assumed in traditional DL models. A nat-
ural next step for this research is to analyze the full set of tone
pairs and compare their distributional properties with develop-
mental data. There is also room for methodological exploration.
For instance, alternative dimensionality reduction techniques
such as principal component analysis, or different distributional
distance metrics like Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, could
be employed. Future work could also explore different F0 de-
scriptors as well as additional contexts (e.g. including lexical
information). Additionally, future experimental work could also
provide direct evidence that complements the current study. For
example, participants could be exposed to varying levels of con-
textual distributional variation, and assessed on how this varia-
tion impacts their ability to learn sound categories.
