Abstract 
Traditional ASR systems normalize transcriptions into spoken 
form for training, leading to spoken form outputs. In contrast, 
modern Transformer-based models directly map speech-to-text, 
preserving the written form. However, existing speech 
databases mainly contain spoken form, causing inconsistencies 
in recognition results. To address this, Inverse Text 
Normalization (ITN) is required, and dual transcription data is 
crucial for effective training. However, constructing such 
datasets is costly and time-consuming. This study proposes a 
data augmentation method leveraging LLMs to automatically 
generate dual transcription data with minimal effort. It employs 
iterative learning to expand the dataset through supervised and 
semi-supervised methods. Additionally, the bidirectional text 
conversion (BTC) model supports both ITN and TN within a 
unified framework. Experimental results demonstrate that the 
proposed method achieved an ERR of 13.4% and 4.7%, 
outperforming prior approaches. 
Index Terms: Inverse Text Normalization, Spoken-to-Written, 
Written-to-Spoken, Bidirectional Text Conversion 
1. Introduction 
In the era of artificial intelligence, speech recognition 
technology has advanced beyond simple speech-to-text 
conversion, now requiring context-aware accuracy and well-
formatted output. Depending on context, the phrase ‘Twenty 
twenty-five’ may refer to a year (2025), a time (20:25), or a 
score (20–25), demonstrating how its written form varies 
depending on meaning. Similarly, long numerical expressions 
are typically represented in digit form (e.g., 2,345,600) rather 
than full-text transcriptions, enhancing readability and 
comprehension. 
Many 
other 
numerical 
and 
symbolic 
expressions require precise formatting to ensure clarity.  
Conventional speech recognition systems traditionally are 
based on spoken-form transcriptions for training, leading to 
outputs that reflect pronunciation rather than written form. This 
leads to inconsistencies when written form output is required, 
making Inverse Text Normalization (ITN) [1, 2, 3, 4] has 
become essential for converting spoken form transcriptions 
back into written form. Recently, Transformer-based end-to-
end speech recognition models [5, 6, 7] have gained 
prominence for directly converting speech signals into text. 
These models have demonstrated strong capabilities in learning 
mappings between speech signals and written form, enabling 
more effective handling of numbers, symbols, and foreign 
words. However, they rely on large-scale databases for training,

making manual transcription efforts infeasible. As an 
alternative, constructing large-scale speech databases from 
broadcast data reduces manual effort but often introduces 
transcription errors and inconsistencies, degrading recognition 
performance [8]. As a result, speech databases can contain a 
mix of spoken form and written form transcriptions, leading to 
further inconsistencies in recognition outputs. 
To resolve this issue, Transformer-based ITN techniques 
have been proposed [9]. Inspired by the success of Transformer 
models in machine translation, this approach frames spoken-to-
written form conversion as a translation task. Just as parallel 
data pairs are essential for training effective machine translation 
models, dual transcription data—where both spoken form and 
written form transcriptions are recorded simultaneously—is 
critical for ITN model training. Building on this approach, an 
LLM-based ITN technique has been proposed [10]. Fine-tuning 
an LLM on dual transcription data has shown improved 
contextual awareness, achieving superior ITN performance 
compared to conventional Transformer-based approaches. 
Despite these advancements, Korean dual transcription data 
remains scarce, posing a major challenge to ITN development 
[11, 12]. Constructing such datasets is costly and time-
consuming due to its heavy reliance on manual work, making 
large-scale data construction impractical.  
To address this issue, we introduce a data augmentation 
approach leveraging LLMs to automatically generate dual 
transcription data, as shown in Figure 1. The proposed method 
follows an iterative data augmentation strategy, progressively 
expanding the dataset through supervised and semi-supervised 
learning. As part of this approach, we propose a Written-to-
Spoken (WTS) conversion method, which applies LLM-based 
Text Normalization (TN) to transform written form into spoken 
form. This method automates the construction of the dataset, 
significantly reducing manual effort. Additionally, an error

2. Baseline Model for ITN 
2.1. Korean LLM Trained from Scratch (GPT-2-Kor) 
The baseline model for ITN is built upon GPT-2-Kor, a 
lightweight Korean LLM trained from scratch using a Korean 
corpus. GPT-2-Kor follows the GPT-2 [13] small architecture 
and is optimized for computational efficiency. 
2.2. Korean ITN Model (Spoken-to-Written, STW) 
GPT-2-Kor was fine-tuned as an STW model for Korean ITN.  
To train the model, dual transcription data was reformatted to 
capture transformation patterns between spoken form and 
written form. The structure of the dual transcription data and 
the training set used in the STW model are presented in Figure 
2. Figure 2(a) presents the dual transcription format, aligning 
spoken form and written form transcriptions in parallel. When 
spoken and written forms differ within a sentence, the 
transcription follows the parallel format shown in Figure 2(b). 
Figure 2(c) demonstrates the structured input format used for 
model training:  
 
“<SPOKEN> [BOS] spoken form sentences [EOS] 
<WRITTEN> [BOS] written form sentences [EOS]” 
 
Here, ‘<SPOKEN>’ and ‘<WRITTEN>’ are special tokens 
indicating the transcription type, while [BOS] (Begin of 
Sentence) and [EOS] (End of Sentence) mark sentence 
boundaries.  
3. Iterative Data Augmentation Based on 
Semi-Supervised Learning 
A major challenge in improving STW model performance is the 
difficulty of obtaining large-scale training data, as dual 
transcription datasets are primarily constructed manually. To 
address this issue, this study proposes a novel approach, 
introducing a new LLM-based Text Normalization (TN) 
technique for Written-to-Spoken (WTS)—the reverse process 
of STW.  
WTS generates spoken form outputs from written form 
inputs, enabling automated construction of dual transcription 
datasets and significantly reducing manual effort. The overall 
framework for data generation and iterative training using both 
STW and WTS models is illustrated in Figure 1. 
3.1. Supervised Data-Based WTS Model Training (Stage-I) 
The proposed method follows an iterative data augmentation 
strategy, progressively expanding dual transcription data 
through supervised and semi-supervised learning. In Stage-I, 
supervised data is used to fine-tune GPT-2-Kor into the WTS 
model. The model is trained by modifying the token structure 
of the STW training dataset as follows: 
 
“<WRITTEN> [BOS] written form sentences [EOS] 
<SPOKEN> [BOS] spoken form sentences [EOS]” 
 
By learning this mapping, the model generates spoken form 
outputs from written form inputs, forming the basis for semi-
supervised learning in the next stage. 
3.2. Semi-Supervised Data Generation Using the WTS 
Model (Stage-II) 
In Stage-II, the WTS model is used to automatically generate 
spoken form outputs, expanding the dataset beyond the 
manually constructed samples. To ensure data quality, a 
conversion error detection and correction process is applied. 
The primary sources of errors stem from numbers, symbols, and 
foreign words. Since manually refining all error patterns is 
inefficient, the correction process focuses on symbol 
transformations. A list of frequently occurring erroneous 
characters was compiled, followed by automatic validation and 
correction. The verified data was incorporated into the existing 
supervised dataset, continuously improving the model through 
iterative learning.

dual transcription datasets with minimal human intervention, 
significantly enhancing data availability and quality.

3.3. STW Model Update Using Augmented Dataset (Stage-
III) 
With the augmented dataset incorporating both supervised and 
semi-supervised data, the STW model undergoes iterative fine-
tuning in Stage-III. The augmented dataset, now including 
verified spoken form outputs, is used to retrain the STW model. 
Through this iterative learning process, the STW model benefits 
from an enhanced diversity of training examples, leading to 
improved accuracy and adaptability. The final STW model is 
expected to handle a wider range of linguistic variations and 
domain-specific ITN more effectively. 
3.4. Bidirectional Text Conversion (BTC) 
In certain domains, such as air traffic control and military 
communication, spoken form is preferred over numerical 
values to prevent misinterpretation. Since different domains 
may require distinct transcription styles, speech recognition 
outputs do not always need to be in written form. To address 
this, we designed the Bidirectional Text Conversion (BTC) 
model, which integrates both STW and WTS conversions into 
a single model, allowing for flexible text transformations based 
on domain-specific requirements. 
The BTC model is fine-tuned using GPT-2-Kor and the 
augmented dataset. It utilizes the data generated in Stage-III, 
incorporating both STW and WTS training formats 
simultaneously, which provides a simple yet effective approach 
for bidirectional text conversion. Figure 3 illustrates the 
inference process of the BTC model, which performs 
bidirectional text conversion by taking spoken form (S) as input 
and generating written form (W) as output, or vice versa, 
depending on the inference direction. Prompts can also 
selectively apply ITN or TN based on the intended application. 
 
4. Experiments and Results 
The Korean LLM model was trained on corpora provided by 
AIHub [14]. The dataset consists of commercially available 
book-based Korean corpora, specialized medical and legal texts, 
National Assembly transcripts, and newspaper articles collected 
from Modu Corpus [15], as well as sentences from Korean 
Wikipedia and Namuwiki, amounting to 7.6 billion (B) tokens 
for training. For fine-tuning, lecture content data from AIHub 
[16] and news article data from ETRI [17] were used, resulting 
in 4.5 million (M) fine-tuned sentences. Performance 
evaluation focused on text that captures spoken-to-written form 
variations, particularly in numbers, symbols, and foreign words. 
These categories are collectively referred to as target words, as 
they are key to assessing the model’s ability to convert spoken 
form into written form. The evaluation dataset consists of Set A 
and Set B: 
• 
Set A: Contains 3,000 news texts from 2002–2003, 
created during the initial dual transcription data 
construction. 
• 
Set B: Contains 3,000 news texts from 2022. 
The evaluation was conducted by measuring Inverse 
Character Error Rate (I-CER) and Non-Inverse Character Error 
Rate (NI-CER). I-CER assesses the model’s accuracy in 
converting target characters, while NI-CER evaluates its ability 
to preserve non-target characters in their original form. The 
analysis of target word proportions (numerals and alphabetic 
characters) in the evaluation datasets is presented in Table 2. 
Set B contains a higher proportion of target words compared to 
Set A, but the ratio of numerals and alphabetic characters 
remains similar across both datasets. 
Table 3 presents the performance comparison of WTS 
models fine-tuned on two different Korean LLMs using 4.5M 
supervised learning sentences. One is GPT-2-Kor (G2K, 112M 
parameters) [18], and the other is Llama-3-Open-Kor-8B (L3K, 
8B parameters). The Llama-3-Open-Kor-8B model is a 
multilingual variant of Llama-3 [19, 20], further trained on 
Korean data. 
Experimental results indicate that model size alone does not 
determine WTS performance, as the smaller GPT-2-Kor 
outperformed the much larger Llama-3-Open-Ko-8B. The WTS 
model generated 7.5M spoken form sentences, bringing the 
total training data to 12M when combined with 4.5M 
supervised 
sentences.

Table 5 compares the proposed BTC model against 
independent STW and WTS models. The independent models 
were fine-tuned on 12M sentences, while the BTC model 
leveraged both directions of the dataset, effectively doubling 
the training data. However, as the data comes from existing dual 
transcription datasets, no additional construction cost is 
incurred. Additionally, the BTC model integrates both ITN and 
TN within a single framework. For the STW task, the BTC 
model achieved an ERR of 13.4% for I-CER and 25.0% for NI-
CER. These values are derived from the total score, where BTC 
improved the independent STW model’s I-CER from 4.41% to 
3.82% and NI-CER from 0.20% to 0.15%. For the WTS task, 
the BTC model achieved an ERR of 4.7% in I-CER and 50.0% 
in NI-CER, reducing the independent WTS model’s I-CER 
from 4.19% to 3.99% and NI-CER from 0.22% to 0.11%. These 
results demonstrate that the BTC model consistently 
outperforms independent models in both ITN and TN tasks. 
Furthermore, by leveraging existing data, the BTC model 
achieves these improvements without additional data collection 
costs. 
Table 6 presents a qualitative comparison of outputs 
generated by the baseline model and the proposed STW model. 
In the first example, the spoken form ‘이와삼분의이이닝’ 
corresponds to ‘three over two and two-thirds innings.’ The 
baseline model rendered the fraction in a mixed format 
combining numbers and words (2 and 2/3), whereas the 
proposed STW model produced a more concise and 
mathematically precise representation (2⅔), improving 
readability and clarity. In the second example, ‘세제곱미터’ 
(cubic meter) and ‘마이크로그램’ (microgram) were converted 
differently. The baseline model generated ‘m3’ and ‘ug’, using 
a combination of numerals and alphabetic characters instead of 
their standard scientific symbols. While ‘㎥’ is normalized to 
‘m3’ during evaluation, resulting in no quantitative difference, 
‘m3’ is not the optimal notation. In contrast, the proposed 
model successfully rendered these units in their standard 
scientific notation ( ㎥ and ㎍), demonstrating improved 
handling of numerical and scientific symbols. These results 
highlight the proposed STW model’s enhanced ability to 
process numerical values and scientific symbols, enabling more 
precise and structured conversions. 
5. Conclusions 
This 
study 
investigated 
ITN 
techniques 
to 
address 
inconsistencies between spoken form and written form 
transcription in speech recognition outputs. To tackle this issue, 
we proposed an iterative data augmentation method based on 
semi-supervised learning, leveraging LLMs. The proposed 
approach automatically generates dual transcription data, 
reducing costs while meeting ITN requirements across various 
domains. Additionally, we implemented a bidirectional 
transformation model, enabling a single model to perform both 
ITN and TN. Experimental results showed that the proposed 
method achieved an ERR of 13.4% and 4.7% compared to 
independent models, demonstrating superior performance and 
efficiency over existing approaches.  
For future research, we plan to explore prompt-based 
techniques to develop domain-optimized transformation 
models, allowing the system to adapt to diverse domains and 
complex contexts.
