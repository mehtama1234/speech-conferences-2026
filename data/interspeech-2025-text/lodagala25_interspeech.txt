Abstract
Curating Text-to-Speech (TTS) datasets is a strenuous task
given the quality considerations. While it is hard to find high-
quality TTS datasets in languages other than English, it is rare to
come across code-switching (CS) datasets. As a part of this work,
we curate a 4-hour Arabic-English TTS corpus consisting of
code-switched Egyptian-English, monolingual Modern Standard
Arabic (MSA), Egyptian, and English, all recorded by the same
voice talent.
We demonstrate the importance of vowelization and the
need for better phonemization of Arabic text. To this effect, we
present the modified espeak-ng phonemizer that handles vari-
ous irregularities of espeak-ng over Arabic text. Upon training
baseline TTS systems over this benchmark, we demonstrate its
efficacy through extensive subjective evaluations.
Index Terms: Text-to-Speech Synthesis, Code-switching, Mul-
tilingual, Dialectal Speech, Phonemization
1. Introduction and Related Work
Great progress in machine learning, deep learning and speech
large language modeling approaches have lowered the barrier in
build high quality Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems. These achieve-
ments are largely attributed to the scale-up of training data and
the strong modeling capabilities of the models. For example,
VALL-E [1] scaled up its training data to 60, 000 hours of En-
glish speech [2] and NaturalSpeech [3] leveraged over 44, 000
hours of transcribed English speech data [4]. Recently, Wenet-
Speech4TTS [5] released over 12, 800 hours in a multi-domain
Mandarin corpus derived from the open-source WenetSpeech
dataset. Also, given the complexity in gathering code-switching
datasets, efforts have been made in addressing code-switching
TTS at a modeling level by either using bilingual data [6, 7] or
by using cross-lingual word embeddings [8] or voice conversion
abilities [9]. To this end, there is a noticeable lack of extensive
open-source datasets for Arabic TTS applications compared to
English or multilingual datasets [10].
Moreover, limited research and data are available for Arabic
TTS. Here, we review previous work: (i) ClArTTS [11], a 12-
hour single-speaker Classical Arabic corpus. Extracted from a
LibriVox audiobook, it was transcribed and vowelized manually.
Proposed models show ClArTTS improves TTS quality over
existing corpora, and it is publicly available for research; (ii)
ARTST [12], a pre-trained Arabic text and speech transformer,
fine-tuned for ASR, TTS, and Dialect Identification. ArTST
*These authors contributed equally.
outperforms multilingual models like Whisper and MMS and
learns TTS without explicit diacritization. The model and fine-
tuned systems are publicly available for research. Such pre-
trained models can perform multiple downstream speech-related
tasks with different input modalities; (iii) NatiQ [13], an end-
to-end Arabic TTS system leveraging Transformer and Parallel
WaveGAN [14], achieving MOS scores of 4.40 (neutral voice)
and 4.21 (expressive). With a real-time factor of 0.09 on GPU,
the models have been trained over male and female voice talents
for MSA only. Data and models are not public.
Earlier works have addressed MSA speech, using high re-
source and clean recordings. (iv) Finally, a recent study by
[10] proposed an unsupervised method for low-resource Arabic
TTS using broadcast news, combining automatic data selec-
tion (DNSMOS), human/automatic vowelization, and transfer
learning. This approach enables efficient TTS development for
under-resourced languages, validated via Arabic Aljazeera data.
Previous work lacks a standardized benchmark for Arabic
TTS and is not tailored to the unique characteristics of the Ara-
bic language. Key gaps include the absence of dialectal TTS,
which is essential for capturing Arabic’s linguistic diversity, and
the lack of code-switching TTS, crucial for handling mixed-
language speech common in everyday conversations.
The major contributions from this work are the following:
• Single-speaker voice recordings in Egyptian Arabic, MSA,
English, and Egyptian-English code-switching. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first work to address dialectal and
code-switching TTS in the Arabic language context.
• We propose modifications to the existing espeak-ng phonem-
izer that is widely used in training TTS systems. Certain rules
for Arabic have been modified to result in an accurate phone-
mization of the text. This in turn aids TTS system training, as
validated by our study.
• We demonstrate the significance of vowelization of Arabic
text while building TTS systems.
• Considering the dialects and code-switching involved, we
believe that this dataset would act as a benchmark for further
TTS training and evaluation in Arabic. Given that the speaker
is a native Egyptian speaker, the data from this study also
helps to analyze the characteristics of L2-speaking over other
dialects and code-switching data.
The SawtArabi benchmark corpus, the modified espeak-
ng phonemizer and the baseline checkpoints along with their
associated code-base are available for public access 1.

2. SawtArabi Benchmark
The word “Sawt" in Arabic stands for “Voice". Our efforts as a
part of this work are to create a benchmarking TTS dataset for
MSA, dialectal and code-switching Arabic. The next subsections
detail the curation and characteristics of this benchmark.
2.1. Text Sources and transcription guidelines
SawtArabi text has been collected from varied sources depending
on the dialect or language. MSA text is a subset of the NatiQ data
after contacting its authors [13]; we ensured that the text was
fully MSA, avoiding any dialectal or classical Arabic. English
data has been randomly selected from LJspeech [15]. However,
the speaker (being a native Egyptian) struggled multiple times
to read certain English sentences; therefore, he was allowed to
edit or replace difficult words (e.g., replacing difficult with hard).
Finally, Egyptian and code-switching texts have been generated
using GPT-4 LLM [16]. For code-switching, we ensured that the
Arabic text primarily followed the Egyptian dialect.
The text given to the professional speaker had no voweliza-
tion, and the speaker had the freedom to make any changes that
made recording easier, as long as they were minor edits. There-
fore, the text had to be reviewed to ensure the transcription was
fully correct and the pronounced vowelization has been applied,
even if it was linguistically incorrect. For Egyptian and code-
switching data, we had to address the fact that dialectal speech,
in many cases, does not follow the standard orthography. The
speaker might pronounce éKCK (vlAvp2) as éKCK (tlAtp). Our
approach was to write the text as it has been pronounced, not as
it should have been written, which is a reasonable assumption as
long as the TTS system is dealing with one dialect at a time and
not mixing dialects, for dialectal CS, such as EGY with MSA
[17], which is a common occurrence in formal speech.
2.2. Dataset Statistics
The entire SawtArabi dataset has been recorded by one profes-
sional speaker (male aged 34 years) who speaks the Egyptian
dialect natively. In order to capture the L2-speaking character-
istics and to build a dataset across dialects of Arabic, we have
collected voice recordings from the same speaker in Egyptian
(EGY), Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), English (ENG), and
Egyptian-English code-switching (CS).
All of the data has been recorded in a noise-free environ-
ment at 22KHz sampling rate and in the 16-bit Signed Integer
PCM audio encoding format. Each of the dialect(s)/language(s)
involved has equal representation in the dataset. For each di-
alect, one hour of speech has been extracted after discarding
segments with silences. For each dialect/language, the data has

While collecting voice-recordings from native speakers of

Most formal and informal conversations in urban settings
worldwide involve code-switching between the native language
and another language, which in often English. However, limited
efforts have been made to collect data sets for downstream speech
tasks to represent this trend. While providing Egyptian-English
code-switching data, SawtArabi also offers the advantage of
including mono-lingual Egyptian and English data, from the
same speaker. We believe this approach to dataset collection
can help training and understanding challenges with both mono-
lingual and multi-lingual TTS systems.
3. Experimental Design
3.1. Vowelizer
The Arabic writing system includes 36 consonants and 8 diacrit-
ics [18]. However, most text is unvowelized, causing ambiguities
that challenge TTS systems. Diacritization (aka vowelization) is
essential for producing intelligible, natural-sounding speech, as
missing vowels can lead to mispronunciations and degrade the
naturalness of TTS [18]. For example, the word ÕÎ« can be pro-
nounced as /Palam/ (flag), /Pilm/ (knowledge), or /Pallama/
(he taught), depending on the diacritics. To assess this effect, we
performed two experiments: one with vowelized transcription of
the dataset and the other without diacritics.
3.2. Phonemizer
Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) conversion is crucial for trans-
forming written text into phonetic representations. Although
vowelized Arabic text captures most phonetic information, cer-
tain phonetic features are still better represented in phonetic
transcription. For instance, assimilation affects pronunciation,
as in the case of the word I. 	K	X (*nb) , where the 	à (n) should be
pronounced as Ð (m) (e.g., /Damb/ instead of /Danb/).
We employ espeak-ng due to its widespread use and mul-
tilingual support, including Arabic. espeak-ng utilizes the In-
ternational Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) and follows a rule-based
approach combined with dictionary lookups, enabling easy cus-
tomization and improved handling of out-of-vocabulary words3.

Despite its effectiveness in Arabic G2P conversion, espeak-

racy. Enhancements include refining the Arabic rule-based file
and applying pre-processing and post-processing before phone-
mization. These modifications were implemented in espeak-ng
version 1.52. To evaluate the effectiveness of the modified phone-
mizer, new experiments were conducted, comparing its results
with the standard espeak-ng implementation.
3.2.1. Same Phonetic Symbol for two different Arabic letters
By default, espeak-ng assigns the same phonetic symbol /D/ to
both 	  (Z) and 	X (*). To address this, the ph_arabic file was
modified to assign /DP/ to 	  (Z). Although espeak-ng always
places a stress mark after the vowel following 	  (Z), which helps
differentiate it from 	X (*), but it does not apply any distinction
when no vowel follows.
3.2.2. Incorrect Pronunciation of Ta’ Marbuta with Sukun
The letter Ta’ Marbuta è (p) should be pronounced as /h/ when
carrying Sukun (no diacritics), but espeak-ng incorrectly retains
it as /t/. This issue was resolved by modifying the ar_rules

3.2.4. Standardizing Shaddah (Gemination) Representation
In IPA notation, Shaddah (gemination) can be represented in two
ways: using a colon after the consonant or doubling the conso-
nant (e.g., ss). As espeak-ng inconsistently alternates between
these representations, a Python post-processing script has been
implemented to standardize the geminate format by always using
the duplicated letter representation, e.g. s: →ss. Additionally,
in some cases, espeak-ng incorrectly produces three phonemes
instead of two for geminated consonants, either as ss: or sss.
3.3. Modeling
In order to demonstrate the effectiveness of the dataset,
we have trained baseline TTS systems on each of the
dialect(s)/language(s).
This involves training a Text-to-
Spectrogram model and a vocoder for each of the di-
alect(s)/language(s). The following subsections elaborate on
the model choices and the training setup.
3.3.1. TTS Training
Considering the non-autoregressive approach and the ability to
train from scratch without the need for any external alignments,
Matcha-TTS [19] has been chosen as the framework for training
the baseline Text-to-MelSpectrogram models.
In order to study the effect of the standard espeak-ng phone-
mizer and the modified espeak-ng phonemizer 3.2, we train a
different Matcha-TTS system on each of the phonemizers for the
EGY, MSA and CS sections of our dataset.
While monolingual sentences have been phonemized by the
standard espeak-ng or modified espeak-ng phonemizers directly
by specifying the language, we follow a slightly different ap-
proach for the CS sentences. CS sentences have been split at
phrase level with each phrase being completely monolingual.
These monolingual phrases have then been phonemized by the
respective phonemizers. The resulting phoneme sequence for
a code-switching sentence comes from the phonemization of
monolingual phrases that are embedded within.
Each TTS system has been trained on a single A-100 (80GB)
GPU with a batch size of 256 for 16, 399 epochs. Rest of the
configurations follow the standard recipe from Matcha-TTS.
Each of the resulting TTS systems have 18.2M parameters.
3.3.2. Vocoder Training
As HiFi-GAN [20] is among the most used vocoders and given
that Matcha-TTS also supports the same, we have trained our
vocoders on each of the dialect(s)/language(s). We have used
the configv1 as a part of our work. This resulted in training
4 vocoder models using the standard training recipe from HiFi-
GAN for 815000 updates.
4. Evaluation, Results and Discussion
4.1. Baseline evaluation
The baseline TTS systems have been evaluated on both objective
and subjective metrics. Mel-cepstral distortion (MCD) and log-
F0 root mean square error (F0 RMSE) have been used as the
objective metrics. MCD as a measure captures how close two
sequences of mel spectra are. The two sequences in this case are
the ground truth and the synthesized speech. Log-F0 RMSE on
the other hand, refers to the logarithm of the root-mean-square
error (RMSE) of the fundamental frequency (f0) contour of the
synthesized speech compared to the target f0 contour of the
reference speech.
Mean Opinion Score (MOS) has been used as the subjective
measure to evaluate the perceived quality of speech samples by
human listeners. This listening test has been designed to evaluate
the intelligibility, naturalness, and pronunciation of the speech
samples on a scale of 1 to 5. MOS score is then computed by
averaging the scores assigned by multiple human listeners with
a 95% confidence interval estimate. As a part of our evaluation,
for each MOS test, we have presented 25 samples each from the
Ground Truth and the speech synthesized by the corresponding
TTS system. This subjective evaluation has been performed by
25 listeners proficient in different dialects, with English being
their L2 language. These 25 sentences have been sourced from
our test sets. As a result, each evaluator rates 25 samples of
ground truth speech and 25 samples of the outputs from each of
the TTS systems. Considering the different systems presented in
Table 2, each evaluator has rated 275 audio samples in total.

tive) for the different TTS systems. While the objective metrics

Table 2 presents the results comparing the standard espeak-ng

standard phonemizer across all datasets and evaluation criteria,
suggesting the need for better G2P conversion in Arabic. To fur-
ther analyze the improvements, we examined the Arabic words
flagged by evaluators as mispronounced. The standard espeak-ng
evaluation has 157 mispronounced words (70 unique mispronun-
ciations), while the modified version has 97 mistakes(44 unique
cases). Both models shared 20 mispronounced words.

4.3. Impact of vowelization
Table 3 presents the results of the experiment comparing vow-
elized and non-vowelized text in MSA. The two systems were
trained using the standard espeak-ng phonemizer, and the same
vocoder was used for both systems since only the TTS system
training differs. As the table shows, the difference between the
two experiments is significant across all criteria, particularly
in pronunciation, highlighting the importance of vowelization
for producing accurate and natural speech. By examining the
phonemized text, we see that espeak-ng added vowels to certain
words based on its Arabic dictionary, which helped improve the
non-vowelized results. Listening to the generated audio revealed
that missing vowels cause consonant clustering, making some
phonemes hard to distinguish and reducing intelligibility. In
MSA, omitted case-ending vowels further contribute to robotic
and unnatural speech.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we presented a unique dataset consisting of single-
speaker voice recordings in Egyptian Arabic, Modern Standard
Arabic, English, and Egyptian-English code-switching. This is,
to the best of our knowledge, the first study addressing dialectal
and code-switching TTS in the Arabic context. Additionally, the
dataset provides insights into the L2-speaking characteristics of
a native Egyptian speaker. We also introduced modifications to
the widely used espeak-ng phonemizer, enhancing the accuracy
of Arabic phonemization, which improved the accuracy of TTS
models. Our results also highlight the importance of voweliza-
tion. Furthermore, the dataset establishes a crucial benchmark
for future TTS systems in Arabic.
As a part of the future work, we aim to expand the dataset
by including more Arabic dialects and exploring the differences
between dialectal and MSA writing conventions.
