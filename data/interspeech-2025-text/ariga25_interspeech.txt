Coping with segmental–prosodic incongruity in spoken word recognition 
in Japanese 
Terumichi Ariga 
The University of Tokyo, Japan 
ariga.terumichi@gmail.com 
 
 
Abstract 
A spoken word consists of two phonological cues: segments 
(phonemes) and prosody (e.g., lexical pitch accent). While both 
segments and prosody contribute to spoken word recognition, it 
is unknown how listeners interpret a spoken word when these 
cues contradict each other. For example, a word *tansu HLL is 
logically interpreted as both a segmental mispronunciation of 
dansu HLL “dance” and a prosodic mispronunciation of tansu 
LHH “wardrobe.” The present study investigated how Japanese 
listeners interpret those segmentally and prosodically 
incongruent words in spoken word recognition using a cross-
modal repetition priming paradigm. When ISI = 0 ms, listeners’ 
lexical decision was delayed more for prosodic 
mispronunciation (prime: *tansu HLL; target: tansu LHH 
“wardrobe”) than for segmental mispronunciation (prime: 
*dansu LHH; target: tansu LHH) In contrast, when ISI = 750 
ms, listeners’ lexical decision was delayed more for segmental 
mispronunciation than for prosodic mispronunciation. These 
results demonstrate that lexical access to segmentally and 
prosodically incongruent words is a dynamic process, in which 
listeners first try a prosody-oriented interpretation and then shift 
to a segment-oriented interpretation. 
Index Terms: spoken word recognition, lexical prosody, pitch 
accent, Japanese 
1. Introduction 
Spoken words consist of multiple phonological cues. For 
example, a Japanese word tansu “wardrobe” is produced with a 
serial combination of five phonemes, namely, /t/, /a/, /n/, /s/, 
and /u/. Simultaneously, it is produced with an unaccented pitch 
accent pattern (LHH; L or H indicates a high or low tonal 
pattern for each mora). In contrast, the word dansu “dance” is 
produced with a segmental combination of /d/, /a/, /n/, /s/, and 
/u/ and simultaneously with an accented pitch accent pattern 
(HLL). Both segments and lexical prosody are critical for 
lexical distinction. How do listeners interpret words when these 
two phonological cues contradict one another? In other words, 
when they listen to a mispronounced word *tansu HLL, do they 
interpret it as tansu LHH “wardrobe” or dansu HLL “dance”? 
The role of prosodic information, especially lexical prosody, 
in spoken word recognition has been debated in the literature of 
psycholinguistics [1–3]. Empirical studies have shown that 
lexical stress plays a minor role for lexical access in English [4–
6]. In contrast, studies in Dutch [7, 8], Spanish [9], Chinese [10, 
11], and Japanese [12–15], have demonstrated that lexical 
prosodic cues are important for lexical access in the spoken 
word recognition mechanisms of these languages. 
A crucial role of lexical prosody in spoken word 
recognition is discriminating words that are distinguished solely 
by lexical prosodic information (i.e., minimal pairs such as 
jidoo HLL “children” and jidoo LHH “automatic” [12, 15]) 
during lexical access. In addition to the independent use of 
lexical prosody, it also influences segmental identification. 
Connine and others [16] demonstrated that when a /t –d/ 
ambiguous sound was presented in the context of _igress, the 
perceptual categorization of the ambiguous segment was biased 
toward being voiced (/d/) if the stress pattern was weak–strong. 
In contrast, categorization was biased toward being voiceless 
(/t/) if the stress pattern was strong–weak. This biasing effect 
occurred because both diGRESS and TIgress (capital letters 
indicate stressed syllables) are real words whereas *tiGRESS 
and *DIgress are nonwords. 
Similar effects are observed in Japanese, where pairs 
contrasted by both segments and lexical pitch accent, such as 
dansu HLL “dance” and tansu LHH “wardrobe,” are used [17]. 
These effects suggest that when segmental input is acoustically 
ambiguous, lexical prosodic information aids in segmental 
perception so that the entire word would become a real word. 
A question then remains as to whether the effect of lexical 
prosody on segmental perception occurs only when segmental 
perception itself suffers from the acoustic ambiguity of the 
sound. Given the redundancy of acoustic cues in speech 
recognition, it is reasonable to assume that lexical prosody 
compensates for segmental processing. However, because 
segments and lexical prosody are independent of each other in 
production, we can systematically produce equivocal 
mispronunciations such as *tansu HLL. It remains unclear 
whether listeners rely on segmental information or lexical 
prosodic information to interpret these ambiguous words. 
With respect to spoken word recognition based on 
mispronounced word input, Lee [11], using a cross -modal 
priming paradigm, suggested that mispronunciation of both 
segments and tones prevents lexical access in Mandarin 
Chinese. However, segmental mispronunciation with tonal 
overlap (e.g., prime: cang2 “hide”; target: lou2 “hall”) induced 
a greater increase in response time than tonal mispronunciation 
with segmental overlap (e.g., prime: lou3 “hug”; target: lou2 
“hall”). This suggests qualitative differences between the 
mispronunciation of segments and that of prosody in the online 
processing of spoken word recognition. 
However, since the importance of lexical prosody in spoken 
word recognition differs across languages [18–20], how spoken 
word recognition is influenced by segments and prosody, as 
well as by their mispronunciation, may be different in Japanese. 
In addition, Lee’s study [11] tested mispronunciation that is 
identifiable as segmental or prosodic. With respect to the 
equivocal mispronunciation mentioned above, how listeners 
interpret words based on incongruous segmental and prosodic

To address these questions, we investigated the process of 
lexical access when listening to segmentally and prosodically 
incongruent words, such as *tansu HLL. We used a repetition 
priming paradigm, which is an established method for assessing 
the process of lexical access [7, 9, 11, 13, 15]. This paradigm 
enables us to evaluate the dynamic process of lexical access by 
adjusting prime–target timing (i.e., inter-stimulus interval: ISI) 
[5, 21, 22]. We conducted two experiments using different ISIs. 
2. Experiment 1 
2.1. Methods 
2.1.1. Participants 
Thirty-two native speakers of Tokyo Japanese (18–53 years 
old; mean = 25.12; SD = 6.74) participated in the experiment. 
They self-identified as being from the Kanto area in Japan. 
They received monetary compensation for their participation. 
2.1.2. Materials 
Twelve minimal pairs that contrasted both the voicing of the 
initial consonant (voiced or voiceless) and the lexical pitch 
accent pattern (accented or unaccented), e.g., dansu HLL 
“dance” and tansu LHH “wardrobe,” were prepared. These 
pairs are the same as those used in a previous study [23]. All of 
these words were highly familiar, and the familiarity did not 
differ across pairs [24]. 
The item words were chosen so that the voicing of the initial 
consonant was lexically correlated with the pitch accent pattern. 
Thus, alternating either the voicing of the initial consonant or 
the lexical pitch accent results in ambiguous mispronunciation. 
For example, *tansu HLL is regarded as both a segmental 
mispronunciation of dansu HLL “dance” and a prosodic 
mispronunciation of tansu LHH “wardrobe.” Similarly, *dansu 
LHH is regarded as both a segmental mispronunciation of tansu 
LHH and a prosodic mispronunciation of dansu HLL. 
To assess the process of lexical access when listeners 
listened to ambiguous mispronounced words, we set four 
conditions within the cross-modal repetition priming paradigm, 
as shown in Table 1. The congruent condition contained a 
correctly pronounced word as an auditory prime and an 
identical word as a visual target (tansu LHH – tansu LHH). In 
the prosodically mispronounced condition, the pitch accent 
pattern of the auditory prime was replaced with the other, and 
the visual target remained the same (*tansu HLL – tansu LHH). 
In the segmentally mispronounced condition, the voicing of the 
initial consonant was replaced with the other (*dansu LHH – 
tansu LHH). Finally, the baseline condition contained a 
phonetically and semantically unrelated word as an auditory 
prime (sabaku “desert” – tansu LHH). 
The audio files were recorded by an experienced female 
native Tokyo Japanese speaker, who is familiar with Japanese 
phonetics, in a sound attenuated room. The production was 
digitized at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz and with 16 -bit 
resolution in Praat 6.3.19 [25] with a Samson Q2U microphone. 
Prime words were originally produced with a carrier phrase 
sore wa ___ “it is ___.” The carrier phrase was removed 
afterwards. Words with mispronunciation were recorded in the 
same manner. Another native Tokyo Japanese listener familiar 
with Japanese phonetics confirmed by auditory impression that 
the mispronounced words were correctly pronounced with the 
other pitch accent pattern in the prosodically mispronounced 
condition, and that they were correctly pronounced with the 
other voicing of the initial consonant in the segmentally 
mispronounced condition. The intensities of all materials were 
normalized. 
The experimental items included unaccented items (tansu 
LHH in the congruent condition) and accented items (dansu 
HLL in the congruent condition) of the pairs. The critical trials 
were 24 in total. However, since we aimed to obtain more 
reliable effect sizes with small sample sizes, and most previous 
studies did not report that the items’ pitch accent pattern 
affected response [12, 15], we did not include this pitch accent 
pattern as a factor in the experiment. To avoid habituation 
caused by repeating trials with similar words, the participants 
experienced one of four conditions per item. The conditions 
were counterbalanced using a Latin square design. We also 
added 72 filler trials, 48 of which had nonword targets 
(nonword proportion was 50%). 
2.1.3. Procedure 
The experiment was conducted online in PCIbex [26]. The 
participants used their own com puter and listened to the 
auditory materials at their comfortable level through 
headphones or earphones. The experiment lasted for 30 min. 
The main task was a lexical decision task in the cross-modal 
priming paradigm, accompanied by a dummy auditory 
comprehension task. The participants first completed 6 practice 
trials and then performed 96 test trials with an optional break. 
At the beginning of each trial, a fixation point (+) was 
presented at the center of the display. The auditory prime was 
played after 1000 ms. The participants were instructed to listen 
to it carefully. At the offset of the auditory prime, the fixation 
point disappeared, and the visual target was subsequently 
presented. The inter-stimulus interval (ISI) between primes and 
targets was thus 0 = ms. Target words were presented in their 
common form, i.e., kanji. However, foreign words (e.g., dansu 
“dance”) were presented in katakana (Japanese syllabary 
characters). The participants were asked to judge whether the 
target words were real words or nonwords and press F for word 
response and J for nonword response. The participants were 
asked to press the key as rapidly and accurately as possible. The 
timeout for the lexical decision was 3000 ms. After the lexical 
decision, participants answered whether the auditory prime 
word they listened to during the trial was trimoraic or 
tetramoraic (some filler items contained tetramoraic prime 
words). This was a dummy task to confirm the listeners’ 
attention to the prime. The dummy task was a forced-choice 
task without timeout. After the response, the next trial started 
automatically. The interval between trials was 1000 ms. 
After the lexical decision task, a follow-up production task 
was conducted to confirm participants’ knowledge of the pitch 
accent patterns of the experimental items. In this task,

2.2. Results and Discussion 
The responses in the follow-up production task were regarded 
as correct if the participants produced words appropriately in 
the pitch accent pattern of Tokyo Japanese. Based on the results 
of the production and lexical decision tasks, data from three 
participants were not used for the reaction time analysis because 
of their low response accuracy (less than 80%). In the reaction 
time analysis, reaction times less than 300 ms or more than 1600 
ms were regarded as outliers and removed from the data. 
Additionally, individual data points from trials in which the 
participants made incorrect responses in the lexical decision 
task or containing primes that the participants mispronounced 
in the production task were excluded. 
The reaction times were analyzed by a linear mixed-effects 
(LME) model in R 4.4.0 [27]. The fixed effect was the prime 
condition. Comparisons between the congruent and baseline 
conditions, between the prosodically mispronounced and 
baseline conditions, and between the segmentally 
mispronounced and baseline conditions were coded as 0.75 and 
–0.25, respectively. The slopes and intercepts of both 
participants and items were included as random effects. An 
optimal model was determined by backward selection using the 
step function. A maximal model containing all the effects was 
initially calculated and the random effects of least variance 
were iteratively removed. After choosing the optimal model, 
data points with an absolute residual greater than 2.5 were 
additionally removed from the final model. 
The mean reaction times for each condition are shown in 
Figure 1. The LME model (formula: RT ~ Prime Pattern + (1 | 
participant)) suggested that the mean reaction time for the 
congruent condition was significantly shorter than that for the 
baseline condition (β = –83.207, SE = 27.696, df = 484.781, t = 
–3.004, p = .003). This decrease in reaction time reflected a 
repetition priming effect, where response to target words was 
facilitated by preceding presentation of the prime words. 
However, the mean reaction time for the prosodically 
mispronounced condition was significantly longer than that for 
the baseline condition (β = 61.533, SE = 27.328, df = 477.022, 
t = 2.252, p = .025), whereas there was no significant difference 
between the mean reaction times for the segmentally 
mispronounced condition and the baseline condition (β = 
48.430, SE = 28.744, df = 481.493, t = 1.685, p = .093). 
The reaction times for the prosodically mispronounced 
condition and the segmentally mispronounced condition 
indicated that mispronunciation of lexical prosody or segments 
disturbed appropriate lexical access to intended words, as 
evidenced by the lack of priming effects in these conditions. 
While both segmental and prosodic mispronunciation affected 
lexical access, the response to segmental mispronunciation was 
neutral compared with the response to the unrelated prime (i.e., 
the baseline condition). In contrast, the response to prosodic 
mispronunciation resulted in greater inhibition. 
This difference is interpreted as an indication that when 
segmental–prosodic incongruity exists in the auditory words, 
listeners tend to interpret them based on prosodic rather than 
segmental information. The use of prosodic information to 
resolve incongruity resulted in greater inhibition in the 
prosodically mispronounced condition (*tansu HLL) and 
relatively less inhibition in the segmentally mispronounced 
condition (*dansu LHH). 
However, since spoken word recognition is a dynamic 
process, in which the cues used for lexical competition change 
over time [28, 29], it is plausible that lexical prosody is utilized 
only at the offset of word presentation (ISI = 0 ms) and that its 
priority usage may be weakened at another time of lexical 
access. To address this possibility, we used delayed priming at 
an ISI of 750 ms in E xperiment 2. In previous studies, a 750 
ms delay allowed the evaluation of the postlexical process of 
spoken word recognition, in which contextual information 
overrides contextually incongruent candidates [5, 22]. In a 
study in Japanese, listeners reinterpreted mispronounced lexical 
prosody within this latency [21]. In Experiment 2, we changed 
the ISI of the target presentation from 0 ms to 750 ms after the 
offset of the prime word. 
3. Experiment 2 
3.1. Methods 
3.1.1. Participants 
Thirty-four native speakers of Tokyo Japanese (19–41 years 
old; mean = 26.12; SD = 5.79) participated in the experiment. 
They self-identified as being from the Kanto area in Japan. 
They received monetary compensation for their participation. 
3.1.2. Materials 
Prime and target items used in Experiment 2 were identical to 
those used in Experiment 1. 
3.1.3. Procedure 
The procedure was the same as that used in Experiment 1, 
except that the ISI between primes and targets was set to 750 
ms to evaluate the postlexical process of spoken word 
recognition when listening to mispronounced materials.

because of their low response accuracy (less than 80%) in the 
production or lexical decision tasks. Outliers were excluded

The inhibition evoked by mispronunciation appeared in the 
opposite direction to that in Experiment 1. When the ISI was 
750 ms, the response to segmental mispronunciation resulted in 
greater inhibition than that to prosodic mispronunciation. This 
finding indicates that an attempt to interpret words based on 
segments ignoring prosodic information was made in the 
postlexical process 750 ms after the offset of the prime 
presentation. The interaction effect between the segmentally 
mispronounced condition and experiment also suggests that a 
segment-based interpretation emerged after the initial process 
of spoken word recognition. These results are in line with those 
of a previous study in which mispronunciation of lexical 
prosody was resolved within 750 ms with reference to 
contextual information [21]. The present results further suggest 
that recognition without lexical prosody occurs during the 
postlexical process when there is segmental –prosodic 
incongruity in the presented words. 
4. General Discussion 
Two cross-modal priming experiments demonstrated that 
participants’ responses were inhibited by prosodic 
mispronunciation at ISI = 0 ms but by segmental 
mispronunciation at ISI = 750 ms. These results suggest that 
lexical access to segmentally–prosodically incongruent words 
is a dynamic process in which listeners first attempt a prosody-
oriented interpretation and then shift to a segment-oriented 
interpretation within 750 ms. When two phonological cues, 
segments and lexical prosody, apparently contradict each other, 
although listeners try both interpretations based on one of these 
cues, the cue-weighting for each piece of information changes 
over time. 
The priority usage of lexical prosody in equivocal situations 
is in line with the observation that it constrains the initial stage 
of lexical access [7–10, 12, 15]. The present study further 
demonstrates that lexical prosody overrides segmental 
mispronunciation during the initial lexical access. This may be 
because lexical prosody is lexical information, whereas 
individual segments are not. Since lexical prosody provides 
more reliable information for identifying the spoken words, 
listeners heuristically rely more on lexical prosodic information 
than on segmental information when these two cues are 
incongruent. However, cue-weighting is completely reversed in 
later stages. Since it is logically possible that lexical prosody is 
mispronounced, listeners interpret words regardless of lexical 
prosodic information in the postlexical process 750 ms after the 
offset of the prime presentation. 
In the present study, however, the 750 ms latency to assess 
the postlexical process in Experiment 2, which was derived 
from English studies [5, 22], was arbitrary. Validation of this 
latency in Japanese spoken word recognition and identification 
of the precise timing for reanalysis must be addressed in future 
studies. 
Even with these limitations, the present study potentially 
challenges traditional spoken word recognition models that do 
not utilize prosodic information [30–32], suggesting that lexical 
prosodic information is important for spoken word recognition 
[3], particularly in Japanese. Moreover, the present study 
supports the dynamic process of lexical competition [28, 29] 
against segmental and lexical prosodic cues, showing both 
rigidity and flexibility to lexical prosodic information that 
changes over the time course of spoken word recognition.
