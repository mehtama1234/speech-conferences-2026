Unified Audio-Visual Modeling for Recognizing Which Face Spoke When and
What in Multi-Talker Overlapped Speech and Video
Naoki Makishima, Naotaka Kawata, Taiga Yamane, Mana Ihori, Tomohiro Tanaka, Satoshi Suzuki,
Shota Orihashi, Ryo Masumura
NTT Corporation, Japan
naoki.makishima@ntt.com
Abstract
We have developed a model that jointly recognizes which face
spoke “when” and “what” from multi-talker overlapped speech
and video of multiple speakers. For understanding of videos in
which multiple speakers are speaking, it is important to recog-
nize which face spoke “when” and “what” from the overlapped
speech and multiple speakers’ videos. Conventional methods
have to combine speech separation, active speaker detection,
and audio-visual speech recognition to address this task. How-
ever, the combined system makes the system complex and sub-
optimal. To address this problem, our idea is to serialize “which
face spoke when and what” of multiple speakers into a single to-
ken sequence, which is recursively estimated with the proposed
unified model using multiple speakers’ videos and overlapped
speech as input. Experimental results demonstrate the validity
of the proposed method.
Index Terms : overlapped speech recognition, audio-visual
speech recognition, active speaker detection
1. Introduction
For a comprehensive understanding of videos in which multiple
speakers are speaking, it is important to recognize which face
spoke “when” and “what”. Conversations such as meetings and
chats typically include speech where multiple people are speak-
ing simultaneously, so the video and speech in such situations
include overlapped speech and simultaneously moving multi-
ple faces, where it can be difficult to determine which face is
speaking and what is being said.
Several studies have tackled partial problems related to the
task of recognizing which face spoke “when” and “what” from
overlapped speech and multiple videos. The typical partial
problem is to estimate what is spoken from a single video of
the speaker’s mouth region, which is known as audio-visual
speech recognition (A VSR). In [1–5], transcription of single-
talker speech is estimated using the given video of the speaker.
For addressing the overlapped speech, a popular approach is to
train a model which transcribes the speech of the target speaker
specified by the videos. In [6, 7], A VSR of overlapped speech
was conducted by using videos of the speakers as keys to im-
plicitly separate overlapping speech.
In practice, multiple videos corresponding to all the peo-
ple present in the scene are obtained. Thus, we have to decide
which face to use before A VSR. The task to estimate which face
is speaking is called active speaker detection [8–15]. In [15], to
estimate which face spoke “what”, an attention layer is intro-
duced to the encoder, which computes the attention score over
multiple face tracks for each audio frame. Although the con-
ventional studies have succeeded in estimating which face is
speaking, they only address a single-talker speech, where the
start and end time of the utterance is clear and only a single
speaker’s video corresponds to the speech. In practice, the video
can include multiple speaking faces as well as non-speaking
faces, leading to overlapped speech caused by multiple speak-
ers speaking simultaneously. Thus, to recognize which face
spoke “when” and “what” from overlapped speech and multi-
ple videos, we have to combine another system such as speech
separation [16–20]. However, the combined system makes the
system complex and suboptimal. To the best of our knowledge,
no prior research has investigated a unified model that estimates
which face spoke “when” and “what” from overlapped speech
and videos of multiple speakers.
To address this problem, our idea is to recursively estimate
“which face spoke when and what” of multiple speakers uti-
lizing overlapped speech and all videos of multiple speakers
as input instead of the conventional approach that transcribes
the single-speaker speech specified by a video. For the au-
toregressive modeling, we serialize transcriptions, timestamp
tags [21,22], and newly introduced video tags of multiple speak-
ers in the same way as serialized output training (SOT) [23].
The video tag is used to associate which face corresponds to the
transcription and the timestamp tag represents the start and end
time of the utterance.
In this paper, we propose the unified modeling of recogniz-
ing which face spoke “when” and “what” in multi-talker over-
lapped speech and video. The proposed method estimates the
serialized token sequence using multiple speakers’ videos and
overlapped speech as input. The serialized token sequence con-
sists of multiple speaker’s transcriptions, timestamp tags, and
video tags that associate each transcription to the correspond-
ing video. In our experiments, we introduce two new objective
criteria to evaluate the prediction performance of which face
spoke “when”, and which face spoke “what”. The experimental
results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms the
combined system that separately estimates “when and what” is
spoken and which face is speaking.

of situations where multiple speakers are speaking, addressing

ple speakers’ videos to estimate which face spoke “when” and
“what”, we train the model so that it estimates the serialized to-
ken sequence that reveals “which face spoke when and what” of
multiple speakers utilizing overlapped speech and all the videos
of multiple speakers.

4. Experiment
4.1. Dataset
We used the Lip Reading Sentences 3 (LRS3) dataset [25] for
our experiments. Although the sentences of LRS3 in the train-
val and test sets are clipped to 100 characters or 6 seconds,
the pre-train set includes longer videos, which leads to a gap
between the training set and the others. Thus, we mixed the
original pre-train, train-val, and test sets and split them into
new pre-train, train-val, and test sets so that speakers do not
overlap between datasets and the train-val and test sets include
longer audio. Since LRS3 consists of single-talker videos, we
created two-speaker and three-speaker simulated mixtures by
mixing the utterances of different speakers. When mixing the
audio signals, the original volume of each utterance was kept
unchanged, resulting in an average signal-to-interference ratio
of about 0 dB. The delay values applied to each utterance were
randomly chosen under the constraints presented in [23]. The
start times of individual utterances differed by 0.5 s or longer
so that every utterance in each mixed audio sample had at least
one speaker-overlapped region with other utterances. Since the
length of the created overlapped speech exceeds that of each
speaker’s video, we copied the nearest frame of the video to fill
the empty frames. For the memory constraint, we only used
one frame every five frames, which resulted in 5 fps videos. We
clipped 96× 96 regions around mouth and converted them to
gray scale. We added Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.05
to every frame of the video.
We used 80 log mel-scale filterbank coefficients as acoustic
features, which were extracted using a 20-ms-long Hann win-
dow with a 10-ms-long shift. We used 8K sentence pieces as
textual tokens. We set Q to 0.5, which means continuous times-
tamps were rounded every 0.5 s.
4.2. Implementation
For the video encoder, we used MobileNetV3-Small [26]. The
speech encoder consists as follows. First, the acoustic feature
was passed to layers composed of two 1× 1 convolutions with
1× 1 strides, two max pooling with a stride of 2, two 3× 3
depthwise convolutions with 1× 1 strides, and two long-short
term memory layers with outputs of 256 dimensions. Next,
we stacked eight Transformer encoder blocks, where the num-
ber of heads in the multi-head attention was set to 4, the di-
mensions of the output continuous representations were set to
256, and the dimensions of the inner output in the position-wise
feed-forward networks were set to 1,024. Before concatenation
in Eq. (9), we added positional encoding to the output of the
speech encoder and the video encoder so that the model cap-
tures the temporal alignment. The dimension of the embedding
in Eq. (8) was 256. We used a Transformer-based model [27,28]
in this paper. For the Transformer encoder in Eq. (10) and
the Transformer decoder in Eq. (11), we stacked two-layer
Transformer encoder blocks and two-layer Transformer decoder
blocks, respectively, where the settings were the same as those
of the speech encoder. For the activation function, we used
Swish [29].
4.3. Settings
We compared the following five methods: conventional single-
talker ASR, conventional single-talker A VSR, combined sys-
tem of multi-talker ASR and lip moving detection, combined
system of multi-talker A VSR and lip moving detection, and the
proposed method. The single-talker models were trained with
single speaker speech and the corresponding video while the
multi-talker models were trained with all the speech and the
corresponding videos including single speaker, two speakers,
and three speakers. For the fair comparison, the architecture
of all the models was the same. The multi-talker A VSR is a
model that adds multiple video inputs to the multi-talker ASR
model, which estimates transcriptions and timestamp tags with-
out video tags from overlapped speech and videos of multiple
speakers. As a naive approach to estimate which face is speak-
ing using the multi-talker ASR or multi-talker A VSR, we exper-
imented with a combined system utilizing a lip moving detec-
tion model. The lip moving detection model, which consists of
the video encoder described in Section 4.2, four Transformer-
encoder blocks, and a classification layer, estimates “1” when
the lip is moving (otherwise, zero) in a frame-wise manner. It
was trained with the multi-talker training data described in Sec-
tion 4.1 so that the copied frames were predicted as zero and its
accuracy on test data was 97.6%. To estimate video tags with
the lip moving detection model, we calculated the match rate of
all possible pairs of the predicted lip movement of each video
and the predicted timestamp tag of each utterance, and assigned
the video tag to each utterance with the highest match rate al-
lowing duplication.
To investigate the effect of the ordering of the transcrip-
tions and video tags of the proposed method, we compared
the following three methods: the proposed method without
transcription, the proposed method with video tags in front of
each transcription (Proposed method w/ tag front), and the pro-
posed method with video tags after each transcription (Proposed

We used the word error rate (WER), and the newly intro-
duced visual word error rate (VWER) and visual time error rate
(VTER) to evaluate the overall performance of the methods. In
contrast to the original WER that only considers “what” is spo-
ken, VWER is a criterion that evaluates the prediction perfor-
mance of which face spoke what. In VWER, every video has
a label transcription, and the predicted video tag’s transcription
become the label for evaluating WER. In other words, when the
predicted video tag is not correct, the predicted transcriptions
are counted as error even when they match the transcriptions of
another video. VTER is a criterion that evaluates the predic-
tion performance of which face spoke when. In VTER, every
video has an utterance-level timestamp label and the predicted
video tag’s timestamp label become the label to calculate TER.
TER was calculated as the sum of missed speaker rate and false
alarm rate [22]. When comparing hypothesized boundaries to
references, we used a tolerance of±250 ms. When calculating
the original WER in multi-talker overlapped ASR settings, we
compared hypotheses with references while considering the or-
der of utterances. We only evaluated textual tokens excluding
the special tokenO, the timestamp tokenT , and the video tag
tokenU.
4.4. Results
Table 1 shows the result. Note that WER is relatively high com-
pared to other studies using LRS3 because we used a different
test set that includes longer audio, as explained in Section 4.1.
We can see that the proposed method outperforms the combined
system in terms of both VTER and VWER. Since the difference
between VWER and WER arises from the prediction error of
video tags, the combined system makes more errors on video
tag assignment compared to the proposed method. We assume
this is because although the combined system that utilizes the
independently predicted lip movement and timestamp tags ac-
cumulate errors, the proposed method is directly optimized to
estimate it.
We can see that the proposed method (w/ tag front) and
the proposed method (w/ tag end) achieve almost the same per-
formance in terms of both VTER and VWER while the pro-
posed method (w/o transcription) outperforms them in terms of
VTER. We assume this is because the prediction error of tran-
scription causes the model to assign incorrect video tags. How-
ever, transcriptions are expected to help estimating video tags
in more challenging scenes where the speakers of some videos
do not speak and the speakers not included in the video speak,
which remains our future work.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed a unified model that jointly recog-
nizes which face spoke “when” and “what” from multi-talker
overlapped speech and video of multiple speakers. Most con-
ventional methods have transcribed “what” is spoken from the
given video frames, but in videos where multiple speakers are
simultaneously speaking, figuring out which face spoke “when”
and “what” is practically important. To address this problem,
we proposed the unified model that is trained to estimate the
serialized token sequence revealing which face spoke “when”
and “what ” from videos of multiple speakers and overlapped
speech. To evaluate the overall performance, we used newly
introduced objective criteria named VWER and VTER in ad-
dition to WER. Experimental results showed that although the
proposed method is a single model that is much simpler than
the combined system, it delivered the superior performance.
