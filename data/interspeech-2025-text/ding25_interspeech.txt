Linguistic Masking and Its Release in Simulated Electric-Acoustic Hearing 
Yuting Ding, Xuefei Wang, Fei Chen 
Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Southern University of Science and 
Technology, Shenzhen, China 
fchen@sustech.edu.cn 
 
Abstract 
Combined electric-acoustic stimulation (EAS) with a cochlear 
implant (CI) and a hearing aid can significantly improve CI 
user’s speech-in-noise performance, which is noted as 
combined stimulation advantage. The present study aimed to 
investigate this advantage in the context of linguistic masking, 
i.e., when the target speech and masker were from the same or 
different languages, and how combined stimulation and 
different linguistic masker affected linguistic release from 
masking (LRM). Mandarin sentences were mixed with 2-talker 
babble maskers spoken in Mandarin, Cantonese or English, 
processed by noise vocoder models simulating CI or EAS 
hearing, and presented to normal-hearing listeners to recognize. 
Experimental results showed the combined stimulation 
advantage under linguistic masking with all three languages. 
The EAS condition yielded significant difference between the 
scores of two LRMs (i.e., English vs. Mandarin, and Cantonese 
vs. Mandarin). Under linguistic masking with Cantonese, there 
was significant difference between the scores of CI- and EAS-
based LRMs. The findings of the present work provided new 
insights on the benefits for CI users using combined electric-
acoustic stimulation. 
Index Terms: cochlear implants, combined electric-acoustic 
stimulation, combined stimulation advantage, linguistic release 
from masking 
1. Introduction 
Cochlear implants (CIs) represent a significant advancement in 
auditory rehabilitation for individuals with profound-to-severe 
hearing impairment, effectively restoring their hearing 
capabilities [1]. The functioning of CIs involves the extraction 
of 
critical 
acoustic 
information 
from 
speech 
input, 
predominantly through the use of multi-channel temporal 
envelope waveforms. This extracted envelope information is 
then used to electrically stimulate the auditory nerves, which 
facilitates sound perception in the brain. Although many users 
of CIs demonstrate commendable speech comprehension in 
quiet settings, their ability to perceive speech is greatly 
compromised in noisy environments. This challenge has been 
well-documented within the field of CI technology and remains 
a focal point for ongoing research and development [e.g., 2-3].  
Numerous studies have advocated for the implementation 
of combined electric-acoustic stimulation (EAS) for CI users 
who maintain residual low-frequency hearing in the implanted 
ear [e.g., 4-8]. The EAS approach involves the surgical 
insertion of a short electrode array into the base region of the 
cochlea, designed to preserve residual low-frequency acoustic 
hearing (typically from 20 to 60 dB up to 750 Hz), while users 
often experience severe-to-profound hearing loss at frequencies 
above 1000 Hz [e.g., 4]. In this configuration, low-frequency 
speech information is delivered through a hearing aid (HA), 
whereas high-frequency speech information is transmitted via 
the CI. Early studies have demonstrated that the incorporation 
of low-frequency acoustic information significantly enhances 
speech perception for CI users, particularly in challenging 
listening scenarios characterized by background noise [e.g., 6] 
and in the comprehension of tonal languages [e.g., 5, 8]. 
Following early investigations into the mechanisms 
underlying the combined stimulation advantage (i.e., in contrast 
to CI-only hearing) in noisy environments, the current study 
examines the impact of linguistic masking in EAS hearing. 
Research has demonstrated that linguistic dissimilarity between 
target and masker speech can enhance speech comprehension, 
a phenomenon referred to as linguistic release from masking 
(LRM), particularly under challenging signal-to-noise ratio 
(SNR) conditions [e.g., 9]. LRM has been documented in 
numerous studies focusing on English [e.g., 10-11] and 
Mandarin speech perception [e.g., 12] involving normal-
hearing (NH) listeners. For instance, study in [11] showed that 
at an SNR of 0 dB or lower, monolingual English speakers 
exhibited poorer recognition of English sentences when masked 
by an English 2-talker babble compared to a Mandarin 2-talker 
babble. While hearing-impaired listeners generally demonstrate 
reduced speech-in-noise performance relative to NH listeners, 
there is a paucity of research investigating LRM in this 
population [e.g., 13-14]. Viswanathan et al. observed LRM 
benefits in simulated CI hearing, where NH listeners recognized 
English speech more effectively when presented with Dutch 
maskers than with English maskers while listening to vocoded 
(a method to simulate CI speech processing or CI hearing) 
English sentences [13]. Lung et al. found that the linguistic 
characteristics of the masker significantly affected the 
recognition of vocoded Mandarin sentences, with increased 
interference noted from English, Cantonese, and Mandarin, 
aligning with earlier findings from NH listeners [e.g., 12]. 
The aim of this study is twofold: 1) We investigate the 
impact of linguistic maskers on speech perception in simulated 
combined electric-acoustic stimulation. The target language in 
this study is Mandarin, with three distinct masker languages 
(i.e., Mandarin, Cantonese, and English) selected based on their 
varying degrees of linguistic similarity to Mandarin. 2) We 
observe two types of LRM effects, i.e., Cantonese vs. Mandarin, 
and English vs. Mandarin. Consequently, we examine how any 
observed combined stimulation advantage interacts with the 
performance associated with these LRM effects. 
2. Methods  
2.1. Subjects and materials 
Nine native Mandarin Chinese listeners (5 males, aged 22 to 25 
years) participated in this study. They were undergraduate 
students from the Southern University of Science and

All participants were bilingual in Mandarin and English but 
were not proficient in Cantonese understanding or speaking, 
and exhibited normal hearing levels (i.e., pure tone thresholds 
at 250–8000 Hz lower than 25 dB HL). The experimental 
procedures involving human subjects were approved by the 
Ethical Review Board of the Southern University of Science 
and Technology. 
The target speech material utilized in this study consisted of 
sentences derived from the Mandarin Hearing in Noise Test 
(MHINT) database [15]. The MHINT corpus includes 24 lists, 
each comprising 10 sentences and 10 keywords per sentence, 
all recorded by a male native Mandarin Chinese speaker at a 
sampling rate of 16 kHz. 
Three types of linguistic maskers were used in this 
experiment, i.e., Mandarin, Cantonese, and English. For the 
creation of Mandarin masker, two male native Mandarin 
speakers read in Mandarin eight sentences extracted from a 
newspaper in a naturalistic style. Their readings were recorded 
in a soundproof booth, digitized at a rate of 16 kHz, and 
standardized for root-mean-square (RMS) level. The eight 
sentences read by each speaker were concatenated to generate 
the one-talker masker signal. The two male Cantonese masker 
signals were created by the same way used in creating the two 
Mandarin masker signals. The English masker speech was 
obtained from the TIMIT database [16], comprising eight 
sentences from each of two male speakers, which were 
similarly combined to form two one-talker English masker 
signals. Each of the 6 (=3 languages × 2 speakers) one-talker 
masker signals had a duration ranging from 15 to 25 seconds. 
More details on creating the linguistic masker signal can be 
found in [12]. Note that this study only utilized masker signal 
by male speakers. This was because the MHINT speech was 
recorded with a male speaker, and using male voice in both 
target and masker speech may mitigate any potential effects 
arising from gender differences in linguistic masking. 
2.2. Signal processing 
To generate the 2-talker babble masker in each language, a 
random segment of the masker speech from each speaker was 
selected, ensuring that their duration matched that of the target 
MHINT sentence, and subsequently equalized for RMS level 
before being combined to form the 2-talker signal. The resulting 
2-talker babble was then mixed with the target MHINT 
sentence (the onsets of babble and target sentences were not 
aligned) at signal-to-masker ratio (SMR) levels of 0 dB and –3 
dB. Finally, the linguistically masked MHINT sentence was 
adjusted to match the RMS value of the original sentence. 
This study employed two signal processing conditions for 
simulated CI and EAS hearing. The first condition simulated 8-
band electric-only stimulation, representative of CI speech 
processing, using a noise vocoder [e.g., 17-18]. Note that 
vocoder simulation has been extensively utilized to mimic CI 
hearing in NH subjects. Real CI users exhibit considerable 
variability due to factors such as the etiology of hearing loss and 
differences in implant devices. The vocoder approach proves 
advantageous for evaluating the effects of various processing 
parameters (e.g., the number of stimulation channels) in CI, 
independently 
of 
patient-related 
variables 
[18]. 
In 
implementing the vocoder process, the speech signals were 
initially processed through a pre-emphasis filter (with a first-
order high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 1200 Hz), and 
subsequently bandpass-filtered into 8 frequency bands ranging 
from 80 to 6000 Hz using sixth-order Butterworth filters. The 
cutoff frequencies for the channel allocation of these bandpass 
filters were set at 80, 221, 426, 724, 1158, 1790, 2710, 4050, 
and 6000 Hz, calculated in accordance with the cochlear 
frequency-position mapping function [19]. From each 
frequency band, the envelope was extracted through half-wave 
rectification followed by low-pass filtering with a cutoff 
frequency of 200 Hz using a fourth-order Butterworth filter. 
The resulting envelope waveform was then multiplied by a 
white noise carrier signal. Each band’s output was further band-
limited using the corresponding bandpass filter, and all 
amplitude-modulated white noise signals (with band-limiting 
processing) were summed to create the noise-vocoded stimulus. 
Finally, the amplitude of this stimulus was adjusted to match 
the RMS power of the original input speech signal. 
The second signal processing condition simulated 
combined EAS hearing. To replicate the effects of EAS in 
individuals with residual hearing below 600 Hz, the speech 
signals were low-pass filtered to 600 Hz via a sixth-order 
Butterworth filter. This low-pass stimulus was combined with 
the upper 5 frequency bands (ranging from 724 to 6000 Hz) 
derived from the 5-band noise vocoder. Note that the amplitude 
of the low-pass stimulus was equalized to that of the CI 
simulation. Additionally, RMS power scaling was conducted 
relative to the input speech signal. Further details regarding the 
CI- and EAS-processed stimuli can be found in [20]. 
2.3. Procedure 
Prior to participating in the speech recognition experiment, all 
participants completed a training session. Both the training and 
testing sessions were conducted in a soundproof room. The test 
materials were presented to participants monaurally (i.e., right 
ear) using circumaural headphones at a self-determined 
comfortable listening level. Participants were informed that 
they would be listening to processed Mandarin sentences, with 
each sentence available for playback up to three times. 
During the training session, participants listened to 20 
vocoded MHINT sentences (masked by Mandarin and English 
maskers at SMR of 0 dB) while reading the corresponding 
transcripts to familiarize themselves with the processed speech 
materials. In the testing session, they were instructed to listen 
to the vocoded MHINT sentences without reading any 
transcripts, and repeat or guess (if unsure) the words they heard. 
The experimenter scored the participants’ responses based on 
the number of words correctly identified in each sentence. The 
intelligibility score for each condition was calculated as the 
percentage of words correctly identified in the target MHINT 
sentences for each list. 
Each participant completed a total of 12 conditions: 3 
masker languages (Mandarin, Cantonese, and English) × 2 
SMR levels (0 dB and –3 dB) × 2 vocoder conditions (CI vs. 
EAS). A unique list of MHINT sentences was used for each 
condition, and no target sentence was repeated across 
conditions. The order of the test conditions was randomized 
across participants. The entire experiment took approximately 
one hour to complete for each participant, and a 5-minute break 
was given every 30 minutes during the testing session. 
3. Results 
3.1. Effect of linguistic masking on combined stimulation 
advantage 
Figure 1 shows the Mandarin sentence recognition scores for all 
conditions. The scores were first converted to rational arcsine

units (RAU) using the rationalized arcsine transform [22]. The 
linguistic babble maskers with varying linguistic contents were 
labeled as “Eng,” “Can,” and “Man,” representing masking by 
English, Cantonese, and Mandarin speakers, respectively. 
Statistical significance was determined by using the 
recognition score (in RAU) as the dependent variable, SMR 
level (0 dB vs. –3 dB), masker language (English, Cantonese vs. 
Mandarin), and vocoder condition (CI vs. EAS) as the three 
within-subject factors. Three-way analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) with repeated measures indicated a significant effect 
(F[1, 8]=76.27, p<0.0005) of SMR level, a significant effect 
(F[2, 16]=565.10, p<0.0005) of masker language, and a 
significant effect (F[1, 8]=119.05, p<0.0005) of vocoder 
condition. Analysis also revealed a non-significant interaction 
(F[2, 16]=0.524 p=0.61) between SMR level and masker 
language, a non-significant interaction (F[1, 8]=3.79, p=0.09) 
between SMR level and vocoder condition, a significant 
interaction (F[2, 16]=5.47, p<0.05) between masker language 
and vocoder condition, and a non-significant interaction (F[2, 
16]=0.13, p=0.88) among SMR level, masker language and 
vocoder condition. 
Post-hoc analysis (with Bonferroni correction if needed) 
showed that between two vocoder conditions (i.e., CI vs. EAS), 
all scores at the EAS condition were significantly (p<0.05) 
larger than those at the CI condition, which showed the 
combined stimulation advantage as expected. With respect to 
the effect of SMR level, all recognition scores were 
significantly (p < 0.05) larger at 0 dB SMR than at –3 dB SMR, 
except in one pair of scores at the [CI, Eng] condition (i.e., the 
vocoder condition was CI, and the masker language was 
English). With respect to the effect of masker language, 
Mandarin sentence recognition score was significantly 
(p<0.017) larger in English or Cantonese masker than in 
Mandarin masker under all paired conditions, and significantly 
(p<0.017) larger in English masker than in Cantonese masker, 
except in the two pairs of scores at the [CI, 0 dB] and [CI, –3 
dB] conditions.

4. Discussion and Conclusion 
Linguistic masking and its release have been studied for normal 
hearing and electric hearing (by CI listeners). The present work 
further investigated how different linguistic maskers affected 
speech understanding in simulated electric-acoustic hearing 
(simulating those listeners fitted with CI and hearing aid). First, 
Figure 1 shows the combined stimulation advantage under 
conditions with all three linguistic maskers, suggesting that the 
impact of linguistic masking does not change the established 
combined stimulation advantage. Hence, the present results 
further extend the scope of combined stimulation advantage. 
Second, EAS hearing demonstrates LRM. That is, when there 
is a large linguistic difference between target and masker 
speech, listeners could benefit this linguistic release from 
masking. Figure 1 clearly shows that, in relative to condition of 
Mandarin target and Mandarin masker (or linguistically 
matched condition), the linguistically un-matched condition of 
Mandarin target and English masker or Mandarin target and 
Cantonese masker yields improved speech understanding 
performance. In addition, as the linguistic difference between 
Mandarin and English (e.g., tonal vs. nontonal) is larger than 
that of between Mandarin and Cantonese (tonal vs. tonal, and 
sharing common phonemes), the linguistically un-matched 
condition of English masker yields larger intelligibility than 
that of Cantonese masker. This result is consistently with early 
findings from normal and CI listeners [e.g., 12, 14]. 
The present further studied how combined stimulation and 
linguistic masker affected the level of linguistic release from 
masking. Some interesting results were revealed in these 
analyses (see Fig. 2). First, it is seen that while LRM with 
English masking is largely larger than that with Cantonese 
masking, statistical analysis showed that only under the EAS 
condition, the difference between two LRMs (English masking 
vs. Cantonese masking) was significant (p<0.05), while under 
the CI condition, there was no significant difference between 
two LRMs. This seemly added new insights on the advantage 
of combined stimulation. While the positive LRM effect was 
also observed in CI hearing, we hypothesize that the limited 
temporal and spectral cues provided by CI speech processor 
diminished the effect of un-matched linguistic masking. That is, 
when masker was English or Cantonese, their masking effects 
did not make a significant difference when they were processed 
by CI speech processor or characterized by a limited number of 
temporal features in CI hearing. In contrast, the usage of hearing 
aid in EAS hearing provided important acoustic cues for speech 
understanding [e.g., 4-8], particularly the tonal and voicing 
information in low frequency. The Cantonese masker caused a 
stronger masking effect than the English masker. Therefore, 
under the EAS condition, a significant (p<0.05) difference was 
observed between the scores of two LRMs (i.e., English 
masking vs. Cantonese masking). Further studies are warranted 
to examine this hypothesis. 
The second interesting finding is that masking language 
may also affect the level of LRM. It is seen in Fig. 2 that under 
the English masking, there is no significant difference between 
the paired LRM scores of CI hearing and EAS hearing. 
However, under the Cantonese masking, the score of LRM 
under the CI hearing is significantly (p<0.05) larger than that 
under the EAS hearing. There is a large linguistic dissimilarity 
between English and Mandarin, and this large linguistic 
dissimilarity determines the level of LRM, which is not affected 
by type of stimulation (i.e., CI or EAS). On the other hand, 
although there is linguistic difference between Cantonese and 
Mandarin (yielding positive LRM effect), Cantonese shares 
some tonal and phonetic features with Mandarin, and adding 
low-frequency cues in EAS hearing exaggerates the masking 
effect of Cantonese masker, potentially counteracting the 
positive LRM effect. Therefore, the EAS hearing receives a 
smaller amount of LRM than the CI hearing. 
The present work could be extended to address the 
following limitations. First, the testing conditions only included 
2-talker masker with the same gender as that of the target 
speech. It is unclear how the gender difference (between masker 
and target speech) interacts with the combined stimulation 
advantage and linguistic release from masking, which warrants 
further studies. Second, in addition to the widely-used 2-talker 
masker, other types of masking conditions have also been tested 
in early works, e.g., using 6-talker masker and cafeteria noise. 
It would be interesting to examine how these masking 
conditions affect the findings in this study. 
In conclusion, the present study examined the effect of 
linguistic release from masking (using three linguistic maskers 
of English, Cantonese, and Mandarin) in the context of 
simulated electric-acoustic stimulation. Consistent with early 
findings from NH listeners and simulated CI hearing, the 
present work found that the recognition of EAS-processed 
Mandarin sentences was significant influenced by linguistic 
masking, increasing influence from English, Cantonese to 
Mandarin. This work also analysed how the type of stimulation 
and masker language affected the linguistic release from 
masking. Interaction between the type of stimulation and 
masker language was found. The EAS stimulation yielded 
significant difference between the scores of two LRMs (i.e., 
English vs. Mandarin, and Cantonese vs. Mandarin). Under 
linguistic masking with Cantonese, there was significant 
difference between the scores of CI- and EAS-based LRMs.
