Selective Auditory Attention Decoding in Naturalistic Conversations Using
EEG-Based Speech Envelope Tracking in Multi-Speaker Environments
Gabriel Ivucic1, Saurav Pahuja2, Dashanka Da Silva2, Tanja Schultz1
1Cognitive Systems Lab, University of Bremen, Germany
2Machine Listening Lab, University of Bremen, Germany
ivucic@uni-bremen.de
Abstract
In daily life, we effortlessly switch attention between different
sound sources while filtering out distractions. Prior research
has shown that the target speaker can be decoded from EEG
in such settings, however, how the switching of attention in-
fluences decoding has not been measured.
We investigated
EEG-based target speaker decoding with exogenous attention
switches in a multi-talker environment, where twenty partic-
ipants alternated listening to two target speakers taking turns
while ignoring two background speakers. Speech envelope re-
construction from EEG showed higher accuracy for the attended
speaker than distractors, with single-trial classification at 76%.
Our findings showed a brief increase in target speaker recon-
struction accuracy after attention switches, suggesting height-
ened alertness when shifting attention to a new speaker. These
findings enhance the understanding of dynamic auditory atten-
tion and support the development of attention-based hearing ap-
plications.
Index Terms: EEG, Auditory Attention, Speech Envelope, At-
tention Switching
1. Introduction
Navigating speech in noisy environments, such as a crowded
room with overlapping conversations, poses a significant chal-
lenge for the auditory system, a phenomenon famously referred
to as the cocktail-party problem [1]. The cognitive ability to
focus on a single speaker while suppressing competing voices,
known as selective auditory attention [2, 3], is essential for ef-
fective communication in everyday life.
Auditory neuroscience seeks to understand how the brain
achieves this remarkable ability by studying how acoustic fea-
tures elicit specific cortical responses. Prior research has inves-
tigated neural representations of various speech features, such
as spectrograms [4], phonemes [5], and semantic features [6].
Among these, the speech envelope—the slow amplitude fluctu-
ations of speech—has proven particularly effective for study-
ing auditory attention.
Research has shown that the recon-
structed speech envelope derived from neural data correlates
more strongly with the envelope of attended speech than with
that of unattended speech, making it a key mechanism for de-
coding selective attention [7]. Additionally, the speech enve-
lope predominantly consists of low-frequency fluctuations (<10
Hz), which align with cortical oscillations in the delta and theta
bands—frequencies known to be crucial for both speech pro-
cessing and attention [8].
To model the relationship between acoustic features and neu-
ral activity, one widely used approach is the temporal response
function (TRF) [9], which characterizes how neural responses
evolve over time in response to auditory input. This approach
can also be applied in the reverse direction, using what are
known as backward models (EEG-to-stimulus reconstruction).
These backward models have been shown to outperform for-
ward models (stimulus-to-EEG mapping) in predicting atten-
tional focus [10]. In this context, backward models can be ap-
plied to speech envelope reconstruction for auditory sources.
As a result, speech envelope reconstruction from EEG has be-
come an effective tool for classifying the attended speaker and
comparing attentional dynamics across experimental conditions
[11, 12].
However, most research to date has focused on neural responses
to speech under static conditions, where participants maintain
attention on a fixed speaker at a fixed location. More recent
work has explored neural dynamics during attention switches
when the attended stimulus changes.
One approach has in-
vestigated exogenously driven switches, where speaker loca-
tions are abruptly swapped to simulate head movements [13],
while another has examined endogenous switches, in which par-
ticipants receive advance cues but must internally initiate the
switch themselves [14]. Both found that decoding of stimulus
features remains robust despite shifts in attention.
Building on this research, we designed a selective attention
experiment that simulates a natural conversation between two
speakers embedded in a noisy environment with two additional
distractor speakers.
Neural responses were recorded using
EEG, making our approach comparable to a prior study using
invasive EEG [15]. Further, we aimed to maximize the number
of occurring attention switches and introduce a greater variety
of speakers, using six target speakers and six distractor speak-
ers, as opposed to the typical male-versus-female setup used in
previous research. In this study, we aim to verify the robust-
ness of EEG-based speech envelope tracking in the context of
following a conversation in a multi-speaker environment. Ad-
ditionally, we assess the direct impact of exogenous attention
switching on target speaker reconstruction accuracy, providing
insights into how dynamic shifts in auditory attention affect
neural tracking of speech.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Experiment for Selective Auditory Attention and At-
tention Switching
We designed an experiment to simulate a multi-talker environ-
ment and investigate how auditory attention switches affect neu-
ral processing. EEG was recorded while participants selectively
attended to alternating speakers in the presence of continuous
background speech.

2.1.1. Setup
Twenty participants listened to one of two target speakers from
loudspeakers positioned at -30° (left) and 30° (right) while ig-
noring two continuous background distractors at -90° and 90°.
All loudspeakers were arranged in a semicircle at a distance of
60 cm. EEG data were recorded at 512 Hz using a 64 active-
channel system (g.HIAMP, g.tec medical engineering GmbH,
Austria). Electrodes were positioned according to the 10/20
system, with two linked-ear reference electrodes and a ground
electrode at Fz. Channel impedances were kept below 20 kΩ.
2.1.2. Audio Stimuli
The target speech was drawn from two audiobooks, ”20,000
Leagues Under the Sea” [16] and ”The Chancellor” [17], nar-
rated by six male speakers (three per book). Each speaker’s
recordings were divided into segments of approximately 18 sec-
onds each, covering 2 to 3 sentences of continuous speech.
Background distractors consisted of six additional male narra-
tors, with whole segments lasting approximately 72 seconds.
Audio levels of all speakers were normalized to -23 LUFS, and
prolonged silences exceeding 500 ms were truncated.
To ensure balanced exposure, we used three fixed cocktail party
configurations, each featuring a unique set of two target and two
distractor speakers. Each configuration was presented in 12 tri-
als, totaling 36 trials. The trial order was counterbalanced to
prevent adaptation effects, with randomized starting positions
of target speakers (left or right). After each trial, participants
answered two multiple-choice questions (MCQ) on speech seg-
ments selected in a pseudo-random but controlled manner to
verify attentional engagement.
2.1.3. Experimental Procedure
Each trial consisted of four 18s ± 2 speech segments, with two
from each target speaker alternately presented, totaling
72s
per trial and resulting in three attention switches. To mimic
turn-taking in a natural conversation, target speakers alternated
speaking with 300 ms pauses between their speech segments,
reflecting typical silence durations in spoken dialogue [18].
However, the content between target speakers was not semanti-
cally related (e.g., no question-answering) to prevent multiple-
choice questions for certain segments from being answered
through information transfer. Throughout a trial, three speakers
were always active — one target speaker and two distractors,
while the two distraction speakers were constantly talking in
the background without the speaker switching. In total, partici-
pants completed 36 trials and experienced 108 exogenous atten-
tion switches. The entire experimental session lasted around 90
minutes, with approximately 42 minutes of recorded EEG data
from the trials.
2.2. Speech Envelope Reconstruction from EEG
We aim to reconstruct the audio envelope, representing the loud-
ness levels of the speech stream, based on its linear relationship
with EEG activity. By comparing the reconstruction accuracy
for each speaker’s envelope, we can decode the target speaker
by selecting the envelope with the highest reconstruction accu-
racy [7].
EEG preprocessing involved the removal of eye blinks using in-
dependent component analysis (ICA) and the interpolation of
bad channels.
Furthermore, data were bandpass filtered be-
tween 0.5 and 12 Hz, as this frequency range was found to
capture neural modulations associated with the speech envelope
most effectively. [9, 19], and downsampled to 64 Hz. Regard-
ing speech stimuli, for each trial, the attended (target) and unat-
tended (distraction) speech streams were separated into their
original clean forms, and their broadband envelopes were ex-
tracted using the Hilbert transform. The envelopes were then
anti-aliased, downsampled to 64 Hz, and normalized between 0
and 1.
To reconstruct the envelopes from the EEG, we use a linear de-
coding model that maps the neural responses from all 62 EEG
channels over 0−200 ms time lags to the envelope samples that
elicited them. We implement this using the receptive field class
in the MNE library [20] with multivariate linear regression and
ridge regularization. The reconstruction is formulated as:

3. Results
3.1. Envelope Reconstruction from Neural Responses
We first present the raw envelope reconstruction results from
neural responses. Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of Pear-
son’s correlations to reconstruct the target speaker stream (al-
ternating between two speakers) and the constant background
distractors across all trials and subjects. For each participant,
the target speaker envelope (mean r = 0.068 ± 0.045) was
significantly better reconstructed than the distractor speakers
(r = 0.009 ± 0.03, p < 10−84, Wilcoxon), and also better
than a baseline trained with reversed envelope labels (gray box,
p < 10−94, Wilcoxon). The distractors also showed a slightly
higher correlation than the random baseline over all subjects,
with a few exceptions (p < 0.002, Wilcoxon).
3.2. Target Speaker Decoding
We further evaluate the ability to perform single-trial decoding
from the envelopes reconstructed from neural responses. By
counting the number of trials (n=36) in which the Pearson r
for the target speaker exceeded that of the distractor speakers,
we obtained an average target speaker detection accuracy of
76% ± 12% (chance level: 33%; upper simulated confidence
limit: 46.7% [22]), as shown in Figure 2. Notably, participant
10 exhibited poor performance under the significance threshold
and was excluded from further analyses. To test the capability

4. Discussion
For this study, we designed a selective auditory attention ex-
periment that simulates following a conversation to explore the
2927

dynamics of exogenous attention switching and its effects on
the neural tracking of speech envelopes from attended speak-
ers. Our results demonstrate that even in the presence of at-
tention shifts, the target speech envelope can still be reliably
tracked, with accuracy consistently outperforming that of dis-
tractor speakers in single trials. This provides further evidence
supporting the utility of envelope reconstruction for decoding
auditory attention [7]. Our findings align with recent studies
[13, 14, 15] that also observed successful envelope tracking dur-
ing attention shifts.
We found that decoding remained above chance level even with
significantly reduced window sizes, dropping from 80% accu-
racy to 44% as window sizes were decreased to just two sec-
onds. This performance reduction is consistent with prior eval-
uations of similar speaker separation angles and window sizes
[23, 24]. The drop in accuracy for lower window sizes can likely
be attributed to the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the neural
responses to speech signals in EEG data. Notably, our envelope
reconstruction results are relatively low compared to intracra-
nial data [25, 26], which offers much higher signal quality. This
issue becomes more pronounced in noisy environments where
clean speech sources are unavailable [23].
Our evaluations of the effect of attention switching on recon-
struction performance revealed a brief increase in decoding ac-
curacy immediately following the switch, followed by a gradual
decrease over time, leading up to the next switch. This suggests
that neural modulation of the speech envelope is dynamic and
responsive to changes in the auditory environment. One pos-
sible explanation for this pattern is increased attentional focus
following the switch, with listeners paying closer attention to
the new target speaker immediately after the attentional shift.
This is consistent with previous neuroscience findings, which
report a decrease in alpha power in parietal and frontal brain
regions shortly after attention shifts, returning to baseline af-
ter approximately five seconds [14], typically associated with
heightened attentional focus. Interestingly, while we observed
a similar pattern for distractor speakers, the effects were not sta-
tistically significant. However, these fluctuations in attentional
focus did not significantly affect decoding accuracy for the tar-
get speaker. Future work could more closely analyze changes
in the EEG signal related to attention-switching events.
Our study focused on exogenous attention shifts, such as fol-
lowing a conversation, rather than voluntary, endogenous at-
tention switches, which have been the focus of previous stud-
ies [13]. The time lag in our used decoding windows remains
a significant challenge for real-time applications, limiting the
practicality of such systems in brain-computer interface (BCI)
technologies. While inducing and timing endogenous shifts in
a controlled environment is challenging, it could offer the ad-
vantage of reducing the time lag in decoding, thereby improv-
ing real-time performance. This is in line with neuroscientific
findings that suggest endogenous attention shifts are linked to
top-down preparatory activity [27]. Tracking this preparatory
activity could provide a more proactive form of decoding, in
contrast to the reactive nature of exogenous shifts.
Looking ahead, future research could focus on directly decod-
ing the moment of attention switching in real-time, enabling
neuro-steered hearing aids that adapt to the listener’s atten-
tion shifts. This could involve leveraging EEG-based features,
such as alpha-power modulations, as well as other markers like
pupil diameter, which has been shown to correlate with atten-
tion switching [14, 28]. Further improvements in decoding ac-
curacy could be achieved by employing more advanced models,
such as deep neural networks (DNNs) [19], though these mod-
els require extensive pretraining with large datasets of speakers
and samples.
5. Conclusion
This study investigated the dynamics of exogenous attention
switching in the context of following a conversation in a noisy
environment and its impact on neural tracking of the speech en-
velope from attended speakers. Our results demonstrate that
the target speaker can be reliably decoded even when attention
switches between speakers. Additionally, we observed a brief
improvement in envelope reconstruction following a switch,
likely reflecting a transient increase in attentional focus when
attending to a new speaker. While improvements in robust real-
time decoding capabilities are needed, these findings lay the
groundwork for future research focused on decoding attentional
switches, with potential applications in neuro-steered hearing
aids for noisy environments.
