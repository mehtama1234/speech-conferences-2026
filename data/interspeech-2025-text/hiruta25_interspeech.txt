Hybrid Data Sampling for ASR: Integrating Acoustic Diversity
and Transcription Uncertainty
Komei Hiruta1, Yosuke Yamano2, Hideaki Tamori2
1Solvance LLC, Japan
2The Asahi Shimbun Company, Japan
komei.hiruta@solvance.jp, yamano-y@asahi.com, tamori-h@asahi.com
Abstract
Efficiently selecting training data is crucial for improving auto-
matic speech recognition (ASR) models while minimizing an-
notation costs. This research extends TypiClust, a data sampling
method originally validated for images, to ASR by jointly con-
sidering acoustic diversity and transcription uncertainty. Our
method clusters speech embeddings from Wav2Vec2 and prior-
itizes typical and low transcription-confidence samples within
each cluster, ensuring selection of representative and hard-to-
train samples.
We evaluate our method on Japanese speech
datasets, CSJ and ReazonSpeech, demonstrating that it achieves
lower recognition error rates than random or single-criterion-
based data selection. These results indicate that selecting data
based on both diversity and uncertainty enhances speech recog-
nition model performance while reducing annotation costs.
Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, active learning,
data sampling
1. Introduction
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is essential in systems that
utilize various speech technologies, such as voice dialogue sys-
tems, subtitle generation, and call center analysis [1, 2]. How-
ever, when the data distribution during training differs from that
in deployment—a scenario known as domain shift—existing
models often fail to perform effectively [3, 4, 5]. For exam-
ple, academic conferences or television broadcasts often present
unique recording conditions and specialized terminology. As
a result, general-purpose speech recognition models frequently
misrecognize the audio, making domain-specific models indis-
pensable [6, 7]. Self-supervised learning models like Wav2Vec2
[8] and HuBERT [9] can improve ASR accuracy even with
minimal labeled data. However, adapting these models to new
speaking styles or domain-specific vocabulary still requires ad-
ditional labeled data, resulting in high annotation costs.
In response to these challenges, the concept of data-centric
AI has gained traction, shifting the focus from merely refining
model architectures to systematically improving and selecting
high-quality data [10]. In this paradigm, ensuring diverse and
representative training samples is considered crucial for robust
performance, especially under domain shift conditions [11].
Active learning naturally aligns with data-centric AI, as it seeks
to achieve high-accuracy models within a limited annotation
budget [12]. In ASR tasks, the sampling strategy must consider
both acoustic diversity—across various speakers and environ-
ments—and the reliability of transcription results [13, 14, 15].
While many existing methods address either acoustic diversity
or transcription uncertainty, only a limited number incorporate
both in a single framework [16, 17]. Consequently, there is a
need for an active learning strategy that jointly considers these
acoustic and linguistic factors.
To address the challenge of combining acoustic diver-
sity and transcription uncertainty in ASR, our research adapts
TypiClust [18], originally validated on image data, to ASR.
TypiClust selects representative samples by clustering self-
supervised embeddings, but its direct application to speech is
non-trivial due to its temporal nature.
Our method clusters
acoustically similar samples using Wav2Vec2-based embed-
dings. It then integrates transcription confidence scores to gauge
recognition reliability, allowing uncertain yet diverse data to be
prioritized. This adaptation enhances ASR accuracy while re-
ducing annotation costs.
In summary, our contributions are threefold:
First, we
adapts TypiClust, originally a diversity-based sampling ap-
proach for images, to speech recognition tasks. Second, the
proposed method combines TypiClust’s diversity sampling with
confidence-score-based uncertainty sampling, addressing both
acoustic variation and transcription reliability. Third, our exper-
iments on the CSJ and ReazonSpeech corpora—each featuring
distinct acoustic properties—demonstrate our method’s effec-
tiveness, with improvements of around 5.0 points in error rate
under certain conditions compared to random sampling.
2. Related Work
2.1. Active Learning
Active learning selects samples that maximize model improve-
ment, using either diversity-based or uncertainty-based crite-
ria [19]. Uncertainty-based methods use entropy or confidence
scores, while diversity-based methods cluster data to select rep-
resentative points [20, 21]. A hybrid approach, TypiClust and
Margin (TCM), has been proposed for image recognition [22],
but its application to ASR remains largely unexplored.
2.2. Self-supervised learning and audio features
In recent years, self-supervised learning models such as
Wav2Vec2 and HuBERT have enabled highly accurate speech
feature extraction and have been widely applied to ASR tasks.
High-dimensional embedding vectors from these models report-
edly capture both acoustic features and some context-based lin-
guistic information [8]. The previous reports indicate that this
feature benefits clustering-based diversity sampling. In practi-
cal ASR, the confidence score has traditionally been used for
active learning and for filtering transcription results [14], mak-
ing it a strong indicator of the model’s uncertainty.
2.3. Active learning in speech recognition
In speech recognition, the input audio signal is a high-
dimensional time series containing tens to hundreds of spectral

features per frame. Meanwhile, the output (transcribed text) is
discrete and varies in length, making it challenging to align with

involves more complexity than in other modalities, such as im-

Previous studies frequently use confidence scores or pre-
diction loss functions to concentrate annotation efforts on error-
prone segments [15, 24, 25, 26]. Mainstream approaches select
samples based on the acoustic model’s output probabilities and
their consistency with a language model [25, 27].
Meanwhile, other research focuses on acoustic-space di-
versity by leveraging compact feature representations that cap-
ture speakers’ characteristics and spectral properties. For in-
stance, certain approaches cluster Mel-Frequency Cepstral Co-
efficients (MFCCs)—which represent speech frequency charac-
teristics on the Mel scale [28]—or i-vectors, which encapsulate
speaker-specific details in low-dimensional vectors [16]. These
clustered representations then guide active learning to select
representative samples [29]. Recent work has also introduced
diversity sampling methods that use high-dimensional embed-
dings from self-supervised learning models such as Wav2Vec2
and HuBERT [30, 31]. However, few studies simultaneously
address acoustic features and transcription uncertainty, indicat-
ing a key challenge in existing research [17, 32].
Several key issues therefore arise in active learning for
ASR: ensuring acoustic diversity (e.g., variations in speak-
ers, environments, noise), managing linguistic errors (unknown
words, accents, dialects), and estimating or correcting tran-
scription uncertainty. In view of these challenges, this study
integrates a clustering method (TypiClust), which uses self-
supervised embeddings, with an uncertainty measure (confi-
dence score). This integration aims to enable more effective
active learning in speech recognition.
3. Proposed Method
3.1. System overview
Figure 1 shows the overall flow of the proposed method.
First, Wav2Vec2 extracts utterance-level embeddings from au-
dio samples, which are then clustered into acoustically simi-
lar groups. For each cluster, we combine a typicality measure
from TypiClust with an uncertainty measure derived from the
transcription confidence score to select representative samples.
Originally validated on image data, TypiClust selects typical
samples using density-aware scoring. We adapt this method
to speech by clustering utterance-level Wav2Vec2 embeddings,
increasing the likelihood of selecting acoustically diverse sam-
ples. Additionally, we integrate transcription confidence scores
to assess recognition reliability and prioritize uncertain sam-
ples, improving ASR robustness under data constraints.
3.2. Ensuring diversity with TypiClust
TypiClust [18] selects the most typical sample in each cluster
as the representative point. This approach has been reported to
better capture local density than centroid-based sampling. It se-
lects representative samples beyond just those near the cluster
centroid, helping maintain acoustic diversity. The method then
selects representative points as typical examples from these re-
gions, prioritizing samples that best represent the acoustic and
statistical properties of each cluster. Specifically, the method
computes a typicality score for each sample in every cluster us-
ing the following equation.

reciprocal of this sum gives higher scores to more representative

3.3. Uncertainty Score Estimation via Confidence Score
Our method computes a confidence score based on the ASR
model’s transcription results. It then prioritizes annotation for
utterances with high uncertainty that is likely to misrecognize.
The proposed method uses the confidence score of transcribed
text derived from an existing ASR model. The confidence score
is defined as the maximum softmax probability of the predicted
word in the ASR model’s output. ASR model’s softmax proba-
bilities were adopted as confidence scores for their reliability in
estimating transcription accuracy. The following equation com-
putes the uncertainty score.

Here, P(y | xi) represents the probability that the ASR model
predicts text y given the acoustic feature xi. This approach em-
ploys the score to identify samples with high uncertainty. It then
prioritizes these samples for selection within each cluster.
3.4. Integrating Diversity and Uncertainty
Before combining TypiClust with the confidence score, we nor-
malize TypiClust’s typicality values to the range [0, 1]. The
method then quantifies clustering-based diversity using this nor-
malized typicality score and measues uncertainty using the con-
fidence score.
• Clustering: Wav2Vec2 utterance-level embeddings are clus-
tered into k groups.
• In-Cluster Sampling: The method calculates an integrated
score for each sample (as described below) and selects the
top m samples per cluster.
• Adjusting the Sampling Ratio: Users can adjust the balance
between uncertainty and diversity to fit the task.
The final integrated score is computed as follows:
S(xi, zi) = λ·Typicality(zi)+(1−λ)·Uncertainty Score(xi)
(3)
Here, λ is a hyperparameter that balances diversity and uncer-
tainty. We test λ = 0.3 (uncertainty-focused), 0.5 (balanced),
and 0.7 (diversity-focused) to analyze how varying λ affects
performance across various data types and volumes. Algorithm

4.3. Evaluation Metrics
This study evaluates ASR performance using Character Error
Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER). These two metrics
measure character-level and word-level recognition errors, re-
spectively.
5. Experimental results and discussion
5.1. Results
Tables 1 show the experimental results on the CSJ and Reazon-
Speech corpora, respectively. Each numerical value indicates an
error rate alongside its 95% confidence interval. In most cases,
the proposed method (TypiClust + confidence score) achieved
lower CER and WER. On the CSJ corpus, it improved CER by
approximately 4.79 points and WER by about 5.29 points com-
pared to Random Sampling. On ReazonSpeech, it improved
CER by around 3.37 points and WER by 2.76 points over Ran-
dom Sampling. Although using TypiClust or the confidence
score alone still yielded some improvements, jointly consider-
ing both diversity and uncertainty generally led to greater per-
formance gains.
5.2. Discussion
The proposed method generally outperforms random sampling
and single-criterion approaches with less data, making it well-
suited for resource-constrained scenarios.
Performance im-
proves with more training data, as observed in the results for
10, 50, and 100 hours. While TypiClust + confidence score per-
forms best for CSJ, using only TypiClust yields better results on
ReazonSpeech when the training audio exceeds 50 hours. This
advantage is likely due to ReazonSpeech’s extensive acoustic
diversity.
Lower λ values improved performance with 10 hours of
data, while higher values were more effective with 50 or 100
hours. This pattern suggests that when the sampling duration is
short, uncertainty contributes more to performance, but as sam-
pling time increases, acoustic diversity becomes a stronger de-
terminant. Interestingly, this trend differs from findings in prior
image-based research [22], indicating a need for future work
to examine the distinct data characteristics of image and audio
domains.
In practical deployments, a straightforward way to choose
λ is to evaluate multiple λ settings on a validation set drawn
from the target domain and then select the value that yields the
lowest error rate. This strategy ensures that the chosen sampling
balance aligns best with the dataset’s characteristics.
6. Conclusion
This study proposes a hybrid sampling method for active learn-
ing in ASR. It combines clustering-based typical sample se-
lection (TypiClust) with a confidence score.
By clustering
Wav2Vec2 utterance-level embeddings and prioritizing high-
uncertainty samples while preserving diversity, the method con-
tributes to ASR performance improvement while reducing an-
notation costs.
Experiments on the CSJ and ReazonSpeech corpora
demonstrate that the proposed approach generally outperforms
random sampling and single-criterion strategies in CER and
WER, though TypiClust alone performs better in some cases.
In future work, we plan to explore uncertainty estimation
directly from self-supervised models and develop dynamic clus-
tering strategies that automatically adjust cluster counts and
sample selection. Additionally, we will compare our method
with state-of-the-art sampling approaches and evaluate its ef-
fectiveness on languages beyond Japanese. We will also address
practical considerations such as inference time, memory over-
head, and computational cost associated with embedding ex-
traction and clustering, aiming to enhance efficiency for large-
scale ASR applications. Addressing these challenges will en-
able more efficient and scalable active learning strategies for
ASR, further enhancing real-world applicability.
