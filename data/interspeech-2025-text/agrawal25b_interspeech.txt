Spot and Merge: A Hybrid Context Biasing Approach for Rare Word and Out
of V ocabulary Recognition
Jatin Agrawal, Bramhendra Koilakuntla, Srikanth Konjeti
1Observe AI, India,
{jatin.agrawal, koilakuntla.bramhendra, srikanth.konjeti }@observe.ai
Abstract
Deep context biasing improves rare word recognition in auto-
matic speech recognition (ASR) but often struggles with larger
biasing lists. Performance on Out-of-V ocabulary (OOV) words
remains limited as the ASR model must learn to generate unseen
token sequences. These limitations become more pronounced
in contact center applications with prevalent business-specific
terminologies. Additionally, using an existing ASR model is
challenging, as deep biasing requires full joint training of the
ASR model and the biasing module. To address these issues,
we introduce ‘spot and merge’ (SAM), a novel Low-Rank
Adapter (LoRA) based system that spots bias words in the
cross-attention weights of the biasing module and merges them
with ASR output. Unlike existing methods, our approach main-
tains strong performance even with larger biasing lists, achiev-
ing a 1.0% absolute word error rate (WER) reduction on Lib-
riSpeech. It also demonstrates robust OOV recognition on an
in-house contact center dataset.
Index Terms: speech recognition, contextual-biasing
1. Introduction
Advancements in end-to-end (E2E) architectures for ASR have
greatly improved transcription accuracy and scalability, mak-
ing them essential for a range of applications, including con-
tact center operations. Prominent E2E approaches such as [1–5]
demonstrate exceptional performance. However, these mod-
els struggle to recognize rare and OOV words. It is especially
critical in contact center settings, where customer interactions
frequently involve domain-specific language, including brand
names, product names and proper nouns.
Context-biasing (CB) methods aim to improve the recogni-
tion of rare words by using a predefined list of biasing words
and phrases, known as the biasing list. This approach is par-
ticularly relevant in contact center applications, where brand
names and product names—commonly forming the bulk of
these words—are typically known beforehand. Research in this
area can be broadly categorized into two categories. The first
category relies on shallow fusion with an external language
model and on-the-fly rescoring during decoding [6–9]. Some
other methods in this category integrate E2E ASR models with
weighted finite state transducers for context-biasing [10, 11].
Shallow fusion methods are highly effective as they preserve
the performance of the base model and eliminate the need for
retraining, ensuring adaptability with minimal overhead. How-
ever, such systems integrate context at inference and do not
leverage model’s internal representations, leading to weaker
context understanding.
The second category of context-biasing methods involves
integrating context directly into ASR models known as deep bi-
asing methods. Initial studies in this field established the foun-
dation [12, 13], leading to the development of the Contextual
Adapter [14], which has since emerged as one of the most
widely adopted methods for deep contextual biasing. It has in-
spired numerous derivative studies [15–22]. Specifically, works
like [20,21] have achieved promising results by embedding con-
textual information into the model’s internal representations,
enabling richer context understanding.
However, the performance of these approaches degrades
significantly as the size of the biasing list increases. Addi-
tionally, they don’t allow adapting an existing ASR model as
they require joint training of the ASR model with the biasing
module. Furthermore, their performance on OOV words re-
mains limited as they rely on the ASR model to output unseen
token sequences. To overcome these limitations, we propose
SAM, a system that integrates the strengths of both shallow and
deep fusion methods. Our novel ‘ spot and merge ’ algorithm
leverages cross-attention weights to spot bias phrases thereby,
reducing reliance on the ASR model. Instead of full train-
ing, we adapt our existing ASR model using LoRA [23]. Our
approach shows superior performance for larger biasing lists
(N = 1000 ), achieving 1.5% and 0.2% absolute WER reduc-
tion as compared to [20] and [21] respectively. We also demon-
strate an 73% absolute improvement in OOV detection F1 score
on an in-house contact center specific dataset. We introduce an
attention-based loss that enhances performance. Furthermore,
we show that Phoneme-BPE [24] outperforms Word-BPE for
OOV recognition. Our key contributions are as follows:
1. We propose a novel ‘ spot and merge’ strategy that leverages
cross-attention weights of the biasing module. It remains ef-
fective even for large biasing lists
2. We show that jointly training LoRA based audio encoder is
enough to train a robust context biasing module. Thus, adapt-
ing an existing ASR model instead of retraining.
3. We introduce a new attention-based auxiliary loss function
designed to enhance cross-attention weights, leading to better
alignment and improved context-biasing performance.
4. We highlight the effectiveness of using Phoneme-BPE in-
stead of Word-BPE for the OOV detection task, achieving
notable improvements in recognition accuracy.
2. Methodology
Our proposed solution (SAM) consists of three components i)
Pretrained ASR Model ii) Context Adapter (CA) iii) Spot and
Merge module. Figure 1 represents the overall workflow.
2.1. Pretrained ASR Model
The ASR model is a Conformer-CTC [25] based model that
consists of an audio encoder of size 100M parameters, paired
with a CTC [1] output layer. It is trained on a large annotated
dataset of 80,000 hours of contact center recordings. Inference
is performed using CTC decoding with beam-search.

2.3. Spot and Merge
For inference, existing deep biasing methods [12–15, 20, 21]
utilize the ASR model jointly trained with the biasing mod-
ule. This hinders their performance on extremely rare and OOV
words since the model struggles to produce unseen token se-
quences correctly. We present our novel ‘ spot and merge’ ap-
proach that eliminates the need for the ASR model to produce
these token sequences. Instead, it leverages attention matrix
(A) generated by the CA module to spot these phrases and
merges them with the ASR model output. As outlined in Al-
gorithm 1, our approach is as follows:
Each element Atm in A represents the likelihood of ob-
serving a phrase with index m at time t. The phrase index with
the maximum likelihood at each timestamp t is denoted as pt:
pt = argmaxm∈[0,M ](At) (10)

end timestamps for each spotted phrase, marking the end when
a streak is broken. A phrase can be spotted multiple times in
different parts of the utterance, represented as a list of start and
end timestamps called time-bounds Om.

3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Training and Testing Datasets
We evaluate our system’s performance on LibriSpeech [26] and
a contact-center specific, in-house conversational dataset.
3.1.1. In-house contact center dataset
In-house training dataset consists of 780 hours of annotated
recordings from contact centers, featuring interactions between
customers and agents in English. A list of rare words is gen-
erated by excluding the top 5000 most frequent words [11, 21].
During training, the bias list consists of rare words present in
the utterances of the current batch along with a set of distractor
phrases, which are randomly selected from the rare word list.
Dynamically generating the bias list with variable distractors
helps prevent model from overfitting.
Test Set 1: The bias list consists of only proper nouns and OOV
terms. To evaluate false positives, the dataset maintains a 1:4
ratio of utterances containing at least one bias phrase to those
containing no bias phrases. The testing dataset comprises 7900
utterances, totaling 19 hours of recordings.
Test Set 2 : The bias list consists of only OOV words neither
seen by the pretrained ASR model nor by CA module in its bias
list. This dataset maintains a 1:9 ratio of utterances containing
at least one bias phrase to those containing none. It consists of
9000 utterances in total, which amounts to 20 hours in total.
3.1.2. LibriSpeech Dataset
The LibriSpeech dataset contains of 960 Hours of training
dataset with ‘test-clean’ and ‘test-other’ being the two test sets.
The bias lists for training and testing datasets are prepared fol-
lowing the same protocols outlined in 3.1.1. For LibriSpeech
experiments, the pretraining of the ASR model and training of
the CA module is done on this LibriSpeech training dataset.
3.2. Modeling Details and Evaluation Metrics

only 1% of parameters trainable and outputs 512-dimensional
features. Low-rank matrices are added to the attention layer
{Wq, Wk, Wv}. For text, we use a SentencePiece BPE tok-
enizer [27] with vocabulary size of 1000. The context encoder

4. Results and Discussion
Results on LibriSpeech . In Table 1, we present a comparative
analysis of our proposed solution against existing CB methods,
specifically P1 [20] and P2 [21], using the LibriSpeech dataset.
Scores for P1 and P2 are taken from their respective papers.
The No-Bias column in Table 1 reflects the performance of the
base ASR model without contextual biasing. As illustrated, our
proposed solution achieves the highest reduction in B-WER for
any number of distractors. Noticeably, the performance gap
between SAM, P1 and P2 widens as number of distractors in-
creases. SAM achieves 53%, 46% B-WER reduction as com-
pared to 22%, 13% by P1 and 38%, 31% by P2 on test-clean
and test-other respectively for N = 1000 . Furthermore, SAM
yields the best overall WER on both test-clean and test-other
dataset with large biasing list ( N = 1000 ). While P2 achieves
a better overall WER at N = 0, SAM achieves higher B-WER
reduction. Moreover, N = 0 condition is irrelevant for real-
world scenarios and thus considered not significant.
Noticeably, both P1 and P2 reduce U-WER as a result of
contextual biasing while SAM does not. This observation can
be attributed to our shallow fusion approach, which preserves
the performance of the base ASR model on words not in bias-
ing list. Interestingly, SAM exhibits a higher B-WER at N = 0
compared to N = 100 . This discrepancy can be due to the
fact that it is trained with N = 50 distractors, and the phrase-
spotting algorithm relies on generating a probability distribution
over the bias list. For N = 0, the quality of this distribution de-
teriorates, as it is effectively reduced to a binary choice between
the bias phrase and the <nobias> term. This impacts model’s
context biasing ability, thereby increasing B-WER.
Results on In-house Test Set 1 . This is a real-world conversa-
tional dataset hence is significantly more challenging than Lib-
riSpeech. In Table 2 we showcase the effectiveness of our sys-
tem with increasing number of distractors. SAM consistently
reduces B-WER while increasing the Recall and F1-score. We
also investigate the effectiveness of our proposedLPS loss in Ta-
ble 2. SAM(V0) represents a version of our model trained with-
out LPS, i.e, LA = LGA-CTC. In particular, SAM (proposed),
trained with LGA-CTC + LPS reported better recall and reduced
B-WER. During training, it converged much faster than V0.

Results on In-house Test Set 2 . This set contains only OOV
terms in the bias list, carefully curated to ensure genuine OOV
inclusion. Table 3 presents term-level precision, recall, and F1
scores. The pretrained ASR model fails to recognize any OOV
term accurately. This issue primarily arises from spelling errors
caused by inaccuracies in the model’s token sequence output.
In contrast, SAM achieves a 63.6% improvement in F1 score
leveraging similarities in audio-text embedding instead of gen-
erating token sequence.
A major challenge in our OOV recognition solution is en-
suring that the context encoder generates accurate text embed-
dings, as it may struggle to infer pronunciations and produce
unreliable representations. We hypothesize that phoneme-based
tokenization can mitigate this issue, as phonemes inherently
capture pronunciation, leading to improved context embed-
dings. To validate this, we replace Word-BPE with Phoneme-
BPE, by converting text into phoneme sequences using a
Grapheme-to-Phoneme model [28]. Each phoneme is assigned
a unique Unicode character to apply Byte Pair Encoding. As
shown in Table 3, the Phoneme-BPE model achieves an abso-
lute 11.4% improvement in the F1 score over the Word-BPE
model, supporting our hypothesis that phoneme-based tokeniza-
tion enhances OOV recognition.
5. Conclusion and Future work
In this paper, we propose SAM, a novel hybrid context-biasing
system that is highly effective and scalable to large biasing
lists. It outperforms existing context-biasing techniques on the
LibriSpeech dataset, and achieves strong results on a more
challenging in-house dataset. We also introduce an auxiliary
loss function based on attention weights, that improves per-
formance. Furthermore, we demonstrate the effectiveness of
Phoneme-BPE for OOV detection. Future work will involve ex-
tending the system’s multilingual capabilities, particularly for
low-resource languages.
