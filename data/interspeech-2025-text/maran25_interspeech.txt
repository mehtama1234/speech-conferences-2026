Beat gestures made by human-like avatars affect speech perception 
Matteo Maran1, Renske Rötjes1, Anna R. E. Schreurs1, Hans Rutger Bosker1 
1Donders Centre for Cognition, Nijmegen, The Netherlands 
matteo.maran@donders.ru.nl, renskerotjes10@gmail.com, anna.schreurs@ru.nl, 
hansrutger.bosker@donders.ru.nl 
 
 
Abstract 
In face-to-face communication, several visual cues support 
speech perception. Even the timing of simple up-and-down 
flicks of the hand, called beat gestures, can convey word stress, 
changing what individuals hear (e.g., CONtent vs. conTENT). 
While beat gestures have been traditionally investigated in 
human-to-human communications, nowadays individuals 
increasingly interact with computer-controlled avatars (e.g., 
virtual assistants). The present study tested whether beat 
gestures produced by an avatar affect word stress perception, 
similarly to human gestures. Furthermore, this study tested 
whether a minimal visual cue such as a 2D moving disc can also 
affect speech perception. Beat gestures made by the avatar 
significantly affected speech perception, albeit slightly less than 
human-made gestures. The disc condition did not affect speech 
perception. The present work lays the foundation for the 
application of (beat) gesturing avatars, which could be used to 
boost speech intelligibility. 
Index Terms: speech perception, audiovisual integration, 
avatar, beat gesture, prosody 
1. Introduction 
In face-to-face communication, speech perception relies on 
both the auditory information and the visual cues produced by 
the speaker [1]. For instance, seeing lip movements can change 
what individuals hear (McGurk effect [2]) and meaningful hand 
gestures support speech intelligibility [3]. Even simple up-and-
down flicks of the hand, called “beat gestures”, affect speech 
perception. Beat gestures are the most frequently produced 
gestures in communication [4] and increase the perceived 
prominence of the linguistic unit they co-occur with [5]. For 
example, beat gestures increase the perceived sentential 
prominence of the word they co-occur with [6], a property 
commonly exploited in public speaking [7]. Notably, recent 
work shows that beat gestures affect speech perception even at 
the lexical level, biasing the perceived prominence of syllables 
within words, ultimately affecting the perceived lexical stress 
(e.g., hearing the noun CONtent vs. the adjective conTENT) [8]. 
In particular, a beat gesture falling on the first syllable of a 
disyllabic word (e.g., content) biases participants to hear the 
word CONtent (Strong-Weak, SW, stress pattern), while a beat 
gesture falling on the second syllable biases towards the word 
conTENT (Weak-Strong, WS, stress pattern). This effect, 
termed “manual McGurk” effect, has been robustly observed in 
both in-lab [8], [9] and online [10], [11] studies, across different

in remote healthcare [16], [17]. Avatars’ beat gestures might 
increase speech intelligibility, facilitating comprehension in 
individuals with temporary or chronic hearing difficulties (e.g., 
due to aging, cochlear implants, etc.) or in non-native speakers. 
The bias in lexical stress perception induced by beat gestures 
could be of particular use in languages that have a large number 
of minimal stress pairs (i.e., pairs of words that differ only by 
lexical stress, such as CONtent and conTENT).  
Previous work suggests that gestures affect the perceived 
human-likeness of avatars [18]. Beat gestures produced by an 
avatar were also shown to provide emphasis at the sentential 
level on the word they accompany [19]. However, whether 
avatars’ beat gestures affect lexical stress perception remains an 
open question, especially in light of the conflicting findings of 
a preliminary report1. In that study, an avatar’s beat gestures 
affected speech perception in simulated cochlear implant 
hearing conditions when words were produced with an 
ambiguous (e.g., content, half-way between CONtent and 
conTENT) or infrequent (i.e., 12% probability of occurrence in 
the tested language [20]) stress pattern. Surprisingly, the 
avatar’s beat gestures failed to reliably affect non-vocoded 
speech perception when the stress cues were ambiguous, 
despite the fact that in this experimental condition visual cues 
could serve as a source of disambiguation. Thus, previous work 
suggests that beat gestures made by avatars might facilitate 
speech perception in individuals with hearing difficulties, while 
leaving open the question of whether they can also be relevant 
for normal hearing conditions. Notably, the visual cues 
provided by the avatar employed in that previous study might 
not have been very natural, especially in terms of kinematics 
and size. Moreover, the avatar lacked any lip movements, 
possibly limiting the extent to which it was considered the 
source of the produced speech. The present study tests whether 
beat gestures made by a more sophisticated avatar can affect 
speech perception, and whether they do so to the same extent as 
human-made gestures.

A second open research question concerns whether some 
degree of human-likeness is essential for artificial visual cues 
to influence stress perception. Previous work showed that an 
isolated pointing hand moving up-and-down does not affect 
lexical stress perception [21]. Additionally, a study showed that 
a minimal visual cue such as a disc, mimicking beat gestures in 
timing and 2D trajectory, elicited de-activation of brain regions 
involved in processing beat gesture and speech synchrony [22]. 
It is likely that, already at early stages of development [23], 
individuals fine-tune audiovisual integration in communication 
to speech and visual cues produced by humans (i.e., the 
interlocutor), but not by non-human entities. The present study 
begins to explore this research question by testing whether a 
minimal moving visual cue, such as a disc having the same 
trajectory as a human beat gesture (henceforth “beat gesture” 
for readability, but at the theoretical level we do not consider 
this visual cue a beat gesture), might affect speech perception. 
Our pre-registered hypotheses are the following. First, we 
expect that the timing of beat gestures made by a human-like 
avatar will affect lexical stress perception, possibly to a similar 
or reduced extent compared to human-made gestures. Second, 
we expect that a moving disc will not affect lexical stress 
perception, since it lacks human-likeness and agency.

The preregistration of this study can be found at

2.1. Participants 
The pre-registered sample size of 50 participants (39 females, 
11 males; mean age = 21.86, range = 17-58) was reached after 
64 participants were recruited using the Radboud Research 
participation system (SONA) and the researchers’ personal 
network, as 14 participants met the pre-registered exclusion 
criteria. All the participants were native speakers of Dutch, with 
normal or corrected-to-normal vision, who did not take part in 
previous studies testing beat gesture and speech integration 
conducted at Radboud University. Participants gave informed 
consent, as approved by the Ethics Committee of the Social 
Sciences department of Radboud University (project code: 
ECSW-LT-2024-10-16-89669). Participants recruited via 
SONA received course credit for participating. 
2.2. Materials and experimental design 
2.2.1. Materials 
Participants watched videos (50 fps) with a human, an avatar or 
a disc (i.e., the three levels of the factor Appearance, Figure 1) 
while a Dutch single word out of four minimal pairs (CONtent 
| conTENT, “content” | “satisfied”, SERvisch | serVIES, 
“Serbian” | “tableware”, VOORnaam | voorNAAM, “first name” 
| “respectable”, VOORruit | voorUIT, “windshield” | “forward”) 
was produced. A beat gesture could be absent, or fall on either 
the first (BeatOn1) or second (BeatOn2) syllable of the 
produced word. The videos of the human condition were taken 
from the publicly available materials of a previous manual 
McGurk study [9]. In these videos the lip movements and beat 
gestures are coherent (i.e., BeatOn1 and lips producing a SW 
word, BeatOn2 and lips producing a WS word), but lip 
movements do not affect lexical stress perception when beat 
gestures are present [9]. The word could be produced with clear 
SW (e.g., CONtent), clear WS (e.g., conTENT), or ambiguous 
(e.g., content) stress cues. The acoustically ambiguous 
condition was sampled from the 7-step phonetic stress continua 
in [9], involving gradual interpolation of the F0 contours from 
the clear SW and WS extremes (duration and intensity were set 
to constant stress-ambiguous values). Specifically, they 
correspond to the audio step 3 for all the minimal pairs, apart 
from SERvisch | serVIES (audio step 4), based on perceptual 
data [9] demonstrating close to 50% SW responses as well as 
good susceptibility to effects of gestural alignment.  
Videos of the avatar condition were generated using 
Wonder Studio’s AI-powered motion transfer and animation 
tool (https://wonderdynamics.com/). Videos of the human 
condition were used as input, and the human actor was replaced 
via AI with the human-looking avatar “The professor”, while 
retaining the audio. Importantly, this avatar closely mimics the 
human movement both in terms of gestures and lip movements. 
Videos for the disc condition were manually created in a four-
step procedure. First, individual frames were extracted from the 
human videos using ffmpeg (http://www.ffmpeg.org). Second, 
in each frame a full circle, similar in color to the human actor’s 
skin color, was added over the position of the hand, based on a 
pre-defined landmark on the index finger. Third, a monochrome 
background, similar in color to the human video, was added 
behind the full circle. Finally, using ffmpeg the frames were 
combined together in a video and paired with the audio taken 
from the initial human video. For all the levels of Appearance, 
catch trials (see Section 2.2.2) videos were created adding a 
white cross at the center of the video.

2.2.2. Experimental design and procedure 
The experimental design consists of the factors Appearance and 
Beat (BeatOn1, BeatOn2). The number of trials per condition is 
adapted from the so-called manual McGurk “mini-test” [10]. 
For each level of Appearance, the experiment included 20 
experimental trials (5 per minimal pair) with a word produced 
with ambiguous stress cues and a beat gesture falling on the first 
syllable (BeatOn1), and 20 experimental trials (5 per minimal 
pair) in which the same acoustic token was paired with a beat 
gesture falling on the second syllable (BeatOn2). We also 
presented 32 trials in which a word was produced with clear 
stress cues (4 SW and 4 WS for each minimal pair) without a 
beat gesture in each appearance condition for perceptual 
anchoring. Finally, for each appearance condition we included 
8 catch trials in which a word was produced with clear stress 
cues (1 SW and 1 WS for each minimal pair) without a beat 
gesture and in which a white cross appeared at the center of the 
screen. In these catch trials, participants were asked to indicate 
that a white cross appeared, thereby motivating them to keep 
their eyes open throughout the experiment.

The experiment was hosted on the online platform Gorilla 
[24]. After providing demographic information, participants 
performed a headphone screening test based on dichotic pitch 
[25], designed to exclude participants not using headphones. 
After a short practice (8 total trials, with all the three levels of 
Appearance shown, as well as catch trials) which could be 
repeated until participants were sure that they understood the 
instructions, participants performed the main task. In the main 
task, participants were asked to indicate whether they heard a 
SW or WS word. Each trial started with two members of a 
minimal pair presented in written form to the participant: the 
SW word (e.g., CONtent) on the left, and the WS word on the 
right (e.g., conTENT). After a fixation cross lasting for 500 
milliseconds (ms), a video was shown to the participants. After 
the video, three response buttons were presented: the SW 
member on the left, the WS member on the right, and IK ZAG 
EEN WIT KRUIS! (“I SAW A WHITE CROSS!”, to respond to 
catch trials) below in the center. After clicking on one of the 
options or 4 seconds had passed, the next trial started after a 500 
ms blank screen. A total of 240 trials (approximately ~25 min) 
was presented in a randomized order. Every 48 trials, 
participants could take a break. After the main task, a debriefing 
questionnaire was presented. 
2.2.3. Pre-registered statistical analysis 
Perceived stress was analyzed using a generalized linear mixed 
model (GLMM), using the R package “lme4” [26]. Perceived 
stress was coded as follows: SW = 1, WS = 0. The analysis 
focused on trials with ambiguous acoustic information (i.e., 
experimental trials with a beat). The fixed effect structure 
included the factors Beat (BeatOn1, BeatOn2), treatment-coded 
with BeatOn1 as reference level, and Appearance (Human, 
Avatar, Disc), treatment-coded with Human as reference level. 
The random effect structure was determined using the 
“buildmer” R package [27] and included random intercepts by 
Item (i.e., the minimal pair) and by Participant, together with 
random slopes for Beat by Participant. P-values were obtained 
using ANOVA Type II Wald χ2 tests. 
2.2.4. Exploratory analyses 
Given the intermixed design it is possible to guess the disc-
gesture relationship. Therefore, non pre-registered exploratory 
analyses tested whether an effect of Beat in the disc condition 
could be observed in participants who guessed what the disc 
represented. Based on their response to the debriefing question 
“What do you think the dot/disc represented?”, 35 participants 
were considered to have guessed (henceforth, “guessing 
participants”) since their responses included one or more of the 
following words: hand (“hand”), arm (“arm”), klemtoon or 
nadruk 
(“emphasis”), 
cadens 
(“cadence”), 
toonhoogte 
(“pitch”), and intonatie (“intonation”). 
Two additional GLMER analyses were implemented: the 
first one including Disc Guessing as a between-subject factor, 
treatment-coded with “Naïve” as reference level, and the 
second one mirroring the pre-registered GLMER but analyzing 
data from the guessing participants only. The random-effect 
structures were determined using “buildmer” [27]. 
3. Results 
3.1. Pre-registered analysis 
We observed a significant main effect of Beat (χ2 = 61.166, p < 
.001) and a Beat × Appearance interaction (χ2 = 47.263, p < 
.001). No significant main effect of Appearance was observed 
(χ2 = 0.757, p = .685). These results suggest that the timing of 
beat gestures affected the perceived stress, but differently in 
human, avatar, and disc conditions (Figure 2). The model 
estimates revealed a significant Beat effect for human (β = -
1.092, SE = 0.120, z = -9.112, p < .001), a significant difference 
between human and disc for the BeatOn1 condition (β = -0.423, 
SE = 0.102, z = -4.133, p < .001), but not between human and 
avatar (β = -0.106, SE = 0.103, z = -1.023, p = .306). Finally, 
the effect of Beat was smaller in both the disc (β = 0.968, SE = 
0.145, z = 6.692, p < .001) and avatar (β = 0.295, SE = 0.145, z 
= 2.039, p = .041) conditions compared to the human condition.

post-hoc

(Bonferroni-

showed that the effect of Beat was significant in the human and 
avatar conditions (both p < .001), but not in the disc condition 
(p = 1). No significant difference was found for the BeatOn1 
condition between human and avatar (p = 1), while they both 
significantly differed from the disc (p < .001 and p = .016, 
respectively). No significant difference was found for the 
BeatOn2 condition between human and avatar (p = .554), while 
they both significantly differed from the disc (human: p < .001;

The exploratory analyses including the between-subject factor 
Disc Guessing revealed a main effect of Beat (χ2 = 61.945, p < 
.001) and a significant Beat × Appearance interaction (χ2 = 
46.915, p < .001), replicating the pre-registered analyses. 
Crucially, no significant Beat × Appearance × Disc Guessing 
(χ2 = 1.693, p = .429) was observed (Figure 3). 
In “guessing participants”, the disc condition resulted in a 
numerically larger proportion of SW responses for BeatOn1 
compared to BeatOn2 (Figure 3). This small numerical 
difference is in the direction of the manual McGurk effect [8].

corrected post-hoc comparisons, motivated by the significant

4. Discussion 
The present study tested whether the timing of beat gestures 
made by an avatar can bias lexical stress perception, as opposed 
to the movement of a simple disc. The results supported our pre-
registered hypotheses. First, the timing of an avatar’s beat 
gestures biased lexical stress perception, albeit slighly less than 
human beat gestures. Second, despite having the same 2D 
trajectory and temporal relationship with speech as a beat 
gesture, a moving disc did not bias lexical stress perception. 
The results from the human condition replicate previous 
online manual McGurk studies. Notably, the present study 
employed an adapted version of the so-called manual McGurk 
“mini-test” [10], which tests gesture and speech integration in 
approximately 10 minutes. The results demonstrate how the 
“mini-test” can be used not only in isolation as a short test of 
human gesture-speech integration, but also in combination with 
other experimental factors (e.g., Appearance) to test relevant 
hypotheses in the field of psycholinguistics, while keeping the 
experimental sessions to an acceptable duration. 
Our study demonstrates that the timing of beat gestures 
made by human-looking avatars can bias lexical stress 
perception. Accordingly, the present results might be integrated 
in modern applications of avatars, in particular by ensuring that 
the timing of beat gestures produced by virtual conversational 
agents aligns well with the prosody of their speech. The 
temporal alignment of gestures and prosody might be 
particularly important to boost speech intelligibility when 
virtual conversational agents interact with individuals with 
hearing difficulties (e.g., elderly population [28], or cochlear-
implant users1). Further applications can be found in the domain 
of virtual (second) language acquisition [15], where avatars’ 
beat gestures might support the decoding of lexical stress and 
facilitate the acquisition of a correct pronunciation [29]. 
The significant manual McGurk effect observed in the 
avatar condition suggests that previous work1 might have 
underestimated beat gesture-speech integration effects with 
virtual agents. It is possible that smooth and natural gesture 
trajectories, such as the ones made by the avatar here employed, 
might increase audiovisual integration effects. Furthermore, the 
present avatar’s lip movements might have reinforced the 
perception that the speech was indeed produced by the avatar. 
Parametric manipulations of these factors in future studies 
might reveal which features of the avatar’s communicative 
visual cues affect the size of audiovisual integration effects.  
Notably, the manual McGurk effect was smaller for the 
avatar than the human condition (BeatOn1 – BeatOn2: 0.16 vs. 
0.21). This reduction might be due to differences in the 
appearance of the avatar, such as its larger hands and its 
cartoonish style, or in its body proportions. Another possible 
explanation, worth exploring in future studies, relates to the 
facial expression and eye gaze of the avatar (Figure 1). The 
avatar’s videos were generated based on human videos in which 
the actor had a neutral face expression and fixed eye gaze (i.e., 
minimizing minimal eye movements). While our avatar was not 
perceived as strongly realistic or human-like (mean realism: 
0.37, measured from 0 to 1 in the debriefing questionnaire), it 
is possible that such a neutral expression, when mimicked by 
the avatar, might have resulted in a feeling of uneasiness, 
similar to the “uncanny valley” [30] effect. Alternatively, the 
reduced effect might also stem from the fact that the avatar and 
the human condition shared the same voice, and therefore 
participants might have perceived the avatar as an artificial 
condition, based on the human video, with less sense of agency. 
As predicted, a disc moving with the same 2D trajectory of 
a beat gesture did not affect lexical stress perception, not even 
in “guessing” participants. This might suggest that not all visual 
cues have affordances that allow them to affect communication 
(see also [21]). Through exposure, the human cognitive system 
might develop to selectively allow visual signals coming from 
a potential speaker, but not from non-human entities, to affect 
speech perception. Accordingly, temporal synchrony between 
speech and a visual signal might be necessary [31], but not 
sufficient, for audiovisual integration in communication, with 
the identity of the visual signal itself (i.e., potential speaker vs. 
impossible speaker) playing an important role. This hypothesis, 
possibly grounded in dissociations previously reported at the 
neural level [22], awaits further testing. It is important to note 
that the trajectory of the disc mimicked human beat gestures’ 
trajectories only in two dimensions, and not in depth. An 
alternative hypothesis for future studies is that 3D movement, 
which characterizes the perception of biological motion [32], is 
a key component underlying beat gesture-speech integration. 
On a final note, in the disc condition a bias towards perceiving 
a SW stress pattern emerged (Figures 2 and 3). As SW is the 
most frequent (88%) Dutch disyllabic stress pattern [20], this 
finding suggests that when the audiovisual signal is ambiguous 
(i.e., ambiguous speech and no human-like visual cue), internal 
heuristics based on frequency might drive speech perception. 
5. Conclusions 
The timing of beat gestures made by a virtual avatar 
successfully 
biased 
lexical 
stress 
perception. 
Future 
applications of avatar technology might exploit the alignment 
of beat gestures and prosody to increase speech intelligibility. 
Conversely, a moving disc failed to affect speech perception, 
possibly due to the lack of affordances that make listeners 
consider it as the source of speech. The reduced influence of 
non-human minimal visual cues on speech perception might 
stem from selective tuning of audiovisual integration in 
communication to signals made by human-like agents.
