Improving End-to-end Mixed-case ASR with
Knowledge Distillation and Integration of Voice Activity Cues
Sashi Novitasari, Takashi Fukuda, Gakuto Kurata
IBM Tokyo Research Lab, Tokyo, Japan
sashi.novitasari1@ibm.com,fukuda1@jp.ibm.com, gakuto@jp.ibm.com
Abstract
E2E mixed-case (MC) ASR is a more challenging task than
unicase (UC) ASR because of the necessity of capitalizing and
punctuating the decoded outputs simultaneously. MC models
that are simply trained on formatted transcriptions often suffer
from various negative impacts, notably a degradation in case-
and-punctuation-insensitive performance due to the increased
learning complexity.
In this paper, we describe novel tech-
niques for training E2E MC ASR models and use them to im-
prove casing-and-punctuation sensitive and insensitive perfor-
mance. Our approach incorporates knowledge distillation from
UC teacher to MC student models not only to improve capital-
ization and punctuation accuracy but also to maximize phone
classification capability in MC ASR. Furthermore, we attempt
to integrate voice activity cues into MC ASR to support text
formatting tasks. Our method significantly reduces errors by
up to 9.2% relative to baseline models that operate at a similar
decoding cost.
Index Terms:
End-to-end mixed-case ASR, capitalization,
punctuation, knowledge distillation, voice activity detection
1. Introduction
End-to-end (E2E) automatic speech recognition (ASR) frame-
works such as connectionist temporal classification (CTC) [1],
recurrent neural network transducer (RNN-T) [2, 3], and Con-
former [4, 5] have recently been used for directly transform-
ing speech audio into transcription text through a single model.
Those systems require transcribed speech data in order to train
the single all-neural models. Thus, given that data collection
covering multiple domains can be expensive, the limitation of
having paired speech-text data becomes profound when build-
ing systems that cover larger and more diverse acoustic and lan-
guage spaces. Since the majority of speech corpora for E2E
ASR model training are paired only with non-punctuated uni-
case (UC) text because of high annotation costs, one compro-
mise is to construct E2E ASR model as-is to output only unicase
text without punctuation marks at the sacrifice of readability.
Unfortunately, E2E ASR frameworks are often not as customiz-
able as hybrid systems [6,7] that can be modularly customized
for the output formats related to casing and punctuation marks.
To solve this problem, reformation of unicase decoded outputs
by using an external text processor has been investigated in the
literature [8–12]. While, in this case, the problems of capitaliza-
tion/punctuation and phone classification can be split, the two-
stage processing will increase the overall system complexity be-
cause more modules are involved in the system, which is often
not preferable.
On the other hand, E2E mixed-case (MC) ASR directly de-
codes input speech into capitalized text with punctuation marks
by training the model using speech data paired with truecased-
punctuated transcriptions. Recently, studies that explicitly ad-
dress the capitalization and punctuation tasks for E2E ASR
[13–18] have been gaining attention for demands for practical
systems. For instance, several studies have showcased frame-
works that directly produce unicase and mixed-case texts by
training a model using both formatted and unformatted speech
transcription data [15,16,19]. They demonstrated the possibil-
ity of improving MC ASR performance with a limited amount
of training data. In another approach, a UC ASR model was
extended with additional output layers for the casing and punc-
tuation label classifications, in which the unicase text output is
reformatted on the basis of those labels [14,17,18]. Large-scale
ASR models, such as Whisper [20], have also been receiving
attention for their text formatting capability, although they may
incur accuracy-cost trade-offs.
Despite the MC ASR framework’s advantage in simplifying
the system, it might have lower phone classification accuracy
in comparison with UC ASR [13, 21] because of the increased
complexity of the model training, a phenomenon that was also
observed in our study. Unlike a UC ASR model that models
only a single written form of a word, an MC ASR model needs
to not only identify words acoustically but also model several
graphical forms of an utterance with different casing and punc-
tuation marks; implying a larger search space than that of UC
ASR. Capitalization and punctuation tasks are difficult in vari-
ous aspects because there are no explicit sounds uttered by hu-
mans that correspond to them, which causes the learning com-
plexity in the ASR models to increase. In particular, these prob-
lems appear in systems designed with the greedy decoding and
situations where no external language model is available.
In this paper, we propose training techniques for E2E MC
ASR to enhance both casing-and-punctuation sensitive and in-
sensitive word accuracy, of which the latter has been rarely an-
alyzed through a comparison with UC ASR [14, 15, 18] de-
spite the aforementioned necessity.
By using the case-and-
punctuation-insensitive metric, we examined details of the per-
formance of word classification itself that might be less notice-
able in case-and-punctuation-sensitive metrics, such as missing
words, because casing or punctuation-related errors often pre-
dominate all others. In this paper, we only dealt with construct-
ing MC models with a similar structure as UC models or with
minor changes for decoding to maintain the overall system com-
plexity.
We propose two approaches to achieve our goal.
First,
we construct an E2E MC system with a knowledge distillation
(KD) training procedure [22] to transfer discriminative acous-
tic knowledge represented in a UC model (as a teacher) into an
MC model (as a student) and improve the case-and-punctuation
insensitive performance in MC ASR. Since the UC model has

To enhance the capitalization and punctuation accuracy, the
second approach extends our method for embedding the E2E
MC ASR framework with voice activity cues obtained from
a voice activity detection (VAD) system [23–27]. ASR sys-
tems are commonly paired with a VAD system that removes the
non-speech parts to reduce computational costs while decod-
ing. Since the VAD can distinguish the voiced and non-voiced
audio frames by estimating their likelihoods, we hypothesize
that feeding this likelihood information into the MC model will
also be useful for predicting the punctuation marks and casing.
In practice, if the ASR has been paired with a standalone VAD
system, the overall proposed system complexity will be similar
to that of the standard systems without the VAD embedding. In
summary, our contributions are as follows.
1. We utilize KD to construct an MC model from a UC model to
improve the case-and-punctuation insensitive performance.
2. We integrate VAD-based information into the MC model to
leverage acoustic clues for accurate punctuation and casing.
3. We demonstrate E2E MC ASR that leverages both KD and
VAD information in training and show that it performs ro-
bustly in inference with or without VAD information.
2. Proposed Modeling Technique
2.1. Baseline fully-supervised multitask MC ASR (MCS)
In prior studies [15, 16], E2E MC ASR systems were designed
to directly produce mixed-case text as their main task along with
unicase text as an auxiliary task through a fully-supervised mul-
titask training approach. For convenience, we will refer to this
framework as the fully-supervised multitask mixed-case (MCS)
ASR. Given an acoustic feature sequence X ∈RI×A, an MCS
ASR model decodes it into an mixed-case text YMC ∈RJmc that
has punctuation marks and a unicase text YUC ∈RJuc through
separated output layers, where I is the length of the acoustic-
features sequence, A is the acoustic feature’s dimension, and
Jmc and Juc are the lengths of the corresponding texts. YUC vo-
cabulary consists only of lowercase (or uppercase) characters,
while YMC vocabulary contains lowercase characters, uppercase
characters, and punctuation marks. The MCS model is opti-
mized through a combined loss LMCS based on the reference
transcriptions in the corresponding formats:
LMCS = λLMC + αLUC.
(1)
In our experiments, we used the non-autoregressive Conformer
ASR framework [4] with a CTC objective [1] as our base
framework.
Thus, LMC = −logPCTC(YMC|X) and LUC =
−logPCTC(YUC|X) are, respectively, the MC output supervised
loss and the UC output supervised loss based on a CTC loss for-
mulation, while λ and α are coefficients for the respective loss
terms. On the basis of our preliminary experiments, we decided
to attach the auxiliary output layer for the UC task to the top
of the main output for MC task, which it also the basis for the
proposed frameworks. In inference phase, the auxiliary layer
is removed, therefore, MCS ASR computational complexity is
similar to that of UC ASR model.
2.2. MC ASR with knowledge distillation (MCKD)
We propose a training framework for an MC ASR system with
knowledge distillation (MCKD) from a teacher UC model. In
this paper, the UC and MC models are each based on a non-
autoregressive Conformer-CTC model. The architecture is the
same for both models except the output layer. During training,
the proposed MCKD (Fig. 1) employs a multitask structure that
is optimized on two tasks simultaneously: 1) punctuated and
capitalized MC speech recognition as the main task, and 2) UC
speech recognition as the auxiliary task. MCKD ASR’s output
layer is extended with an auxiliary output branch, where the
KD loss is calculated between this auxiliary UC output and the
teacher UC model output and it back-propagates through the
entire MCKD network. After the training, the auxiliary layer is
removed for inference; therefore, the complexity of the MCKD
ASR model remains unchanged from the UC ASR model.
The proposed model training process involves the KD loss
and supervised loss. The KD loss (LKD.UC) is calculated be-
tween the unicase soft outputs from the UC ASR model ( ˆY T
UC)
and the MCKD auxiliary unicase output ( ˆY S
UC):
LKD.UC = KD Loss(PCTC( ˆY T
UC|X), PCTC( ˆY S
UC|X)).
(2)
In our preliminary experiments, we explored a mean-square er-
ror (MSE) function and the Kullback–Leibler divergence for-
mulation for our KD loss function. We update the model by
using the KD loss combined with the supervised ASR loss for
MC text (LMC):
LMCKD = λLMC + βLKD.UC,
(3)
where λ and β are coefficients for the corresponding loss terms.
LMC is calculated in a supervised manner based on the CTC
formulation, similarly to the baseline MCS.
2.3. MCKD with voice activity cues (MCKD-VAD)
We further extend the proposed MCKD training framework
with the VAD information integration mechanism (MCKD-
VAD), as shown in Fig. 1. VAD module is commonly used
to classify each signal frame in an audio stream as to its voice
activity type (e.g. speech, silence), which it separately works
as an external module to ASR system. The VAD output is then
utilized to determine the start and end points of audio segments
with speech, where the detected segments along with some ad-
ditional onset and offset frames are to be passed to ASR. Even
in a speech segment determined by the VAD model, the likeli-
hoods of the speech frames are much different from each other
because of consonants, vowels, lip noise, and so on. Thus, we
assume that this information is useful as an auxiliary compo-
nent for phone classification, casing, and the punctuation task
in MC ASR.
3335

The proposed method incorporates a standalone neural
VAD model [25] into the ASR model training framework and/or
for inference of ASR decoding. VAD integration begins with
a voice activity prediction by the VAD model associated with
input speech feature sequence X. The output from the VAD
model before the softmax is then projected to the same dimen-
sion as that of the ASR Conformer layer’s, which can be formu-
lated as:
XVAD = FNN(VAD(X)),
(4)
where XVAD ∈RI×D is the projected VAD output and D is the
dimension of the Conformer layer. XVAD is then integrated into
the intermediate Conformer layer inside the ASR model. Inte-
grating additional low-level acoustic features into the interme-
diate layer is intended to make the model effectively take new
information without hindering the original internal log-Mel fea-
tures and leverage them together in the deeper layers. A similar
idea can be also applied to other types of neural network frame-
work, i.e., it is not limited to the Conformer. Suppose that the
Conformer network consists of l layers, we add the VAD infor-
mation XVAD to the l/2-th Conformer layer output (Xl/2):
Xl/2.final = Xl/2 + γXVAD,
(5)
where Xl/2.final is the final output of the intermediate Conformer
layer that will be passed to the next Conformer layer, and γ is
the coefficient for XVAD. The proposed MCKD-VAD model is
optimized with the same objective as the MCKD model (Eq.
3). We freeze the VAD model during the MCKD-VAD training
and update only the projection layer for VAD and the model
components for ASR.
We examine a MCKD-VAD model that performs robustly
with or without integrating the VAD information XVAD into the
Conformer during inference. This enables the proposed system
to be also used in the same inference setting as the UC ASR sys-
tem. In our setting, we use γ=1 for half of the training batches,
denoting that VAD information is applied, and γ=0 for the other
half, denoting that the VAD information is not applied, in turns.
3. Experiment Settings
3.1. Data
In our experiments, we consider realistic ASR conditions such
as the telephony and meeting conversations, where MC tasks
might be more challenging. Our ASR models were trained us-
ing in-house English speech-text data that mainly consisted of
call-center conversations between multiple speakers, including
males and females, recorded under multi-acoustic conditions.
The teacher UC model was trained with more than 10K hours
of those data, while the student MC model was trained on more
than 5K hours of speech data, which was a subset of the UC
model training data, with the capitalized and punctuated tran-
scriptions. The VAD model was trained using 2K hours of in-
house audio data consisting of spontaneous speech, read speech,
and non-speech (noise) audio. All of the audio data were sam-
pled at 8 kHz, from which we extracted log-Mel speech features
with a window size of 10 ms and augmented them with delta and
double delta coefficients. Every two consecutive frames were
stacked followed by alternate frame skipping, which resulted in
a 240-dimensional feature vector every 20 ms. The output vo-
cabulary of the UC model was composed of 42 characters that
included lowercase characters, digits, apostrophe mark, whites-
pace mark, and blank mark for CTC. The output vocabulary
from the MC model consisted of 97 characters after adding the
uppercased characters and the punctuation marks into the UC
model vocabulary.
We tested our model on in-house and also publicly avail-
able datasets. The in-house test data consisted of 1 hour of
real call-center conversations between multiple English speak-
ers, including males and females, recorded under clean con-
ditions (CC-clean). Additionally, we tested our model on 1.5
hours of call-center and meeting recordings that contained the
environmental noises (CCM-noisy) and recorded from mixed-
accent English speakers, including males and females. We also
evaluated our model on Fisher LDC2004S13 [28], which is
composed of telephony conversation audio and the correspond-
ing truecased-punctuated transcription. We focused on utter-
ances longer than 60 secs, for which capitalizations and punctu-
ations would be practically more meaningful in the correspond-
ing transcriptions.
3.2. Model configuration
The UC and MC models in our experiments had similar
Conformer-CTC architecture. The encoder was composed of an
FNN with 768 cells and ten Conformer blocks. Each block had
two FNN modules in which the multi-head self-attention mod-
ule and the convolution module were sandwiched. The multi-
head self-attention module had dhead=8 and datt=96 for the num-
ber of heads and the dimensions of the attention module, and
the convolution kernel size was 31. The last Conformer block
was followed by an FNN output layer. The output layer size was
42 for the UC model and 97 for the MC model. In the baseline
MCS and proposed model, the MC output layer was followed
by an auxiliary output layer for the UC task. All the models had
roughly 155M trainable parameters. We used a learning rate of
5e-5 and a batch size of 32 for the ASR training. The base-
line MC ASR and the proposed MCKD ASR model parameters
were initialized from an UC ASR model, which it was also used
as the teacher for the proposed MCKD and MCKD-VAD ASR.
The proposed MCKD-VAD model parameters were initialized
from the MCKD model parameters. Additionally, we applied
several regularization techniques such as sequence noise injec-
tion [29] and SpecAugment [30] while training both the base-
line and proposed models. In MCKD-VAD training, SpecAug-
ment was applied to the teacher only for the use case in noisy
situations. The baseline MCS model used α=0.2, while MCKD
ASR used β=0.2 and MCKD-VAD used β=0.4, our best set-
ting. All the models were fixed to λ=1. For the MCKD-VAD
model, we used a speech frame-level VAD [25] that had four
CNN layers and classified the input frames into “speech,” “non-
speech,” and “music” classes. The VAD output was processed
through three FNN layers before it was integrated into the ASR.
No external language model was used in any ASR inference.
3.3. Evaluation metric
Our models were evaluated on the basis of the word error rate
(WER) in several text formats [31].
We denote WERcp as
the case-and-punctuation-sensitive WER, where the ASR out-
put was evaluated against the truecased-punctuated reference
text. Here, the same word in the reference and ASR output but
with different casing or punctuation was regarded as a substitu-
tion error. WERuncp denotes a case-and-punctuation-insensitive
WER, where the reference and ASR output text were all lower-
cased and the punctuation marks were removed. We also calcu-
lated the case-sensitive WER (WERc) by removing the punctu-
ation marks from the reference and ASR output while keeping
the casings. To evaluate the punctuation, we use a WER based
on punctuation (WERp) that was calculated by replacing all of
the alphanumeric words in the reference and ASR text with a
uniform token while keeping the punctuation unchanged. For

4. Results and Discussion
4.1. Baseline ASR
Our baselines were the UC ASR model teacher that taught
the proposed models, the single-task MC ASR model that op-
timized only based on MC texts supervisedly, and the fully-
supervised multitask MC (MCS) ASR model optimized based
on Eq. 1. As seen in Table 1, the single-task MC and MCS
had better WERcp, WERc and WERp than those of UC ASR,
but their WERuncp were degraded, which implies they produced
more incorrect words regardless the format. Our observations
indicated that the deteriorated WERuncp was mainly caused by
the increased number of substitution and deletion errors, affect-
ing both the originally plain words and the cased or punctu-
ated words. Since the learning complexity of MC-based ASR
increased when learning multiple variants of text form associ-
ated with phones, the MC-based models were more difficult to
converge in terms of the case-and-punctuation-insensitive as-
pect compared with the UC ASR model. For the experiments
reported in the next subsection, we focus on MCS as our best
MC ASR baseline.
4.2. Proposed MCKD ASR
Table 1 shows the performance of the proposed MCKD model
on the clean call center (CC-clean) audio. Initially, we experi-
mented on an “MCKD+MCS” model that applied both the su-
pervised and KD losses on the auxiliary UC task optimization.
Although we observed an improvement over the baseline MCS
model, better results were produced when using only the KD
loss for the UC task, as the learning objective was simpler.
Our best MCKD model reduced WERcp from the 28.7% of
MCS model to 27.7% while it also improved WERuncp by 8.4%
relative to that of the baseline MCS model and achieved a com-
parable WERuncp to that of the UC model. These results show
that the auxiliary UC task optimization with KD was effective,
because it allowed the student network to learn the word clas-
sification knowledge that had been correlated with the acous-
tic information through the teacher network, which it might be
more beneficial for the convergence of the MC model.

4.3. Proposed MCKD-VAD ASR
We carried out the MCKD-VAD ASR inference with and with-
out VAD information integration. The inference with VAD in-
formation integration followed the mechanism described in Sec-
tion 2.3, while the inference without VAD integration had the
same mechanism as those of UC, MCS, and MCKD ASR.
As shown in Table 2, on CC-clean, the proposed MCKD-
VAD model performed robustly in terms of inferences with and
without the VAD integration; it outperformed the baseline MCS
and had a similar WERcp and WERuncp to the MCKD ASR when
VAD information was not used for inference. Higher gain was
observed when VAD features were used during ASR inference,
where a relative reduction in WERcp and WERuncp of 5.6% and
9.2 % over the baseline MCS model, respectively, was achieved.
Compared with the MCKD ASR model, MCKD-VAD ASR
model’s gain was more noticeable in terms of WERp. We found
that punctuation marks such as commas and periods could be
associated with voice activity, such as short pauses, in the spo-
ken utterances and that this led to a better punctuation recog-
nition and in turn better casings. With the proposed regulated
MCKD-VAD model training mechanism, our model maintained
that capability even in the VAD information-less inference.
We additionally experimented on the CCM-noisy (Table 3)
and the Fisher test sets (Table 4). The MCKD ASR and MCKD-
VAD ASR models outperformed the baselines. Moreover, the
MCKD-VAD ASR model, which used the VAD features for in-
ference, outperformed the UC ASR WERuncp on CCM-noisy.
This indicates that VAD information was particularly helpful in
acoustically more challenging situations, as the likelihood for
voiced/non-voiced speech parts might prompt the ASR system’s
attention to process those parts more carefully in noises.
5. Conclusion
We proposed a model training algorithm for E2E MC ASR
with KD and a VAD cues integration mechanism to improve
ASR’s case-and-punctuation sensitive and insensitive perfor-
mance.
Our experimental results showed that the proposed
KD from a UC model to a MC model improved the model’s
case-and-punctuation insensitive performance, while the VAD
information resulted in a case-and-punctuation sensitive perfor-
mance gain. As for future work, we are interested in exploring
other teacher structures for KD and different additional infor-
mation for the MC network.
