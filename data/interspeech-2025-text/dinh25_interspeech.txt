Extended Loss: Incorporating Long Context into Training Models when using
Short Audio Frames
Quang Minh Dinh∗1, Hoda Rezaee Kaviani2, Mehrdad Hosseinzadeh2, Yuanhao Yu2
1Simon Fraser University, Canada
2Noah’s Ark Lab, Huawei Technologies Canada Co., Ltd., Canada

Abstract
Recently deep learning solutions have been successfully ap-
plied to many signal processing tasks including acoustic echo
cancellation (AEC). Most existing works focus on architecture
design and ignore practical issues such as the effect of frame
length on the performance of end-to-end AEC models. In real-
time applications, frame length can be as small as 10ms. Since
the observed context is very limited during training, it results in
boundary discontinuities (glitches) in the final output. While us-
ing long frames or post-processing can help, it adds extra delay
which may not be desirable depending on the application. In
this paper, we investigate the practical issue of handling short
frames for AEC, and propose an efficient remedy. By keeping
the long context information in each batch and using it during
loss calculation, we compensate for the short frames. Our solu-
tion is model-agnostic and does not affect the inference time.
Index Terms: Echo cancellation, signal processing, neural net-
works, speech enhancement, real-time processing
1. Introduction
In recent years, the popularity and demand for teleconferencing
systems have increased significantly. The existence of acous-
tic echos is one of the major sources of poor speech qual-
ity ratings in voice and video calls.
Acoustic Echo Cancel-
lation (AEC) aims to recover clean near-end audio, given the
noisy and far-end signals. Early methods for this task employ
different digital signal processing (DSP) techniques; however,
these methods do not perform well when model assumptions
are violated, e.g., non-linearities in the processing chain, or fast
time-varying acoustic conditions [1]. With the advent of deep
learning techniques, many supervised acoustic echo cancella-
tion (AEC) techniques have been proposed and outperformed
their traditional counterparts. These models either directly op-
erate on time [2, 3, 4] or frequency domain [5, 6, 7].
Amongst the most recent methods, CRUSE [5], Align-
CRUSE [6] and DeepVQE [7] belong to a family of residual
CNN auto-encoder with a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) bottle-
neck models that perform especially well on different speech
enhancement tasks, including echo cancellation. Another ar-
chitecture successfully applied to AEC tasks is Conformer [8],
which combines the power of CNNs and Transformers. [9] and
[10] are conformer-based models trained with an ASR loss and
an auxiliary loss, and are highly effective on the AEC task.
In the design of AEC solutions, it is common to divide
the input signal into multiple fixed-length frames and process
them independently by the network. During training, regard-
less of their domain or architecture, all of these methods cal-

culate loss function on a single frame. Processed frames are
later concatenated to form the final output. One major problem
with this approach is that the lack of contextual information be-
tween adjacent frames during training results in boundary dis-
continuity (glitches) in the output. In the context of speech en-
hancement, [11] discusses the same issue and proposes a way of
handling short frames for a specific waveform-based speech en-
hancement model by adding a recurrent neural network (RNN)
module to explicitly model the relationship between adjacent
frames. Other solutions like applying the overlap-add method
[12] or predicting the current frame given the surrounding con-
text [13] have been proposed which result in additional delay.
In this paper, we study the influence of frame length on the
performance of end-to-end deep learning-based AEC systems
and propose a solution to handle the problem of lack of context
when training models on short audio frames. Instead of rely-
ing on post-processing, or the existence of neighboring frames
during inference, we keep the long context information in each
batch during training and use it during loss calculation. The
existence of this information allows us to compensate for short
audio frames during training. For the experiments we chose two
of the most recent AEC architectures, CRUSE [5] and Deep-
VQE [7] , which is the state-of-the-art on AEC task, and imple-
mented our Conformer based AEC model. To the best of our
knowledge, this practical issue has not been addressed in de-
signing AEC systems. We show that our proposed solution is
architecture-independent and can work with various loss func-
tions. The contributions of our work can be summarized as fol-
lows:
• We study the practical issue of handling short frames in AEC
tasks and propose an efficient solution to address this prob-
lem.
• Our method is model-agnostic. To support this claim, we ex-
plore a few of the most recent architectures (including SOTA)
and loss functions and compare the results.
• Proposed method achieves better results without adding extra
complexity to the model, or affecting the inference time.

tally transmitted to the other side, played through a loudspeaker
at near-end (depending on the scenario, a near-end signal can
also add to the mix simultaneously. The mixed signal can con-
tain the background noise as well). The mixed signal at the
near-end is then transmitted back to the far-end side leading to
the far-end hearing the ”echo” of their own signal.

signal to happen: 1) far-end single talk (FEST): only the far-end
speaker is talking and the near-end is quiet. 2) near-end single

2.1. Input Features
We first sample a batch of size b from of the far-end and micro-
phone signals at a pre-defined sample rate. Let F ∈Rb×1×n
and N ∈Rb×1×n where n is the number of samples to be the
far-end and microphone signals, respectively. We sample all
audio signals in a mono fashion, hence F and N both have 1
channel. Next, the raw sampled audios are converted to a spec-
trum feature space. Following other works in the literature [7, 6]
we also use Short-time Fourier Transform (STFT) to project the
sample audio waveforms to the feature space:

2.2. Model Architecture
Since our solution is model-agnostic, we use a variety of mod-
els with different numbers of parameters (from 0.6M to 12M)
to showcase the effectiveness of our proposed loss function.
The de facto standard model design choice for the AEC task
is mask-based architecture. In this approach, the network takes
the near-end and far-end signals as input, either through sep-
arate branches or stacked together, and predicts a mask. The
predicted mask is then applied to the real and complex part of
N via simple multiplication to get the echo-free output, O:

for the mask-based approaches is that (ideally) the mask modi-
fies the component of the near-end features that are responsible
for the existing echo in the signal. After applying the mask on
the microphone features, a corresponding inverse transforma-
tion (e.g. inverse STFT) generates the waveform for the clean
signal.
Specifically, we use CRUSE [14], DeepVQE [7], and Con-
former [8] models as our backbone AEC model.
For each
model, we aim to modify as little as possible to make them suit-
able for the task of acoustic echo cancellation. Since DeepVQE
was originally designed for the AEC task, we didn’t modify the
architecture. As for CRUSE and Conformer models, since they
were originally proposed for the task of speech enhancement
and noise cancellation, modification is needed to adapt them for
the task of AEC. Therefore, we stack the near- and far-end sig-
nals together and feed them as a single input to the model.

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets
Our training dataset is synthesized on-the-fly using DNS Chal-
lenge [17] dataset with random parameters for each sample
(e.g., signal-to-noise ratio, distortion, signal-to-echo ratio). To
generate training samples, we randomly select clean speech au-
dios, level normalize them, and concatenate them to the desired
duration. For both near and far-end, the clean speeches are sep-
arated by 500ms to 2s silence segments. Noise segments are
either sliced or repeated to the target duration. Echo is syn-
thesized by convolving the far-end speech signal with a ran-
dom small room RIR, before being mixed with the near-end
speech signal with a signal-to-echo ratio (SER) between 5 and
25. Noise is then added to the echo mixture with a signal-to-
noise ratio (SNR) uniformly selected between 5 and 10. We
re-scale the final mixture to a random target level between -35
and -15 dBFS.
We use the globally aligned version of the real recordings
from the blind test set in the AEC Challenge 2023 [1] to evaluate
our models, as delay is not the main concern of this work.
3.2. Evaluation Metrics
For both DT and FEST scenarios, we report the AECMOS score
[18] which is a neural network-based perceptual speech quality
metric that is highly correlated with the human mean-opinion-
score (MOS). For FEST, we also report ERLE, which is only
appropriate if measured in a quiet room without background
noises. For NEST, we report the AECMOS degradation score
[18], (which is correlated to the call echo degradation quality),
DNSMOS P.835 signal (SIG), background (BAK), and over-
all (OVRL) scores. We also employ the AECMOS degradation
score for DT in addition to the AECMOS echo.
3.3. Implementation Details
All audios are sampled at 16kHz. For feature generation, we
use a squared root Hann window of length 32ms, a hop length
of 16ms, and a discrete Fourier transform length of 512. Fol-
lowing [5], for all CRUSE experiments we use a squared root
Hann window of length 20ms, a hop length of 10ms and a dis-
crete Fourier transform length of 320. We use the AdamW op-
timizer with LR = 1.5 · 10−4 and a weight decay of 5 · 10−6.
Experiments for extended setup are with 5 frames unless stated
otherwise.

To show the effectiveness of our method, we trained the
same model on short audios with two extended setups: 3-frames
(row 2), and 5-frames (row 3). Both of these extended setups
outperform the single-frame model (row 1) across all metrics.
One interesting observation is that our 5-frame extended model
outperforms row 4 in FEST. It is worth noting that the difference

In Figure 2 we show samples of enhanced spectrograms
on the same DT signal, with 3 different models. All models
have the same architecture, and the inference duration is short
(368ms) for all models. The first and second rows (from top
to bottom) correspond to the far-end and microphone signals,
respectively. The 3rd row is enhanced by a model trained on
short audios without extended loss, and row 4 is the same ar-
chitecture, trained on short audios with our proposed extended
loss. Between the two, our method performs significantly better
in removing echo and noise. This is specifically more visible
in the first half of the signal. The last row, is the same archi-
tecture trained on long 10s audios, while inference is 368ms.
This model performs better than row 3, but still has some left-
over echos and noise (more visible in the first half of the signal),
compared with our results in row 4. This is consistent with our
observation that changing the inference time, affects the quality
of final audio.
To show our solution is architecture-independent, we
trained other models (CRUSE, DeepVQE, and DeepVQE-S),
with and without our extended setup. The results are shown
in Table 2. We observe that all of the extended setups outper-
form their corresponding single-frame version. For DeepVQE-
S, we experimented on both long and short frames. Using the
extended setup improves the results for both scenarios.
For
all experiments, we used complex compressed MSE loss, ex-
cept for Conformers, where we used a weighted combination
of complex compressed MSE, asymmetric loss, and SNR loss
on the active areas of the speech. It can be seen that the com-
bination of Conformer with the new loss already outperforms
many of the other experiments. What is more interesting is that
our extended setup on this model boosts the results even further.
The last row of this table shows our best model, performing ex-
ceedingly well on all metrics, especially ERLE for FEST, and
significantly outperforming other SOTA architectures.
4. Discussion and Conclusions
In this paper, we investigated the practical issue of handling
short frames and its effect on the performance of deep learning-
based AEC systems. While using longer frames or adding a
post-processing step can be an option in some cases, this may
not be a solution for real-time applications due to latency and
complexity. However, the lack of context associated with a short
frame hurts the training of the model. We proposed a solution to
handle the lack of context during training without adding extra
complexity or delay to inference by utilizing intra-batch context
information in the loss function during training. This allows us
to compensate for the short length of each frame during training.
Our solution is model-agnostic and does not affect on inference
time.
