Speech stimulus design to study the neural coding of speech and the impact of
cochlear synaptopathy

1Lyon Neuroscience Research Center, Universit´e Lyon 1, CNRS UMR5292, Inserm U1028, France

Abstract
Since auditory neural coding mechanisms have been uncovered
using simple artificial stimuli, the nature of their involvement
in speech perception has remained unclear.
However, using
analysis-resynthesis techniques, it is possible to design natu-
ralistic speech stimuli that present the same appeal as artificial
tones in terms of precise control and parametrisation. Here,
speech stimuli were designed to assess the involvement of a
specific coding mechanism: the coding of temporal fine struc-
ture through phase-locking. This mechanism is supposed to be
impaired in cochlear synaptopathy, a form of hearing loss that
remains undetected through pure tone audiometry. The design
process described here seeks to accommodate all the constraints
that can arise from large multi-centric studies covering different
species, methodologies, and languages.
Index Terms: speech analysis-resynthesis, hearing loss, neural
coding
1. Introduction
Speech perception relies on the decoding of acoustic informa-
tion into phonetic categories that are then stringed into words,
themselves sequenced into sentences [1]. This process has been
tied to specific neural mechanisms that are responsible for en-
coding the temporal fine structure (TFS) of auditory stimuli [2].
TFS encoding is thought to enable precise coding of frequency
information through the phase-locked firing of the auditory neu-
rons. Consequently, TFS coding is limited by the phase-locking
abilities of the auditory nerve, and only happens at frequencies
low enough for synchronised firing to occur [3]. TFS coding
abilities are hindered in case of cochlear synaptopathy (CS),
a condition that reduces the number of neural fibres that can
fire synchronously. CS is largely prevalent in older adults and
is also known as “hidden hearing-loss” since it does not affect
pure tone audiometry, which is the standard clinical diagnostic
tool [4, 5]. It is believed to result in a degradation of speech
intelligibility in noisy environments [6]. However, its hidden
nature makes it difficult to diagnose in humans, and although
the consequences of CS on the general coding of sounds has be
documented in animals, the direct link between the neural disor-
der and the speech-in-noise perception deficit remains elusive.
To better understand the interplay between CS, the neu-
ral coding of TFS, and speech perception, a unified approach
across humans and animals models need to be adopted. Be-
havioural accounts of speech perception can be obtained from
human participants, as well as compound electrophysiological
data (EEG, such as auditory brainstem responses, ABR) [7].
Animal models allow the same compound electrophysiological
recordings, but also allow single unit recordings that offer a di-
rect view of the degree of neural synchronisation with the TFS
(phase-locking) that the observed neuron is able to provide [8].
Computational modelling can then be used to combine the dif-
ferent approaches across the different species [9, 10]. However,
for these data to remain comparable, it is crucial that the same
stimuli are used in all the phases of such a project. Further-
more, using a simple discrimination task facilitates modelling
and links to physiological recordings. Here, we describe how
such stimuli were designed and produced, the constraints they
had to satisfy, and how they could be generated in a flexible
way.
2. Constraints
The design constraints are separated into two categories: intrin-
sic requirements that are directly induced by the purpose under-
lying the creation of the stimuli; implementation, or operational
requirements that are imposed by the specific tasks and methods
that will be used.
2.1. Intrinsic requirements
2.1.1. Ecological validity
To be generalisable to speech perception at large, the stimuli
have to capture some of the essential traits of speech. To satisfy
this requirement it seems obvious that (a) the stimuli must con-
sist of, or be as close as possible to, real speech excerpts. This
also means the speech stimuli must (b) pertain to the language
of the (human) listeners involved in the experiments.
2.1.2. Sensitivity to cochlear synaptopathy
Because we are interested in the effects of CS, we want to fo-
cus on speech features that are potentially sensitive to variation
in TFS processing abilities. In other words, we are interested
in speech contrasts that either do or do not involve TFS pro-
cessing. It is assumed that TFS coding requires neural phase-
locking, which is limited in frequency. There is much debate
about what the upper frequency limit of phase-locking may be
[3], with proposed values between 1.5 and 10 kHz. Based on
this review, it can be speculated that TFS would be optimally
coded below 1.5 kHz, but that coding would progressively de-
teriorate above this frequency, and eventually entirely vanish
above 10 kHz. Therefore, for our current purpose, we have cho-
sen a TFS processing limit of 1.5 kHz, meaning that frequencies
below this limit may be coded using TFS mechanisms, while
frequencies above this limit are not guaranteed to be coded this
way. Note that the frequency importance function for speech
information peaks around 1.5 kHz [11], indicating that frequen-
cies on both sides of this limit are likely to contribute compara-
bly to intelligibility.

2.1.3. Steady-state and transient
Lorenzi et al. [2] linked TFS perception abilities to speech per-
ception using an identification task for stimuli in the form /aCa/
where C is a consonant. Unlike vowels that tend to be steady-
state, consonants are transient sounds where the spectral con-
tent is changing rapidly. This raises the possibility that TFS
might be specifically involved in the perception of transients.
However, neural responses to non-stationary sounds are more
difficult to analyse than those to steady-state sounds for which
long-term spectra can be more easily interpreted. As a result, it
is desirable to create both steady-state and transient stimuli to
cover both aspects.
2.2. Operational requirements
2.2.1. Duration of stimuli
Natural speech mostly consists of rather long utterances like
sentences that have a duration in the order of seconds. How-
ever, ABR measurements require a large number of repetitions
[12]. For this reason, it is usually performed with very short
stimuli, < 100 ms. Similarly, single unit recordings – which
record the activity of single auditory neurons – are performed on
live, anaesthetised animals using a procedure that is time bound
[8]. Therefore, this method also benefits from using short stim-
uli. Regarding the behavioural task in humans, it would also
require a fair number of repetitions to be relatable to other mea-
surements. In addition, the test is likely to be embedded into a
much larger test battery in order to provide complete audiolog-
ical assessment of the participant. As a result, it is also prefer-
able to keep the behavioural test as short as possible, which also
points towards shorter stimuli.
2.2.2. Continuum of stimuli
The stimuli are to be organised in pairs contrasting specific
phonemic contrasts. Behavioural tasks could then simply be
identification or categorisation of the phoneme. However, to
ensure that the task difficulty can be adjusted, it seems desir-
able to have a continuum of stimuli ranging from one end of
the contrast to the other. This will allow pre-testing to select
pairs of items that have a pre-determined level of behavioural
discriminability. Furthermore, in studies involving participants
with impaired hearing, it is common to be faced with the issue
that while the normally hearing group is at ceiling, the group
with hearing loss is at floor performance. To keep comparisons
meaningful and interpretable, it is recommended to use adapted
levels of difficulty for the two groups. Continua of stimuli will
make it possible to select pairs of items that are suitable for
listeners with various listening abilities without the concern of
being stuck at floor or ceiling performance.
Regarding the physiological recordings, it also desirable to
obtain continua of stimuli. Indeed, when comparing only two
stimuli in a stochastic process like neural activity, it is difficult
to reliably relate observed differences in activity to the stim-
uli themselves. With a continuum, more powerful regression
analyses can be used to relate the continuous change in neural
activity to the continuous change in acoustic information in the
stimulus.

3. Implementation
3.1. Phonemic contrasts
Two constraints apply to the choice of phonemic contrasts:
2.1.1.b implies that the considered phonemes have to belong
to the inventory of the language of the human participants con-
sidered for this material. In our case, participants could be Ger-
man, Dutch or French. We thus looked for phonemic contrasts
that were common to these three languages, and that also satis-
fied 2.1.2 and 2.1.3.
To satisfy 2.1.2, we selected phonemic contrasts that are
generally realised through acoustic traits that are either below or
above the chosen 1.5 kHz limit. To satisfy 2.2.1 (keep stimuli
short), we selected single vowels for the steady-state stimuli,
and consonant-vowel (CV) syllables for the transient stimuli.
3.1.1. Steady-state stimuli: vowels
We reviewed the vowel inventories of German, French and
Dutch as listed by the International Phonetic Association [13].
First, when superimposing the canonical vocalic triangles, it ap-
pears that there is some overlap between languages (Figure 1A).

3.1.2. Transient stimuli: syllables
Although Lorenzi et al. [2] used speech tokens of the form
vowel-consonant-vowel, ABR recordings have been popular-
ized with shorter CV syllables [12] which we also adopted here
to satisfy 2.2.1.
Again, examining the consonant inventories of German,
French and Dutch, there is considerable overlap. Delattre et
al. [14] described schematic transients for CV syllables starting
with /b/, /d/ and /g/. Since /g/ is not present in Dutch (as the
letter ‘g’ is realised as /x/), we focused on /b/ and /d/. We
selected two vowels, /i/ and /u/ that are involved in the vowel
contrasts, and that provide syllable contrasts above and below
the 1.5 kHz putative TFS limit as illustrated in Figure 1B. As a
result, /du/-/bu/ is a contrast that happens below the 1.5 kHz
boundary and may involve TFS coding; whereas /di/-/bi/ is a
contrast that happens above the 1.5 kHz limit and therefore does
not involve TFS coding. Again, example words are reported in
Table 1.
3.2. Analysis-resynthesis from voice recordings
To ensure that the stimuli are natural and capture realistic
traits of speech (2.1.1), we used actual recordings from a male
speaker rather than relying solely on speech synthesizers. The
recordings were then analysed, i.e.
decomposed, using the
WORLD vocoder [19, 20, 21].
The recordings were obtained using an SM58 microphone
(Shure, Niles, Illinois, USA) and an EVO4 soundcard (Audient,
Herriard, Hampshire, UK). A sampling rate of 44.1 kHz and a
32-bit (float) precision were used. The talker was a 43-year-
old, French native speaker measuring 1.85 m, with no history
of speech production disorder and no history of smoking. The
recordings had an average f0 of about 105 Hz.
To decompose the recordings using WORLD, the f0 was
first extracted using the dio and stonemask methods [22]. The
spectral envelope was then estimated using cheaptrick [23]. Fi-
nally, the spectro-temporal aperiodicity map was estimated us-
ing D4C [19].
For all the stimuli, a spectral envelope was carefully pre-
pared according to the constraints listed above. The stimuli
were then be resynthesised with a specified f0 and duration.
3.2.1. Vowels
The average spectral envelope of each vowel was approximated
with a sum of Gaussian functions. For /o/-/u/, all the Gaus-
sian components above 1.5 kHz were aligned, to keep differ-
ences only < 1.5 kHz. The continuum between the two vow-
els was realised by shifting a single Gaussian component from
200 Hz (/u/) to 380 Hz (/o/). For /i/-/y/, the components be-
low 1.5 kHz were aligned, while the ones above were shifted
from 1.70, 2.10, 3.00 kHz (/y/) to 1.95, 2.80, 4.00 kHz (/i/),
respectively. The produced vowels are displayed in panels (A)
and (B) of Figure 2.
3.2.2. Syllables
For the syllables, no Gaussian mixture was used because the
/d/-/b/ contrasts do not solely rely on differences in formant
trajectories. Instead, spectral-temporal regions were defined in
the spectral envelope matrix, such that no difference between
stimuli could occur after 100 ms. For /du/-/bu/, the region
comprised before 100 ms and below 1.5 kHz contained either
/du/ or /bu/, whereas the region above 1.5 kHz contained the
average of the spectral envelopes of /du/ and /bu/. The seg-
ment after 100 ms contained the spectral envelope of the /u/-
sound described above. For /di/-/bi/, the procedure was the
same except that changes only permitted above 1.5 kHz, and
the segment after 100 ms contained the spectral envelope of the
/i/-sound described above. For all the stimuli, great care was
dedicated to aligning the onsets of the high and low frequency
parts. Continuum steps were achieved by using weighted sum
of the spectral envelopes of the items in a pair in the region
allowing change. The produced syllables are displayed in Fig-
ure 3.
3.2.3. Post-processing
Early versions of the stimuli produced unexpected responses in
animal recordings, showing a strong high frequency component
(> 10 kHz). While this component would be unlikely to be
heard in humans, most rodents used in animal studies have a
hearing range that is shifted towards the high frequencies rel-
ative to humans. To avoid unwanted effect on physiological
recordings, the component had to be controlled. A detailed ex-
amination of the stimuli revealed that, for some of the items,
a step was introduced between two samples, right before each
glottal pulse. It is suspected that this small artefact may be gen-
erated in the PSOLA resynthesis procedure. To neutralize the
artefact, all stimuli were low-pass filtered below 9 kHz with a
4th-order zero-phase Butterworth filter.

5. Conclusions
The method described here, and the preliminary results ob-
tained from modelling, indicate that it is possible to create nat-
ural speech stimuli that can be used in simple discrimination
tasks while targeting specific neural coding mechanisms.
A
computational model of the human auditory system has been
used to confirm that, as per design, the contrast between some
of the generated stimuli is coded through the targeted neural
coding mechanism (TFS), while others are not. The produced
set of stimuli has since been used in neurophysiological exper-
iments in animals, as well as in electrophysiological and be-
havioural experiments in humans [26, 27]. They are available
at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10322257.
Overall, the followed approach demonstrates that current
analysis-resynthesis techniques make it feasible to generate
tightly controlled speech stimuli that still retain naturalness,
while being designed for very specific purposes. The technique
could be further extended to explore other neural coding mecha-
nisms, and to assess various components of language more sys-
tematically.
