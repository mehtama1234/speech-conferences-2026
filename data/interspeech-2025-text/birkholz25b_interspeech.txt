Influence of wall coverings of 3D-printed vocal tract models on measured
transfer functions
Peter Birkholz1, Dominik Sch¨afer1, Patrick H¨asner1, Jihyeon Yun1, Iris Kruppke2, R´emi Blandin3
1Institute of Acoustics and Speech Communication, TU Dresden, Germany
2Institute of Textile Machinery and High Performance Material Technology, TU Dresden, Germany
3CNRS, IETR-UMR 6164, Universit´e de Rennes, France
peter.birkholz@tu-dresden.de
Abstract
Physical replicas of the vocal tract are often produced by 3D-
printing. A proven method to obtain the acoustic transfer func-
tion of these models is based on the principle of reciprocity,
where an external sound source is used and the acoustic re-
sponse is measured inside the model at the glottal end. How-
ever, earlier investigations showed that for some model geome-
tries the measured transfer functions deviated from the expected
resonance pattern due to spurious poles and zeros. These arti-
facts are probably caused by sound transmission through the
model walls, or by whole-body vibrations of the replicas. In
this empirical study, two methods to reduce these artifacts were
compared, namely wrapping the models with sound-absorbing
fabric and embedding the models in sand. Axisymmetric 3D-
printed tubes for ten different vowels were analyzed. The re-
sults showed that both methods reduce artifacts and at the same
time improve the repeatability of the measurements.
Index Terms: vocal tract replicas
1. Introduction
The increasing use of 3D printers has led to many studies in
which 3D-printed models of the vocal tract are used to investi-
gate speech and singing. These models range from simple ax-
isymmetric tube models [1, 2, 3, 4] to geometrically realistic
models based on MRI scans of the vocal tract [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. For
their acoustic characterization, many studies determine the vol-
ume velocity transfer function H(ω) = Um(ω)/Ug(ω), where
Ug(ω) is the volume velocity at the glottis and Um(ω) is the
volume velocity at the mouth opening.
A widely used method to determine H(ω) is based on the
principle of reciprocity [5, 6, 10]. In this method, a loudspeaker
positioned in front of the model emits a broadband sweep sig-
nal Ps(ω) towards the mouth of the model to excite the vocal
tract resonances. At the same time, a microphone measures the
sweep response P1(ω) inside the model at the glottal end. Ki-
tamura et al. [5] showed that P1(ω) and H(ω) are related ac-
cording to
P1(ω) = (1 + Zrad(ω)/Z0) · Ps(ω) · H(ω),
(1)
where Zrad(ω) is the radiation impedance of the model, and
Z0 is the characteristic impedance of a plane wave.
Kita-
mura et al. [5] and Delvaux and Howard [6] approximated
H(ω) as P1(ω)/Ps(ω), where Ps(ω) was measured as the free-
field frequency response of the loudspeaker (without a vocal
tract model). Fleischer et al. [10] showed that a more accu-
rate approximation of H(ω) is obtained when P1(ω) is di-
vided by a reference pressure Pref(ω) measured directly in front
of the closed mouth of the vocal tract model, i.e., H(ω) ≈
P1(ω)/Pref(ω). In addition, the use of a sine sweep as the
excitation signal also enables the determination of the linear
vocal tract response, which is free of the harmonic distortions
generated by the loudspeaker and microphone [11, 6].
Despite these positive aspects of the measurement method,
the transfer functions obtained for some 3D-printed vocal tract
models may still show unexpected artifacts.
For example,
Birkholz et al. [12] used this method to measure the transfer
functions of 3D-printed axisymmetric tube models for different
vowels. The models were printed with the hard plastic mate-
rial PLA (polylactic acid) with an infill ratio of 100% and a
wall thickness of 3 mm. For some of the models, the transfer
function deviated from the expected pattern of an all-pole trans-
fer function below 5 kHz and showed traces of spurious poles
and zeros between 1–3 kHz. This may prevent the accurate de-
termination of the frequencies and bandwidths of some of the
resonances.
The cause for these artifacts is not entirely clear.
It is
unlikely that they are caused by higher-order modes, as these
modes should only occur at higher frequencies in axisymmetric
tube models [13]. However, the artifacts might originate from
the transmission of sound through the walls of the models, or
from whole-body vibrations of the models. There are several
options to reduce these effects. Fleischer et al. [10] coated their
models with a 1 cm thick layer of plaster to increase the mass
of the walls and prevent sound transmission through the walls.
This effectively avoided the artifacts, but the coating with plas-
ter is very time-consuming and cannot be easily reversed. In
contrast, Birkholz et al. [8] wrapped their models in multiple
layers of sound-absorbing fabric to reduce sound transmission
through the vocal tract walls. Finally, Blandin et al. [14] embed-
ded their models in sand to absorb the mechanical vibrations of
the model walls.
While all these measures seem to improve the quality of the
measured transfer functions to different degrees, they have not
been systematically compared for 3D-printed vocal tract mod-
els of different shapes. Therefore, the goal of the present study
was to compare how the methods of wrapping the models with
sound-absorbing fabric and embedding the models in sand re-
duce artifacts and improve the repeatability of measurements
compared to using the models without any wall covering. Com-
pared to coating with plaster, both of the methods used here
are easier to apply. The present study extends a recent parallel
study [15], which used only three instead of ten different model
geometries, but supported the analysis with 3D-acoustic simu-
lations.

2.2. Measurements
The measurement setup is shown in Figure 1b and is similar to
the one in [10]. The vocal tract model is shown embedded in
a bucket of sand. The upper (glottal) end is closed with a plate
containing a measuring microphone (MK 301 E by Microtech
Gefell) that records the sound pressure inside the model at the
level of the glottis. The lower end, representing the mouth,
faces a wideband loudspeaker (VISATON speaker, type FR 10-
8 Ohm in a custom-made enclosure), which is located at a dis-
tance of about 60 cm.
The transfer function measurement was performed in two
steps analogous to [10] using the Software MeasureTransfer-
Function (MTF, www.vocaltractlab.de) running on a
laptop computer. In the first step, the loudspeaker emitted an
exponential sweep with a power band from 100 Hz to 10 kHz
and a duration of 9.92 s, while the glottis microphone recorded
the sweep response P1(ω). In the second step, the mouth of the
model was closed with a stiff plate. Then the same sweep as
in step 1 was emitted by the loudspeaker, while the sound pres-
sure Pref(ω) right in front of the closed mouth was recorded
with a GRAS 40SC probe microphone. Nonlinear distortions
in the recorded signals were manually removed based on the
method by Farina [11] that is implemented in MTF. Finally,
the volume velocity transfer function was obtained as the ratio
P1(ω)/Pref(ω). The different sensitivities of the two micro-
phones and the non-flat frequency response of the probe mi-
crophone were compensated for. The signals were recorded
and played back using a USB audio interface (Aureon Xfire
8.0HD) at a sampling rate of 96 kHz and a quantization of 24
bits. All measurements were performed in an anechoic cham-
ber at a temperature of 20 ◦C. The measurement procedure is
illustrated in the YouTube video youtu.be/9AoRS9X2BNY
(with a slightly different setup).
Each of the ten models was measured three times under
each of three different conditions (10 × 3 × 3 = 90 measure-
ments in total). The three measurements per model and con-
dition were performed on different days. Hence, the models
were completely removed from the measurement setup between
measurement repetitions and reinserted. This makes it possible
to analyze the precision, i.e. the repeatability of the measure-
ments. The three conditions were the following:
1. The model wall was not covered.
2. The outer wall of the model was tightly wrapped with mul-
tiple layers of sound-absorbing fleece. The fleece was made
from synthetic fibres of at least three different fibre materials:
white fleece (chlorofibre), dark/greyish fleece (polyamide)
and a thread for mechanical stabilisation. The fabric had a
weight per unit area of 585 g/m2. It was cut into the shape of
a scarf measuring 150 cm × 13 cm.
3. The model was placed in a bucket, where the mouth opening
of the model was positioned directly over a hole in the bot-
tom of the bucket. Around the model, the bucket was filled
with sand with an average sand grain diameter of 1.0 mm.
The bucket had a conical shape with an upper diameter of
15.5 cm, a lower diameter of 8.5 cm, and a height of 19 cm.
The outer side of the bucket has been wrapped with sound-
absorbing fabric to minimize acoustic reflections.
Photos of the setup for the conditions 1, 2, and 3 are shown
in Figures 2b, c, and d, respectively. For condition 1 (no wall
cover), Figure 2b shows the first measurement step with the
open mouth, and Figure 2a shows the second step, in which
the mouth is closed with a plate and the probe microphone is
placed directly in front of it. Figure 1c shows a view into the
sand bucket from above for condition 3.

the level of the local minima between the equidistant resonances
should decrease only slowly with increasing frequency (see,

drop significantly above 7 kHz for condition 3. We suspect that
this is due to the larger flange at the mouth end added by the
bottom of the bucket in condition 3. However, this needs to be
verified in future work.
3.2. Repeatability of measurements
Figure 3 also shows that the three measured transfer functions
per condition and model tend to differ less for the conditions 2
and 3 compared to condition 1. This indicates a better repeata-
bility, and hence precision of the measurements, for conditions
2 and 3. To quantify the repeatability, we calculated the av-
erage standard deviation between the three measurements for
each model in each condition. The standard deviations were av-
eraged separately for the frequencies from 0–5 kHz, and from
5–10 kHz. The results are shown in Table 1. For the 0–5 kHz
range, the average standard deviations decrease for all models
consistently from condition 1 to 2, and from condition 2 to 3.
For the 5–10 kHz range we find the same general pattern, with
the exception of a few models (/a,u/ and the cylinder) for which
condition 2 led to the lowest values. Overall, the repeatability
of the measurements is best for condition 3 (sand), worst for
condition 1 (no wall covering), and in between for condition 2
(fabric). Two-sample t-tests for the conditions 1 vs. 2, 1 vs. 3,
and 2 vs. 3 indicate a significant difference only between the
conditions 1 and 3 in both frequency intervals at α = 0.05
(considering Bonferroni correction for multiple tests).
4. Discussion and conclusions
The results showed that transfer function measurements of 3D-
printed vocal tract models are better repeatable and lead to
less artifacts when the models are tightly wrapped into sound-
absorbing fabric, or when they are embedded in sand. With
the models embedded in sand, the repeatability and the reduc-
tion of artifacts is overall best. However, the sand embedding
method used here has also drawbacks: The level of the transfer
