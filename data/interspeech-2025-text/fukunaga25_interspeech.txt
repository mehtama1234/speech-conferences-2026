Backchannel prediction for natural spoken dialog systems
using general speaker and listener information

1Tokushima University, Japan

Abstract
Backchannel responses are a crucial component of conver-
sations enabling more effective communication through listener
feedback. Current backchannel prediction models classify these
responses into just three categories, using speech, text, and lis-
tener IDs. These IDs, which contain detailed personal infor-
mation, cannot be applied in real-world dialog systems how-
ever, and three-category classification limits response genera-
tion capabilities. Therefore, we propose a model for predict-
ing a backchannel’s ‘surface form’ using only general speaker
and listener embeddings. Our experiments show a 1.3% im-
provement in prediction accuracy when performing 3-category
classification, and a 0.9% improvement when performing 11-
category classification, compared to conventional ID embed-
dings, demonstrating an enhancement in performance that is
deployable in real-world systems.
Index Terms: backchannel prediction, spoken dialog system,
speaker and listener information
1. Introduction
Backchannels are conversational elements uttered by listeners
during a speaker’s speech to facilitate communication by pro-
viding feedback. These backchannels are not limited to short,
verbal responses, but also include non-verbal cues such as nod-
ding and smiling. These subtle signals (nods, non-verbal vo-
calizations, brief comments, etc.) not only convey understand-
ing and empathy from the listener and provide feedback to the
speaker, they also play a crucial role in sustaining the conver-
sation. In recent years, as speech input and dialog applications
based on spoken dialog systems have become more practical,
achieving a system that can engage in dialog as naturally as hu-
mans still remains challenging. In order to realize dialog with
AI agents comparable to human conversations, it will be nec-
essary to replicate the natural backchannel responses which are
frequently used during human interactions.
Previous research on backchannels has explored various as-
pects of this phenomenon, such as determining when dialog
systems should produce backchannels [1–8]. Recent studies
on classification of backchannels (BC) divide them into two
main types: “Continuers” [9], which are generic BCs which
acknowledge understanding and encourage the speaker to con-
tinue their narrative, and “Assessments” [10], which are specific
BCs consisting of short, context-dependent responses [11–16].
Some studies have further split Assessment BCs into “Under-
standing” and “Empathetic” assessments, in order to make bet-
ter predictions [17–19]. Other works have suggested annotating
each function of the backchannel and classifying short feedback
which is more detailed than a backchannel [20–22].
There have been two major challenges in recent research
on backchannel prediction. First, studies have demonstrated
that incorporating additional information about speakers and
listeners, alongside linguistic and acoustic features of their
speech, can improve accuracy. However, when implementing
a backchannel prediction model into a spoken dialog system,
it is difficult to input personal IDs with extensive, personally
identifiable information for many, or unknown, users. There-
fore, these models need a way to acquire and utilize informa-
tion about speakers and listeners that can be applied to any user.
Second, predicting backchannel categories alone does not al-
low the system to determine which specific backchannel to use.
Even within the “Continuer” category, there are multiple types
of backchannels to choose from, and their nuances can vary sub-
tly. Moreover, users can be put off if the system selects the same
backchannel repeatedly. As a result, the model needs to be able
to predict which specific backchannel the system should select,
not just the correct backchannel category.
Therefore, in this paper we propose a backchannel predic-
tion model that uses general information about speakers and lis-
teners to predict the ‘surface form’ of backchannels, which is
the more specific and appropriate type of backchannel response
that should be selected by the system, taking into account the
way backchannel prediction models are actually implemented
in spoken dialog systems.
Major our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• Unlike previous research, which has proposed the use of in-
puts such as speaker IDs, which cannot realistically be used
in actual spoken dialog systems, and backchannel category
classifications such as Continuer or Assessment, we propose
a model that uses self-reported, general speaker and listener
information to predict the correct ‘surface form’ of backchan-
nel responses, an approach which can be implemented in ex-
isting spoken dialog systems.
• By reproducing the model proposed by Ortega et al. in [13],
we conduct a detailed comparison of different methods used
to employ dialog participant information, as well as differ-
ences in performance across languages.
• Through experiments using dialog data, we analyze differ-
ences in the backchannel prediction accuracy of various mod-
els based on the presence or absence of speaker information,
the methods used to employ this information, and the effect
of speaker-listener relationships on backchannel selection.

For our language model, we used BERT [23], which has
been widely used in previous research. The input to the model
consists of 4 turns of dialog text before the backchannel occurs,
during which 768-dimensional features are obtained from the
CLS (classification) token output.

studies, recent research has shown that HuBERT is more ef-

model is 5 seconds of audio before the backchannel occurs. We
obtained 768-dimensional features through average pooling of
the HuBERT output.
The following information was used as input for the speaker
and listener model; the speaker’s gender and age, the listener’s
gender and age, and the category of the relationship between the
speaker and listener (Family, Friend, Business, School, Other).
The details of the input information are shown in Table 1. The
model extracts 128-dimensional features for each piece of in-
formation through an embedding layer, concatenates these fea-
tures, and processes them through a fully connected layer, ulti-
mately obtaining 256-dimensional features.
3. Datasets
3.1. CEJC
The Corpus of Everyday Japanese Conversation (CEJC) [27]
is a well-balanced, audio-visual corpus of typical, daily life
Japanese conversations. It consists of 557 dialogs, with 200
hours of speech data in total, and the genders and ages of
the speakers are balanced. The CEJC contains many multi-
party (three or more speaker) conversations, however predict-
ing backchannels under such condition is much more challeng-
ing. Therefore, in this study we only used the approximately 45
hours of conversations that involve only two speakers.
The Japanese backchannel responses obtained from the
CEJC corpora which occurred frequently and were semantically
classifiable were categorized into 11 ‘surface types’. The most
common backchannel ‘surface form’ of each ‘surface type’,
their English translations, and number of occurrences of each
type of backchannel are shown in Table 2. Backchannels such
as laughter are known as ‘non-verbal backchannels’ [28–30].

3.2. Data augmentation
The extracted backchannels are numerically imbalanced, so us-
ing all of the data as-is would result in accuracy bias. If we per-
form alignment using the less frequent backchannels, we can
only use around 500 instances per type. In order to increase
the amount of learnable data, we performed data augmentation.
For audio data, we extracted 5-second segments from 0.2 and
0.5 seconds prior to the original segment and treated these as
pseudo-correct data. For text data, we expanded the data by
deleting the last 3 and 5 characters to correspond with the au-
dio time segments. While the deletion of immediatly preced-
ing data does impact prediction accuracy, research by Adiba et
al. [5] has demonstrated that reasonable prediction performance
is still achievable under these conditions. This augmentation
was made only for backchannels with less than 1,500 occur-
rences, and was not applied to backchannels with 1,500 or more
occurrence. In the following experiments, the dataset is limited
to a maximum of 500 samples per backchannel category with-
out data augmentation, and a maximum of 1,500 samples per
backchannel category with data augmentation.
4. Experimental Setup

backchannel responses extracted from the CEJC dataset were

data. Following Ortega et al. [13], ‘uh-huh’ and its variants
were classified as “Continuers”, while other backchannels were
classified as “Assessments”. For the “No-BC” category, we also
followed their methodology to extract utterances that were not
considered backchannels. The number of instances within each
classification were 5,000 for “Continuers”, 5,000 for “Assess-

To compare the effectiveness of our approach with con-
ventional listener-only information embeddings, we conducted
a comparative analysis based on Ortega et al. [13]. Follow-
ing their methodology, linguistic features were extracted us-
ing the five words preceding a backchannel event, as encoded
by Word2Vec [31]. Acoustic features were derived from the 2
seconds preceding the backchannel event, using 13-dimensional
MFCCs extracted using the OpenSMILE toolkit [32], and pro-
cessed through a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Lis-
teners were assigned unique IDs in numerical order, and these
IDs were processed through an embedding layer to generate
listener-specific features. Using the CEJC dataset, we repli-
cated the model proposed in [13] to facilitate a direct compar-
ison with our proposed approach. Additionally, to evaluate the
difference in performance between listener ID embedding as
used in [13] and our proposed use of general speaker-listener
information, we conducted experiments where we replaced the
speaker-listener model in our proposed architecture with lis-
tener ID embeddings.
4.2. Training settings
The pre-trained models used in this study were independently
developed by Inui Lab at Tohoku University 1and by Rinna 2.
While some previous studies have relied on the output of these
pre-trained models, we performed fine-tuning to adapt the mod-
els to our task. The training dataset consisted of up to 1,500
samples from each backchannel category, with the data split into
training, validation, and test sets a ratio of 6:2:2.
The classifier architecture comprised three layers, with hid-
den layer sizes of 1,024 and 512, followed by the number of
output classes. Dropout layers with a dropout rate of 0.25 and
ReLU activation functions were applied between each layer.
The model was trained for 100 epochs using a batch size of
128. We employed the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1e-5 and used cross-entropy loss to guide the learning process.

6. Analysis and Discussion
6.1. Analysis
Regarding the results of the main experiment this study, high
accuracy rates were observed for the backchannel responses
“Ee” (I see), “Hai” (Yes), and “(laughter)”. “Ee” and “Hai” are
honorific expressions in Japanese, typically employed when ad-
dressing individuals of higher social status. The superior classi-
fication performance for these response suggests that our model
effectively incorporates speaker and listener relationship infor-
mation.
Fig 3 shows the distribution of the five speaker-listener
relationship types used as input to model for each of the
11backchannel responses. We can see that the bachchannels
“Ee” and “ Hai” occur more frequently in Business contexts
compared to Family or Friend situations. This distribution
aligns with their function as polite backchannel expressions
which are more frequently used in professional relationships
than in more familiar ones. This clear contextual pattern likely
contributed to the model’s ability to predict these expressions
more accurately, using the provided speaker-listener relation-
ship information. While “(laughter)” shows no notable distri-
bution patterns across relationship types, its high prediction ac-
curacy can be attributed to its contextual nature, since listeners
often respond with laughter when the speaker has just laughed.
This makes laughter relatively easy to predict, based on dialog
history and acoustic features.
6.2. Discussion
Our experiments demonstrated improvement in backchannel
classification accuracy through the incorporation of general
speaker-listener information in both three-category backchannel
prediction (for comparison with previous studies) and in the 11
category backchannel prediction task used in this study. How-
ever, the accuracy of the proposed method was slightly lower
than that achieved by methods proposed in previous research
which use personal ID embeddings. It is important to note that
while methods using personal ID embeddings have achieved
higher accuracy, they face a significant limitation: They cannot
be practically implemented in real-world spoken dialog systems
due to the difficulty in collecting and inputting personal ID pro-
files for random users. In contrast, our approach, which relies
on easily obtainable user inputs such as gender and age, can be
utilized by any user. Thus, our research highlights the trade-offs
between performance and practicality when using systems with
and without detailed speaker-listener information, especially in
relation to real-world deployment in spoken dialog systems.
While our analysis focused on the relationship between
backchannel responses and speaker-listener information, other
factors warrant investigation. For example, Figueroa et al [21]
have demonstrated that backchannel types vary according to the
speaker’s gender. Additionally, differences based on whether
conversation participants are in the same age group, or belong
to different generations, should also be examined. Furthermore,
Park et al [19] have shown that incorporating factors such as
elapsed time since the beginning of the dialog, as well as con-
versation topics, can improve prediction accuracy. We plan to
investigate these elements, along with contextual factors such
as conversation location.
7. Conclusion
In this study we have proposed a model that predicts 11 types of
backchannel ‘surface forms’ using general speaker and listener
information such as gender, age, and relationship. When used
for three-category backchannel prediction, as in previous stud-
ies, our proposed approach using speaker-listener information
showed a 1.3% improvement in classification accuracy. How-
ever, this was 1.7 % lower than the acuuracy of a conventional
method using personal ID embeddings. When used for the
prediction of 11 types of backchannels, our proposed method
demonstrated a 1.8% improvement in accuracy compared to us-
ing only a pre-trained model , however this was still 0.9% below
the accuracy achieved by the conventional method using per-
sonal ID embeddings. Although we did not exceed the accuracy
achieved in previous research, we have established a new bench-
mark for backchannel prediction accuracy using more general-
ized speaker and listener information, an approach which takes
into account the issue of practical implementation in spoken di-
alog system.
8. Limitation
In this study we have successfully validated the effectiveness
of utilizing general speaker-listener information as opposed to
previously employed individual ID-based approaches. How-
ever, when applied to a task involving the predition of 11 types
of ‘surface form’ backchannel responses, our proposed method
achieved a maximum accuracy of just over 39%, which while
notably low, was only 0.9% lower than a converntional method.
This limited performance can be attributed to the fundamental
difficulty of predicting backchannels in conversation, due to the
lack of strict grammatical or ‘naturalness’ rules limiting possi-
ble backchannel responses in given contexts. Moving forward,
we propose transitioning from the current single-label learning
approach to a multi-label learning framework that can accom-
modate multiple valid responses. It is important to note that our
current dataset does not include multi-label annotations. There-
fore, careful consideration must be given to both the methodol-
ogy for label annotation and the evaluation metrics for such a
model.
