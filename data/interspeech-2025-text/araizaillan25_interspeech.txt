Robot-assisted Recognition of Vocal Emotions in Pseudospeech for Cochlear 
Implanted Adolescents 
Gloria Araiza-Illan1, Luke Meyer1, Bert Maat1, Deniz Başkent1 
1Department of Otorhinolaryngology / Head and Neck Surgery, University Medical Centre 
Groningen, University of Groningen, The Netherlands 
g.a.araiza.illan@rug.nl, l.meyer@rug.nl, a.maat@umcg.nl, d.baskent@rug.nl 
Abstract 
Recognising vocal emotions in speech is difficult for children 
with hearing loss and who use a cochlear implant (CI). As 
regular monitoring could be burdensome, we propose a NAO 
robot as a test interface. Adolescents with CIs (10-17yr) 
performed the EmoHI test for recognising vocal emotions in 
pseudospeech (no linguistic emotion information), once with a 
computer and once with a NAO. Interfaces are compared via 
test results and durations, and participants’ perception of the 
interfaces. Test results (sensitivity index, d’) were similar (0.36 
± 0.36 vs. 0.37 ± 0.43), but durations were significantly longer 
on the NAO (4.18 min ± 39 sec vs. 5.13 min ± 51 sec). The 
computer had a higher perceived usability, but the NAO was 
rated more enjoyable, engaging and preferable. Overall, the 
NAO sound quality seems sufficient for conducting the EmoHI 
test, even with a CI. The higher ratings of enjoyability for NAO 
may be especially useful in conducting such tests in populations 
with hearing devices. 
Index Terms: vocal emotion recognition, cochlear-implanted 
adolescents, human-robot interaction, psychophysics, user-
experience. 
1. 
Introduction 
Recognition of emotions in speech is vital for correctly 
understanding and interpreting the meaning, which is essential 
for social communication. Vocal emotion recognition relies on 
the perception and processing of relevant prosodic cues [1]. In 
hearing loss, such cues may not be sufficiently audible, or even 
altered due to suprathreshold distortions [2], or in cochlear 
implants, due to limitations of electric hearing [3], [4]. As the 
correct interpretation of the emotion prosodic cues would rely 
on use of past learning experiences, vocal emotion recognition 
in children, even those with normal-hearing, can take many 
years to develop to reach adult-like performance [5], [6], [7]. 
Hearing aids (HAs) and cochlear implants (CIs) provide 
compensation for hearing loss; however, it is not clear to what 
degree such compensation would be possible for vocal emotion 
recognition, or if such compensation would be possible for all 
device users [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. For example, while children 
with CIs seem to have higher scores in voice or speech 
perception tests than adult CI users [10], they seem to have 
difficulty in vocal emotion recognition [5], [6]. 
Identifying potential difficulties and delays in vocal 
emotion recognition in children with hearing devices may 
require regular monitoring, which can be burdensome. To help 
maintain attention, concentration, and engagement, some 
computer interfaces use cartoons and animations, as well as a 
game-like progression and rewards [6], [11]. Socially assistive 
robots can also provide an effective interface to enhance 
participant’s engagement and even performance during a task, 
whilst offering a positive experience [12], [13], [14]. Literature 
has shown that the physical embodiment of an agent is preferred 
over its virtual counterpart, and can contribute favourably to its 
social presence [15], [16], potentially motivating interactors to 
exert more effort on a given task [17]. In areas such as vocal 
emotion recognition, which seems challenging for children and 
adults with hearing loss, such factors could be crucial.  
Humanoid robots, such as the NAO from Aldebaran 
Robotics, are an example of socially assistive robots that are 
often used in areas such as education [18], and emotional and 
developmental therapy [19], [20]. The NAO has been shown to 
facilitate various learning applications in human-robot 
interactions due its small size, and friendly and human-like 
appearance [21]. The aim of this study is to evaluate the 
feasibility of the NAO robot to conduct a vocal emotion 
recognition test with adolescents with CIs.   
2. 
Methods and materials 
2.1. 
Participants 
Forty-one Spanish speaking adolescents (10 - 17 yr; 15.16 ± 
1.96 yr; 8 identified as female, 11 as male, and 22 preferred not 
to say) took the EmoHI test twice, one after the other (computer 
and robot, Figure 1). All participants were attendees of a 
Spanish summer camp for CI adolescents. Data from five 
participants were excluded for the analysis of the audiological 
component due to either missing or incomplete audio recording 
of their interaction with the robot, during which the vocal 
emotion recognition test was conducted. The audio recordings 
were used for the corroborating of spoken responses from 
participants. The legal guardians of the adolescents provided 
their written informed consent prior to the start of the study.

2.2. 
Stimuli 
The EmoHI corpus consists of a closed set of pseudospeech 
stimuli with three emotional intonations (happy, angry, and 
sad), produced by four (two female and two male) native Dutch 
speakers [6]. The two pseudospeech sentences used are “Koun 
se mina lod belam” [kʌun sǝmina: lɔd be:lam] and “Nekal ibam

soud molen” [ne:kal ibam sʌut mo:lǝn] (from the Geneva 
Multimodal Emotion Portrayal (GEMEP) Corpus; [22]). While 
these phrases are plausible sound combinations for Western 
languages, they have no meaning in Indo-European languages, 
hence the participants cannot make use of the linguistic content 
for identifying the speech emotions. The number of presented 
stimuli totalled to 36; four speakers x three emotions x three 
sentence productions (one sentence repeated once + the other 
sentence). 
Stimulus presentation level for all interfaces was calibrated 
to 75 dB SPL, using a Knowles Electronics Mannequin for 
Acoustic Research (KEMAR, GRAS, Holte, Denmark) head 
assembly and a Svantek sound level meter (Svan 979). 
2.3. 
NAO setup 
Two humanoid NAO robots were used in parallel, a V5 H25 
and a V6. We named them “Sam” and “Robin”, gender-neutral 
names to elude a priori gender assignment of the robots.  
The original EmoHI MATLAB code was rewritten into 
Python, allowing the test and stimuli to be run and stored 
directly on the robots. The stimuli were played using the 
onboard soundcard and through the internal stereo loudspeakers 
on either side of the robots’ heads. One of the preprogrammed 
voices available with the robots was used for when the robots 
talked, which differed from the voices of the test stimuli. This 
difference was explained to participants.  
The experimental design was based on the “Wizard of Oz” 
(WoZ) technique [23], in which the robot is controlled by a 
human supervisor, unbeknownst to the participant interacting 
with the robot. This allowed participants to communicate with 
robots using speech, as previous studies reported people prefer 
to interact with robots via verbal communication [24], [25]. 
The interaction (all in Spanish) started with an introductory 
component, which included a short conversation with the 
participant before the main test explanation. The conversation 
consisted of a series of simple questions, including “what is 
your name?”, and “how are you doing today?”. Following the 
conversation, the test was explained and commenced. At the 
end of the test, another simple conversation was added, 
inquiring whether or not participants enjoyed the test and 
thanking them for their participation. Participants were asked to 
give their responses verbally to each of the presented questions 
and stimuli (naming the emotion). The human supervisor 
(“wizard”) manually logged the spoken responses of 
participants, both during the conversation and the testing 
components of the interaction. In order to allow for a “wizard” 
to log participants’ verbal responses, and to have control over 
multiple robot actions in parallel (e.g., moving the hands and 
body, blinking, speaking, presenting stimuli and giving 
feedback), the test implementation was redesigned to use the 
Robot Operating System (ROS). The full duration of the 
interaction was audio recorded for the offline (post-study) 
analysis of possible human error due to the WoZ 
implementation. 
2.4. 
Computer setup 
An HP Pavilion X360 and a Microsoft Surface Pro with 
Windows 10 and MATLAB 2022b were used in parallel. 
Stimuli were played through the computers’ internal sound 
cards, and a set of Logitech Z200 (Lausanne, Switzerland) 
external loudspeakers. Participants logged their responses using 
an external mouse connected to each computer. 
The MATLAB computer interface (Figure 1, panel B) 
corresponds to a child-friendly circus-themed animation (as a 
serious game) composed of three cartoon clowns, each of which 
shows a specific facial expression: happy, angry, and sad. A 
parrot in the centre of the screen presented the stimuli, after 
which the participant clicked on the clown with the facial 
expression that matched the heard emotion of the voice. 
2.5. 
Evaluation of the interface perception 
Two questionnaires were used to assess how participants 
perceived the usability of both the NAO robot and the 
computer, and which interface (either robot or computer) the 
participants preferred based on their user experience (UX). 
2.5.1. Usability questionnaire 
The System Usability Scale (SUS) [26] is widely used to assess 
the perceived appropriateness of the proposed purpose of any 
interface [27]. The original SUS consists of 10 items, 
alternating between positive (odd items) and negative (even 
items), rated using a 5-point Likert scale. We used a shortened 
version of the Spanish translated SUS (hereafter referred to as 
child-SUS), based on the recommendation of two native 
Spanish speaking speech and language therapists, as three items 
were found to be too complex for child participants. Table 1 
shows the English version of the child-SUS.

replaced with either “robot” or “computer”.

I think I would like to use the [interface] frequently.

[interface] quickly. 
I found the [interface] very cumbersome to use. 
I felt confident using the [interface]. 
I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going 
with the [interface]. 
In SUS, each item is scored between 0 and 4. For the odd 
numbered items, this corresponds to the Likert rating minus 1. 
For the even numbered items, which are reverse coded, this 
corresponds to 5 minus the Likert rating [26]. In the original 
SUS, these item scores are summed and multiplied by 2.5 to 
obtain a final SUS score out of 100. For the child-SUS, the 
scores are summed and multiplied by 3.57 to obtain the final 
SUS score out of 100. Furthermore, we also computed the 
corresponding SUS curved grading scale (CGS). The CGS is 
also consistent with industrial practices, and has become 
common that a score of at least 80 (grade of A-) is indicative of 
above average positive user experience [27]. 
2.5.2. User experience questionnaire 
ISO 9241-210 [28] defines UX as “a person’s perceptions and 
responses resulting from the use or anticipated use of a product, 
system, or service”. We assessed participants’ UX via a 5-item 
questionnaire that required a binary response (selecting either 
the computer or the robot). The responses to each of the five 
items in the questionnaire are reported individually.

2.6. 
Experimental setup and procedure 
The study was conducted at a summer camp house in Spain. 
Interfaces were placed at four different rooms and participants 
sat at a table facing each interface. The order of the test interface 
was randomised, counter-balancing potential learning and 
fatigue effects. Participants were first trained with four practice 
stimuli, randomly taken from the 36 stimuli. During the test, 
participants were presented with all 36 stimuli in random order 
and were instructed to indicate the recognised speech vocal 
emotion (happy, angry, or sad).  
Positive and negative visual feedback were presented 
during training and data collection. For the computer, confetti 
was displayed for correct responses and a headshake from the 
parrot for incorrect responses. Similarly, the robot nodded when 
a correct response was given, and shook its head when an 
incorrect response was given.  
After each EmoHI test, participants filled in the child-SUS 
for that specific interface. At the end of the entire experimental 
session, participants filled in the UX questionnaire, evaluating 
their preferences between the two interfaces. 
2.7. 
Data analysis 
Participants’ sensitivity index d-prime (d’) was calculated per 
emotion per interface based on hit rates and false alarms [29]. 
In this study, the maximum possible d’ is 2.77 for the 3-
alternative-forced choice model, which corresponds to 91.67% 
maximum hit-rate; and 8.33% maximum false-alarms [29]. In 
interpreting d’, increasingly positive scores indicate a stronger 
probability of correct recognitions (hits), scores near zero 
indicate random recognitions and decreasing negative scores 
indicate a high probability of incorrect recognitions (false 
alarms), i.e., a systematic incorrect categorization. d’scores 
were used in a 2-by-2 repeated-measures ANOVA with two 
within-subject factors (two interfaces ╳ three emotions). 
Where sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections 
were applied. Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA were also 
performed using the same factors described above, motivation 
for which is further discussed in [30]. In addition, d’ scores 
were averaged across all emotions per interface and hence 
referred to as “overall/combined vocal emotion d’”. As this 
removed the factor of emotion, a paired-sample t-test (classical 
and Bayesian) was performed between these averages for either 
interface. To examine the effect of the interface on the test 
duration, classical and Bayesian t-tests were performed. 
Finally, paired-sample t-tests were also performed for each of 
the SUS items based on their raw Likert values between the 
computer and NAO interfaces.

3.2. 
Test duration 
The test duration was 4.18 min ± 39 sec with the computer, and 
5.13 min ± 51 sec with the NAO. Comparison of these durations 
showed the NAO was significantly longer than the computer 
[t(34) = 6.12, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.26, BF10 = 2.77e+4].   
3.3. 
Evaluation of the interface perception 
Results of the child-SUS showed the computer was given a 
higher mean score (76.02 ± 16.04), and thus a higher CGS grade 
(B) than the NAO robot (63.01 ± 13.97, C-).  
Results of the UX questionnaire (Figure 3) showed a 
preference for the NAO robot across all UX items, except for 
the item “Which was easier to hear?”. The most preference was 
shown for the item “Which is more engaging?”.

4. 
Discussion 
The outcome of the study showed both through classical and 
Bayesian inference methods that when a significant effect was 
present, it was due to the emotion factor, not the interface factor.  
Regarding the significant effect of the emotion factor, as 
happiness and anger are both high arousal emotions, and share 
a number of vocal acoustic features, they are often confused. 
This was previously shown with adult CI users [31], and our 
results now confirm the patterns to be similar also in adolescent 
CI users. In contrast, sadness is low arousal, with distinct 
acoustic features than high arousal emotions, making it easier 
to recognise it in the EmoHI test, resulting in a higher d’ score.  
Regarding the interface factor, the present study expands 
the findings of previous literature comparing these two 
interfaces, but with other types of auditory and speech 
recognition tests, in typical-hearing adults, and in sound-treated 
rooms [13], [30]. The present study demonstrates the feasibility 
of the NAO robot as a test interface to be broader. The NAO 
can also be a feasible interface for assessing vocal emotion 
recognition, even when used in an uncontrolled and noisy 
environment (such as the summer camp), and even when tested 
with adolescents with CIs, despite the challenges of the 
developmental effects and hearing loss [5], [6]. 
The duration to complete the EmoHI test was significantly 
longer when using the NAO in comparison to the computer. An 
explanation for this may lie in the UX questionnaire results, 
where the majority of participants reported that the computer 
was easier to hear than the NAO. Due to the noisy conditions in 
which the NAOs were located at the summer camp, and the 
participants finding it difficult to hear the robots, it is possible 
that they might have had to focus more on throughout the test, 
taking longer to consider each of their responses.  
Results of the child-SUS showed that overall perceived 
usability of the computer was greater than that for the NAO. 
NAO received a CGS grade four grades below than the 
computer. However, both interfaces received a grade lower than 
A-, the minimum SUS score for above average positive user 
experience [27]. This suggests that none of the interfaces are 
perceived by CI adolescents as highly appropriate for the 
proposed purpose (vocal emotion recognition). Furthermore, 
this might be linked to the difficulty that CI adolescents 
experience in recognising vocal emotions in general, as was 
explained in Introduction. The EmoHI test was designed to 
make the recognition task as doable as possible, by only using 
three core emotions, using stimuli that are short enough for test 
duration and long enough for expression of emotions, and by 
keeping the total test duration relatively short [6]. Despite this, 
perhaps there is still opportunity to improve and optimise the 
design of the EmoHI test to provide an increased positive 
experience for participants, which could be realised with input 
from the adolescent CI users.  
The higher child-SUS scores may depict a greater 
familiarity with a computer interface. However, this may in fact 
have the inverse effect regarding interface preference, 
supported by the majority of the adolescents preferring the 
robot over the computer in the UX questionnaire. Both the low 
child-SUS score and the general preference for the NAO may 
be linked to a novelty effect, given that the participants reported 
to not have interacted with the robot before. It is therefore 
possible that, through repeated interactions, as participants 
become more familiar with the NAO, its perceived usability 
may increase, but there is also the possibility that the preference 
for the NAO robot may decrease. 
It is also possible that the child-SUS results were affected 
by the aforementioned non-ideal setups for the NAO robots. 
The locations of the robots (randomly chosen) were more open 
to the general ongoings of the camp, such as people entering the 
spaces or chatting relatively loudly nearby. Therefore, it may be 
that the computers appeared more usable, as participants were 
in much quieter conditions when using these interfaces.  
Post-hoc characterisation of the speakers of all interfaces 
showed no evidence to expect that a difference in loudness and 
timbre of the stimuli would be perceived by participants. 
Finally, regarding the implementation of the WoZ, it was 
hypothesised that for participants, verbally responding to the 
NAO would be a more natural and simple method of response 
logging, compared to using the robot’s sensors. Not only does 
the verbal method greatly reduce the steps from recognition to 
response, but also minimises informational overload by limiting 
the processing system to only auditory information, excluding 
extra visual information, according to the multimedia learning 
theory [32]. Nevertheless, the WoZ potentially brought some 
limitations. This setup placed a great deal of reliance on the 
human supervisor (“wizard”) to accurately record the responses 
of the participants (source of human error).  
5. 
Conclusions 
Our study showed the potential of a widely used socially 
assistive robot as an effective interactive vocal emotion 
recognition testing interface for CI adolescents, comparable to 
the standard computer interface (as a serious game). Taking into 
account that children and adolescents with hearing devices may 
need regular monitoring and testing, it is of clinical value to 
have varied, trustworthy and portable technological tools that 
could also offer a positive and engaging experience.
