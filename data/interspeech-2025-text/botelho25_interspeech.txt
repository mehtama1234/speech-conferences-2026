Abstract
This work describes a comprehensive approach for the auto-
matic assessment of cognitive decline from spontaneous speech
in the context of the PROCESS Challenge 2025. Based on
our previous experience on the use of speech and text-derived
biomarkers for disease detection, we evaluate here the use of
knowledge-based acoustic and text-based feature sets, as well
as LLM-based macro-descriptors, and multiple neural represen-
tations (e.g., Longformer, ECAPA-TDNN, and Trillsson em-
beddings). The combination of these feature sets with different
classifiers resulted in a large pool of systems, from which, those
providing the best balance between train, development, and in-
dividual class performance were selected for model ensembling.
Our final best-performing systems correspond to combinations
of models that are complementary to each other, relying on
acoustic and textual information from the three clinical tasks
provided in the challenge dataset.
Index Terms: Pathological speech, dementia detection, PRO-
CESS challenge, speech embeddings, macro-descriptors
1. Introduction
Dementia, which affects fifty-five million people around the
world, is one of the major causes of disability and dependency
among older people [1]. It is marked by a progressive decline
in cognitive functions beyond what is considered normal in bio-
logical aging [1]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive neu-
rodegenerative disorder, is the most common form of dementia.
Although memory impairment is the most prominent symptom,
speech and language impairments are also persistent and useful
in detecting early onset of the disease [2, 3, 4]. Mild cognitive
impairment (MCI) is a clinical condition that often leads to de-
mentia. It is characterized by noticeable deficits in one or more
cognitive domains that exceed typical age-related changes but
do not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia [5, 6].
While numerous studies have leveraged speech and lan-
guage biomarkers for detecting dementia, AD, and MCI (e.g.,
[7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]), this field remains hindered by a lack
of standardization and sufficiently large, representative bench-
marks for systematic comparison of methodologies. Notable
exceptions include the ADReSS Challenges, which focus on
AD detection [13, 14, 15], and the Taukadial Challenge, which
incorporates data in English and Mandarin to address MCI de-
tection [16]. Recently, the PROCESS Challenge introduced
a dataset designed for three-class classification, distinguishing
healthy controls, individuals with MCI, and patients with de-
mentia, a problem setting that is less commonly studied [17].

In this paper, we describe our participation in the PROCESS
Challenge 2025 where, building on our previous work, we pro-
posed and experimentally evaluated diverse approaches for dis-
tinguishing healthy controls, individuals affected by MCI, and
dementia patients. Namely, we explored complementary data
representations targeted at capturing distinct manifestations
(i.e., potential symptoms) of early cognitive impairment often
reflected in speech and language production. Such represen-
tations include knowledge-based (KB) acoustic and linguistic
features, text- and speech-based neural embeddings, and macro-
descriptors derived from LLMs, which were combined with
multiple classification strategies. The resulting top-performing
systems were fused using logistic regression to achieve two
final systems. This fusion strategy allowed us to leverage
complementary information and substantially improve the fi-
nal classification performance. The diagram in Figure 1 sum-
marizes our approach. Additional details on feature extraction
and results are available at github.com/mcatarinatb/

2. Corpus
The PROCESS Challenge dataset [17] was designed to promote
research on early detection of dementia via speech analysis.
Dataset Overview. The corpus includes three diagnostic
classes: (i) the Healthy Control (HC) group includes volun-
teers without cognitive impairment, and individuals possibly ex-
periencing memory issues unrelated to neurodegenerative con-
ditions; (ii) the MCI group; and (iii) individuals diagnosed with
early-stage Dementia, exhibiting varying degrees of cognitive
decline. Detailed demographic and disease severity statistics for
the training and development sets are reported in Table 1.
Cognitive Assessment Scores. The dataset also offers the
Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score for a subset of
participants. The MMSE, commonly used to assess cognitive
impairment, ranges from 0 to 30 and attains lower scores in
individuals who completed comparatively fewer years of edu-
cation and/or have diagnosable diseases [18].

Cognitive Assessment Tasks. The corpus includes audio
recordings from three types of elicitation tasks, resulting in
three audio files per speaker:
• Cookie Theft Description (CTD). Subjects describe the
well-known “Cookie Theft” picture [19], to assess cognitive
functions such as language comprehension and memory.
• Phonemic Fluency (PFT). Subjects utter as many words
starting with the letter “P” as possible within a minute, to
evaluate executive function and phonological processing.
• Semantic Fluency (SFT). Subjects are asked to list as many
animals as possible in one minute, to assess verbal fluency,
lexical access, and semantic memory.
3. Methodology
Figure 1 shows an overview of the proposed method. The
speech signals are pre-processed to remove noise prior to au-
tomatic transcription. Features are then extracted from both
the cleaned speech signals and their corresponding transcripts,
serving as inputs for classifier training. Finally, the best-
performing individual systems are combined through model en-
sembling to generate the final predictions. This fusion of com-
plementary information helps tackle the challenges faced in ac-
curately classifying dementia cases in this dataset.
3.1. Pre-processing
A preliminary inspection of the recordings identified two un-
desired factors: the presence of loud noises (signaling tones)
and the interventions of interviewers. The former was dealt
with through signal processing approaches while, for the lat-
ter, we experimented with speaker diarization systems for the
automatic removal of those interventions. However, early ex-
periments showed that state-of-the-art diarization systems had
very poor performance in this setting, often removing large seg-
ments corresponding to the subject. For this reason, and af-
ter a preliminary evaluation of manual removal of interviewers’
interventions that showed little difference in performance, we
opted to skip this step.
Noise Removal. Several recordings in the challenge dataset
contain signaling tones from the recording protocol that marked
the beginning and the end of the tasks, in addition to various
other loud noises. To prevent such noises from interfering with
the detection of cognitive impairment, an energy-based denois-
ing step was introduced to replace those sounds with low-energy
random Gaussian noise in segments without speech.
Automatic Speech Recognition. We compared two versions
of OpenAI’s Whisper [20], whisper-medium.en and whisper-
large-v2, as well as CrisperWhisper [21], a variant of Whisper
designed to capture every spoken element, including disfluen-
cies. This characteristic makes CrisperWhisper a promising tool
in the study of neurodegenerative disorders, where disfluencies
hold diagnostic information.

3.2. Feature Extraction
To model manifestations of cognitive impairment, we used fea-
ture representations derived from both the acoustic and linguis-
tic components of speech, including neural and KB features.
Acoustic features. In terms of KB features, we compared four
different sets: eGeMAPS [22] and ComParE [23], both ex-
tracted with OpenSMILE [24]; a set of 33 acoustic features ex-
tracted with Praat [25], henceforth referred to as Praat; and a
subset of these, containing 11 pause-related features, referred to
as Pauses. The Praat features strongly overlap with those de-
scribed in [26]. In terms of neural speech representations, we
used ECAPA-TDNN speaker embeddings (ECAPA) [27] and
TRILLsson paralinguistic embeddings [28].
Text features. We compared two different KB feature sets: one
set of 11 linguistic features ( Ling) proposed by [26], and one
set of 8 linguistic features designed for fluency tasks (Fluency).
Additionally, we explored the macro-descriptors ( Macro) pro-
posed in [29], estimated using Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct [30]1. In
terms of neural representations, we explored embeddings from
three pre-trained Language Models (PLMs), namely BERT
[31], RoBERTa [32], and Longformer [33].
3.3. Classification
For classification, we use Support Vector Machines (SVMs),
Decision Trees (DT), Random Forest (RF), and Multi-layer Per-
ceptron (MLP). Besides, we explore Fuzzy Fingerprints (FFP),
an interpretable classification technique that has proved to be
useful when there is high class imbalance in text datasets [34].
To address data scarcity, the training set was divided into
five folds, ensuring a balanced distribution of classes and gen-
der. Classifiers were trained using five-fold cross-validation,
and predictions for the development set were obtained by av-
eraging the outputs of the five classifiers. These averaged pre-
dictions were then used to compute the final performance met-
rics. All systems were evaluated in terms of unweighted aver-
age F1-score (UAF1), following the challenge guidelines. The
F1-score for each class is also reported. As the official test set
was unavailable, performance is reported only for the training
set (using cross-validation) and the held-out development set.
While most systems were trained for each of the three tasks
separately, preliminary experiments showed that acoustic neural
embedding-based systems benefited from a combination of the
three speech tasks to obtain speaker-level predictions.

3.4. Model Ensemble
To exploit the potential complementary information embedded
in individual systems (i.e., one or more feature sets followed
by a classifier), we adopted a late fusion strategy. After eval-
uating every single system, the top-performing ones were se-
lected, with a focus on those providing complementary informa-
tion while exhibiting a balanced performance across the cross-
validation and development sets, as well achieving non-zero
F1-scores for the dementia class whenever possible. Then, the
prediction scores provided by each system for each class – ei-
ther as probabilities, softmax scores or, in the case of SVMs,
distances to the class-separating hyperplane – were aggregated
using a logistic regression model. Specifically, for each target
class, system-dependent scaling factors and offsets were esti-
mated and calibrated to approximate log-likelihood ratios using
the training scores obtained in cross-validation [35]. Then, the
fusion models were applied to the development set and the class
with the maximum log-likelihood ratio score was the predicted
one. In these experiments, we considered all possible ensemble
combinations of up to six single systems at a time.

Limitations. Among the limitations of this work, we first high-
light the challenges arising from the dataset, namely the signifi-
cant imbalance between diagnostic classes and the extreme data
scarcity for the dementia class (cf. Table 1). These issues may
partly explain the performance gap observed between the held-
out development set and the test set, as well as the lower perfor-
mance compared to previous studies on the ADReSS [36, 29] or
Taukadial [9] Challenges, even considering that those targeted
binary classification problems. Moreover, it is also necessary
to take into account the complexity and subjectivity inherent
in clinical assessments. The distribution of MMSE scores pro-
vided in the dataset exhibits considerable overlap between con-
ditions –even between groups that are presumed to be more dis-
tinct, such as HC and Dementia–, highlighting the difficulty of
differentiating between cognitive disorders in their early stages.
A second limitation is that our approach does not consider
demographic data, which physicians typically consider in clin-
ical scenarios. This omission is due to two factors: the limited
size of the dataset and the unavailability of demographic infor-
mation for the test set, making its incorporation infeasible in our
approach. As a result, demographic differences, such as age dis-
tributions of dementia patients between different partitions, may
have had a negative impact on our models. This also has a neg-
ative impact on the “real-word” applicability of our approach
and this dataset, since demographic information is crucial for
accurate clinical decision making.
A third limitation is the potential influence of interview-
ers’ interventions on the results. An automated diagnostic tool
should not be influenced by the behavior of healthcare prac-
titioners. While this issue could be partially mitigated by di-
arization, our experiments with state-of-the-art diarizers failed
to yield high-quality outputs. Moreover, even with diarization,
the subject’s behavior remains influenced by interventions, such
as prompts to recall specific information (e.g.,“farm animals”).
Although it is natural for individuals with cognitive impairment
to require additional support during speech tasks, this depen-
dency highlights a key limitation of the data collection method.
Finally, the potential for overfitting must be acknowledged.
Despite using cross-validation on the training set and maintain-
ing a held-out development set, the risk of overfitting increases
due to the extensive comparison of approaches on a limited
dataset. Future research and validation on larger datasets are
necessary to address this issue.
5. Conclusions
This paper presents our contributions for the automatic assess-
ment of cognitive decline from spontaneous speech in the con-
text of the PROCESS Challenge [17]. We conducted extensive
experimentation, addressing the challenge from multiple per-
spectives. This included evaluating the accuracy of various ASR
models, exploring numerous acoustic- and text-based feature
descriptors (and their combinations), and studying a wide range
of classification approaches. Our findings suggest that fusion of
complementary systems achieve higher performance. Systems
based on pause-related speech features and macro-descriptors
are consistently selected across model ensembles, highlighting
the effectiveness of KB speech features and high-level assess-
ment concepts as potential biomarkers of cognitive decline. Ad-
ditional insights demonstrate the capability of utterance-level
speech embeddings to encode and fuse information from mul-
tiple speech assessment tasks, as well as of Longformer in cap-
turing long-term dependencies, particularly in the CTD task,
where processing of context is essential. The limitations en-
countered, however, highlight the ongoing research challenges
in developing automatic systems for cognitive decline assess-
ment, particularly in real-world scenarios involving transitional
stages, small corpora, and significant data imbalance.
