Abstract
Anomaly detection in multivariate time series data is an exten-
sive field of research with significant impact on a broad spec-
trum of real-world applications. Building a reliable anomaly
detection system is extremely challenging due to data imbal-
ance, lack of labels, next to the actual definition of anoma-
lies. The current study proposes a new transformer-based ap-
proach, together with a publicly available time series dataset –
the TIKI data corpus – comprising various performance met-
rics of a Kubernetes cluster distributed over roughly 500 mil-
lion timestamps. The proposed network, entitled PredTrAD, is
compared with the state-of-the-art anomaly detection models –
TranAD and DTAAD – in several experiments: (1) verification
on three well-known benchmark corpora, (2) evaluation on the
TIKI data, and (3) cross-entity anomaly detection. Across all
experimental constellations, the proposed model has shown a
significant performance growth, proven by F1 score enhance-
ments of 2.41% up to 16.81%.

1. Introduction
The rapid and global process of digitization has led to huge
amount of time series data which require monitoring and down-
stream analysis. Anomaly detection, defined as the process of
identifying data points that significantly deviate from the ma-
jority of instances [1], has become increasingly important in
this context. Deep learning methods have emerged as highly
promising in this research field, offering the ability to learn
complex patterns directly from raw data without extensive fea-
ture engineering [2]. However, anomaly detection for time se-
ries data faces several challenges: patterns are highly domain-
specific [3], labeling requires significant expertise, and class
imbalance can lead to accuracy paradox issues [4]. Addition-
ally, practical systems must carefully balance sensitivity with
precision to avoid alert fatigue [4].
The current study pro-
poses leveraging the transformer architecture [5] for time se-
ries anomaly detection, introducing a novel model architec-
ture: the Prediction-based Transformer for Anomaly Detection
(PredTrAD), in two different versions V1 and V2 (see Figure 3).
These architectures combine parallel sequence processing with
positional encoding and self-attention mechanisms, enabling ef-
ficient processing of large-scale temporal data [6, 7]. The re-
mainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews
related work. Section 3 describes the data material. Section
4 presents the proposed methodology, followed by our exper-
imental setup, results and a discussion in Section 5. Finally, a
summary, conclusion, and future outlook are given in Section 6.
2. Related Work
Recent advances in deep learning have transformed anomaly de-
tection, particularly through the transformer-based architectures
[5]. The current state-of-the-art methods are TranAD [7] and
DTAAD [6]. TranAD uses an encoder and two decoder net-
works, implementing a two-phase adversarial training process
with model-agnostic meta learning (MAML) [7]. It calculates
a focus score to evaluate reconstruction quality and uses the
Peak Over Threshold (POT) procedure [8] to identify anoma-
lies. Building on TranAD, DTAAD combines temporal con-
volutional networks (TCN) with the transformer architecture
[6]. It consists of two main components: a TCN-based autore-
gression layer and a Transformer encoder-decoder layer. Like
TranAD, it uses two-phase adversarial training and MAML.
3. Data Material
In this study three commonly used benchmark datasets are used,
all of them widely applied in the research field of anomaly de-
tection to be able to properly evaluate and compare the qual-
ity of different anomaly detection approaches. The two bench-
mark repositories – Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) satel-
lite data & Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) rover data – de-
scribe two public data corpora from NASA, both proposed by
Hundman et al. [9]. They contain telemetry data like radiation,
temperature, power, computational activities, et cetera. SMAP
monitors 55 different entities across 25 dimensions, while MSL
encompasses 27 entities with 55 monitored dimensions. The la-
bels were derived from Incident Surprise Anomaly reports and
therefore all telemetry channels were reviewed to ensure that
the anomalies are in the corresponding telemetry data, and spe-
cific anomalous time ranges among all dimensions were labeled
manually by experts [9]. The Server Machine Dataset (SMD)
is similar to the novel and proposed TIKI dataset, as it also
includes metrics like CPU load, network usage, memory con-
sumption, et cetera [10]. The dataset consists of 28 entities,
with each entity representing a server, monitoring 38 different
metrics. In general, the SMD dataset was presented by Su et al.
[10] and contains data collected over a five-week period from
a large internet company. The test data was separately labeled
by domain experts for each dimension of each entity based on
incident reports. The industry partner TIKI has its own data
platform, named TIKI Data Science Platform (DSP), which pro-
vides companies and their own data scientists a platform for ma-
chine learning operations (MLOps). Almost every component
of the TIKI-DSP is part of a large Kubernetes cluster, which
utilizes Container Advisor to gather information about different
metrics (e.g. resource usage, open threads, etc.) of a specific
container (similar to virtual machines), and stores it in a time
series database. This data describes the proposed TIKI dataset,
a time series corpus including 35,559 pods. A pod refers to a

4.3. Network Model
The proposed PredTrAD model is available in two different
versions – PredTrAD-V1 and PredTrAD-V2 (see Figure 3).
PredTrAD was fundamentally inspired by the transformer con-
cept [5], originally designed for the task of natural language
processing.
Figure 3 provides a schematic representation of
the general architectural design, including both model versions.
In principle, these models are designed to operate prediction-
based, as opposed to reconstruction-based methods, such as
TranAD and DTAAD [6, 7]. To illustrate, Figure 2 shows the
input and the desired output of PredTrAD. The window size w
is defined by the two variables w1 and w2, which are the length
of src and tgt, respectively.
Figure 2: Illustration of a single window and the split into
model inputs (src & tgt) and desired outputs of PredTrAD-V1
and PredTrAD-V2

xt−w2.
This element serves as a start token, which means
that PredTrAD-V1 is trained to predict the subsequent elements
designated as yt,PredTrAD-V1. For this reason, we utilize masked
multi-head attention in the decoder to exclude future predictions
and consider only previous elements of the respective window.
PredTrAD-V2 is trained in a manner analogous to PredTrAD-
V1, yet its fundamental objective is to predict solely the final
element of the respective time window as illustrated in Fig-
ure 2. The left part of Figure 3 is similar to the original trans-
former architecture [5]. At the beginning, src ∈Rb×w1×d and
tgt ∈Rb×w2×d, where b refers to batch size and w1 and w2 de-
note the length of the src and tgt windows, respectively, are fed
into a linear layer to transform the parametric dimensionality d
to dmodel, followed by a learnable positional encoding, which
utilizes normal positional encoding in combination with a feed-
forward network to add more flexibility to this fundamental pro-
cess. The computational flow of the encoder-decoder architec-
ture remains unchanged compared to the description provided
by Vaswani et al. [5]. The outcome of the decoder exhibits
the shape Rb×w2×dmodel. PredTrAD-V1 uses this abstract rep-
resentation and directs it to a linear layer that transforms the
last dimension from dmodel to dtest. As a result, the desired out-
put would be yt,PredTrAD-V1, as visualized in Figure 2. Consid-
ering Figure 3, it is notable that PredTrAD-V2 is basically an
extension of PredTrAD-V1. This is accomplished by apply-
ing the contracting path of the original U-Net architecture [11]
to the result of the transformer decoder, with the intention of
extracting specific temporary features from this complex repre-
sentation. As visualized in Figure 3, the green-highlighted part
corresponds to the PredTrAD-V2 component, where the win-
dow size of the decoder input w2 is set to w2 = 10, and the
model dimension dmodel is defined as dmodel = 512. The main
idea behind the more complex PredTrAD-V2 is to treat the out-
come of the transformer decoder as feature maps with the shape
Rb×c×w2×dmodel, where the additional variable c refers to the
number of channels. The initial configuration of the first fea-
ture map comprises a single channel. Subsequently, a contract-
ing step containing two consecutive convolutional layers that
process the input, followed by max pooling to reduce spatial

4.4. Anomaly Detection
During inference, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the
prediction and the ground truth value is calculated for each
timestamp and dimension.
These values are then used as
anomaly scores. As prior research has shown [6, 7, 9, 10, 12],
for a fair comparison, we use the Peak Over Threshold (POT)
method [8] that automatically determines the anomaly threshold
based on the respective anomaly scores, which is further used
to classify each timestamp as either anomalous or normal.

5.3. Cross-Entity Anomaly Detection
Previous experiments examined only the entity-based anomaly
detection that involves a single trained model for each entity. In
this experiment, we evaluate the ability of a single model to de-
tect anomalies across multiple entities. The core idea of this ex-
periment arose due to the high number of entities (35.559 pods)
of the TIKI dataset. The motivation behind this approach is that
cross-entity anomaly detection would significantly reduce the
maintenance effort associated with anomaly detection models
for a dataset comprising a vast number of entities. Given the
similarities between the TIKI and SMD dataset, particularly in
their monitored metrics, 8 entities of the SMD dataset are em-
ployed in this experiment to explore cross-entity anomaly de-
tection. The TIKI data corpus offers a comprehensive collection
of metrics, however, it was not utilized due to the limited avail-
ability of anomaly labels, making the extensively labeled SMD
dataset more suitable for evaluating anomaly detection perfor-
mance. Two key methodological improvements have been im-
plemented: First, we replace the original POT with Dynamic
Streaming Peak Over Threshold (SPOT) [8] for threshold se-
lection, as POT’s constant threshold based on both training and
test anomaly scores proved inadequate. Dynamic SPOT instead
provides an adaptive threshold derived only from test anomaly
scores. Secondly, the validation procedure was enhanced by
monitoring the models’ F1 scores on the validation dataset, di-
rectly evaluating their anomaly detection capabilities during the
validation phase. Due to hardware constraints, only TranAD,
DTAAD, and PredTrAD-V1 were optimized for the utilization
of Dynamic SPOT. The complex architecture of PredTrAD-
V2, combined with a lack of time and computational resources,
made an efficient threshold optimization too complex for the
time being. Table 4 presents the performance metrics across all

6. Conclusion and Future Work
Two new transformer-based methods,
PredTrAD-V1 and
PredTrAD-V2, were developed for multivariate time series
anomaly detection. Through three key experiments, their effec-
tiveness was thoroughly evaluated. In the comparative analysis,
PredTrAD-V2 demonstrated superior performance by outper-
forming existing methods, showing a 2.41% and 6.54% increase
in F1 score compared to TranAD and DTAAD respectively,
while significantly reducing the FPR to 0.76%. When tested on
the TIKI dataset, PredTrAD-V2 with min-max scaling achieved
an impressive F1 score of 95.50 %, demonstrating a 4.42% im-
provement over DTAAD and 12.48% over TranAD. In the final
experiment testing cross-entity anomaly detection, PredTrAD-
V1 emerged as the top performer with 89.24 % F1 score, sur-
passing TranAD by 16.81% and DTAAD by 15.13%. Due to its
lack of optimization for Dynamic SPOT, PredTrAD-V2 exhib-
ited elevated FN rates, while maintaining low false alarm rates
during threshold adjustment procedures. This work opens up
several promising avenues for future research. A more in-depth
comparative analysis of prediction-based versus reconstruction-
based strategies has the potential to provide valuable insights
into the underlying causes of observed performance differences.
Our hypothesis is that, given the original Transformer’s primary
objective–to predict the next element t + 1 based on past values
up to timestamp t–prediction-based methods may be more ef-
fective at capturing temporal dependencies and contextual sig-
nals, particularly in complex multivariate time series. Further-
more, the baseline experiment could be extended by investi-
gating additional datasets across different domains to evaluate
the anomaly detection performance in varied contexts. More-
over, optimizing the dynamic threshold may be a promising ap-
proach for the future. Furthermore, researchers may explore
the dynamic SPOT approach in greater detail, particularly fo-
cusing on optimizing the threshold procedure that distinguishes
between anomalous and normal data points. The entire source
code, all relevant experiment scripts and a subset of the TIKI
dataset are made publicly available on GitHub under BSD-3 li-
cense at https://github.com/jan-176/PredTrAD.
