Abstract
Birds produce complex vocalizations through the coordi-
nated dynamics of neuromuscular control, where the syrinx acts
as the sound source, and suprasyringeal vocal tract structures
function as acoustic ﬁlters. In this study, we developed com-
putational models of the avian syrinx and upper vocal tract, re-
constructed from high-resolution micro-CT imaging. Using ﬁ-
nite element analysis in COMSOL Multiphysics, we introduce
dual sources at the bottom of the trachea to analyze the ﬁlter-
ing mechanisms underlying biphonic vocalization. The simu-
lation results are experimentally validated using real bird vo-
calizations. To further elucidate the biomechanical factors gov-
erning resonance modulation, we systematically vary tracheal
length, glottal radius, and beak angle. Additionally, we perform
an analysis of syllable bandwidth to assess the inﬂuence of vo-
cal tract dynamics on the frequency range observed in biphonic
vocalizations.

1. Introduction
Bird sound is produced by the syrinx [1, 2], a specialized vo-
cal organ located at the junction of the trachea (windpipe) and
primary bronchi (air passages to the lungs). During expiration,
airﬂow induces vibrations in its thin membranes, generating the
fundamental acoustic signal. The pitch and tone of the pro-
duced sound are modulated by the dynamic tension and position
of these vibrating membranes. The complexity of avian vocal-
izations varies across species, depending on the neuromuscular
control of the syrinx. Oscine songbirds (e.g., canaries, robins)
possess highly developed intrinsic muscles that enable precise
control over phonation, allowing for rapid frequency modula-
tions and complex syllable structures. In contrast, suboscines
and non-passerine birds (e.g., crows) have a more rigid syrinx
with fewer control mechanisms, resulting in less intricate vo-
cal patterns. The primary sound generated by the syrinx under-
goes ﬁltering through the suprasyringeal vocal tract, including
the trachea, glottis, and beak, shaping the ﬁnal acoustic out-
put [3–6]. Birds capable of vibrating both sides of the syrinx
independently, known as biphonic or bilateral phonators, are
primarily songbirds (passerines). This ability allows them to
produce two distinct sounds simultaneously, either harmoniz-
ing with themselves or generating rapid, complex songs. No-
table examples include thrushes, canaries, mockingbirds, lyre-
birds, and starlings. Since both sides of their syrinx can function
independently, these birds can sing two notes at once, switch
frequencies rapidly, or produce continuous sound without in-
terruption. This vocal ﬂexibility results in more intricate and
acoustically diverse songs compared to species that utilize only
one side of the syrinx . The syrinx anatomy and spectrogram of

Computational models can replicate how variations in vo-
cal anatomy produce unique acoustic patterns [7], which serve
as species-speciﬁc signatures and aid in bioacoustic classiﬁ-
cation [8–19]. Commonly used electrical network models of-
ten neglect the inﬂuence of the organs’ actual shapes [3, 17].
In [20], researchers introduced a three-dimensional human vo-
cal tract model through two-dimensional ﬁnite element analy-
sis. The study [5] examined vocal imitation abilities in par-
rots and crows compared to humans, utilizing COMSOL Multi-
physics to investigate the impact of vocal tract dimensional vari-
ations on sound production. The study [21] employed micro-CT
imaging and 3D ﬁnite element models to simulate sound recep-
tion and auditory responses in Little Penguins, predicting their
audiometric range from 100 Hz to 10 kHz in both terrestrial
and underwater environments. Further, studies such as [22, 23]
explored tracheal elongation and formant production in birds,
demonstrating that vocal tract geometry signiﬁcantly inﬂuences
formant patterns, independent of body size. Two biomechanical
models of the syrinx for studying the onset of sound generation
and the control of higher harmonics are proposed in [24]. How-
ever, the analysis is conducted using a single unilateral pair of
labia. In contrast, we analyze the biphonic vocalization using
the anatomy of unilateral phonators (birds that vibrate only one
side of the syrinx at a time).
We developed a computational model of the syrinx and up-
per vocal tract, including the trachea, glottis, and beak, which
we refer to as the primary model, using COMSOL Multi-
physics. Later, we introduced two sources at the bottom of the
trachea to investigate the ﬁltering of biphonic vocalization. The
response is compared with real-time audio samples of the do-
mestic canary, which has a similar anatomical structure to that
of the house sparrow.

2.3. Implementation
In this study, we modelled the bird’s vocal tract resonance using
COMSOL Multiphysics’ pressure acoustics frequency domain
interface over the 1-8 kHz range typical of house sparrow vo-
calizations [31]. To investigate biphonic sound production and
modulation, we employed two sources, with the tracheal input
pressure (syrinx output) set to 1 Pa (94 dB). The simulations are
validated using real bird vocalizations. We also modiﬁed the
computational models to analyze the effects of tracheal length,
glottal radius, and beak angle on resonance. Additionally, sylla-
ble bandwidth analysis is performed to assess how variations in
vocal tract ﬂexibility inﬂuence the frequency range in biphonic
sound production.

3.2. Morphological Geometry and Sound Filtering
We modiﬁed the 3D geometry to examine the inﬂuence of tra-
cheal length, glottis radius, and beak angle on monophonic and
biphonic sound production. Previous studies reported tracheal
elongation of 3% in zebra ﬁnches [32] and 11% in white house
sparrows [33] during vocalization, causing minimal frequency
shifts. To assess the effects of tracheal length, we reduced the
initial tracheal length by 11% and 20% while maintaining a con-
sistent diameter of approximately 1 mm. The simulation results
for the monophonic model, shown in Figure 6, indicate a shift
in resonance frequency from 5.5 kHz to 5.58 kHz and 5.91 kHz
for the 11% and 20% shorter models, respectively. Similarly,

Although studies on the larynx are limited, it is known that
laryngeal muscle contraction reduces vocal tract diameter [34].
To simulate this effect, we varied the glottal radius and ana-
lyzed its impact on frequency. Figure 6, 7 illustrate the variation
in resonance with glottis radius for monophonic and biphonic
models. In the monophonic model, a glottis with a 0.5 mm ra-
dius exhibits an increase of 0.52 kHz compared to a 0.2 mm ra-
dius, as shown in Figure 6. For the biphonic model, which fea-
tures two resonant peaks, a glottis with a 0.5 mm radius shows
an increase of 0.49 kHz compared to a 0.2 mm radius for the
ﬁrst peak, and an increase of 0.40 kHz for the second peak, as
shown in Figure 7. This indicates that an increase in glottis ra-
dius leads to a rise in resonance frequency.

In Figure 8, resonant peaks shift to higher frequencies as
the beak angle increases from 10◦ to 20◦. For the monophonic
model, the resonance frequency rises from 5.5 kHz at a 10 ◦
angle to 5.7 kHz at 20◦. Similarly, in the biphonic model, the
ﬁrst peak increases from 5.2 kHz to 5.3 kHz at a 10 ◦ angle,
while the second peak increases from 6 kHz to 6.1 kHz at a 20◦
angle. This frequency shift indicates that a wider beak angle
increases the vocal tract’s opening, thereby altering its resonant
frequencies. The reduced length of the effective resonating tube
favors the transmission of higher frequencies [35].
3.3. Bandwidth of Syllables in Biphonic V ocalization
A syllable is the smallest unit of a bird’s song, typically a sin-
gle continuous sound produced within one breath cycle. The
arrangement of these syllables into a structured pattern forms a
phrase. In biphonic vocalization, a syllable contains two in-
dependent frequency components, while a phrase consists of
a sequence of syllables, each of which may be biphonic. Bi-
phonic vocalization often results in sideband frequencies and
subharmonics. Three birds, namely the domestic canary, wood
thrush, and northern cardinal, are selected from the Xeno-canto
database [36]. For each species, 10 ﬁles with variable durations
are used to analyze the bandwidth of syllables. Figure 9 shows
the bandwidth distribution and IQR (orange lines) across three
bird species, highlighting vocal tract ﬂexibility and biphonic
syllable production. The wood thrush exhibits the narrowest
bandwidth range (0.4–2.2 kHz, median 1.3 kHz) with the small-
est IQR of 0.7 kHz, indicating a more constrained vocal tract.
Both the domestic canary (0.5–3.5 kHz, IQR 1.1 kHz, median
2.2 kHz) and the northern cardinal (0.7–3.6 kHz, IQR 1.8 kHz,
median 2.5 kHz) demonstrate broader bandwidth ranges and
greater variability, suggesting more adaptable vocal tract con-
ﬁgurations. Their overlapping frequency ranges indicate high
vocal ﬂexibility, enabling them to produce a variety of sounds,
including biphonic vocalizations [37].
3.4. Insights and Future Scope
The literature reports that different articulated parts of the os-
cine vocal tract, including trachea, glottis, oropharyngeal cavity,
and beak, functions as an integrated system to shape vocaliza-
tions. Our model demonstrates how morphological changes in-
ﬂuence vocal tract resonance. By extending this framework, we
can synthesize species-speciﬁc bird calls, enabling comparative
analysis of vocal adaptation across taxa. Further, our model
though based on unilateral phonator anatomy, offers valuable
insights into bilateral phonation, where birds generate harmon-
ically complex sounds using independent sources. This opens
pathways for tonality studies, where harmonic decay patterns
can quantify species’ vocal characteristics. Future research
could integrate real-time vocal tract deformation, allowing the
generation of synthetic bird calls that closely resemble natural
vocalization. Additionally, cross-species modeling could help
investigate evolutionary constraints on vocal tract morphology,
shedding light on how birds optimize their acoustic signals for
communication, mating, and territorial behavior.
4. Conclusion
Our study demonstrates that real domestic canary vocalizations
exhibit two resonant peaks in the mid-frequency range, align-
ing with the simulation results of our biphonic computational
model. We examined how variations in tracheal length, glottal
radius, and beak angle inﬂuence resonance, ﬁnding that shorter
tracheal lengths and wider beak angles favor higher frequen-
cies. Bandwidth analysis further reveals that the northern cardi-
nal and domestic canary possess the most ﬂexible vocal tracts,
allowing them to produce a wider range of frequencies and more
complex vocalizations, including biphonic sounds. These ﬁnd-
ings underscore the role of vocal tract morphology in shaping
avian vocalizations.
