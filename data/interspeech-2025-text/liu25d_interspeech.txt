HWB-Net: A Novel High-Performance and Efficient Hybrid Waveform
Bandwidth Extension Method
Xin Liu, Shulin He, Xueliang Zhang∗
College of Computer Science, Inner Mongolia University, China
xin.liu@mail.imu.edu.cn, heshulin@mail.imu.edu.cn, cszxl@imu.edu.cn
Abstract
Speech Bandwidth Extension (BWE) reconstructs missing
high-frequency components in narrowband signals to enhance
perceptual quality.
Conventional signal-processing methods
face performance limitations, while deep-learning approaches
require substantial computational resources. To address these
issues, we present HWB-Net, a novel Hybrid Waveform Band-
width network that combines half-wave rectified signals with
raw waveforms through a lightweight architecture, with a
lightweight architecture, reducing both complexity and cost.
Specifically, integrating half-wave rectification into BAE-NET-
Lite enhances the model’s understanding of speech signals,
leading to improved subjective and objective results, and re-
placing the decoder with a simple linear layer plus a weighted
Gaussian mixture model (WGMM) significantly cuts down pa-
rameters (0.20M) and computational complexity (0.013G/s).
Evaluations demonstrate HWB-Net’s competitive performance
and practical viability for real-time communication through bal-
anced efficiency-accuracy tradeoffs.
Index Terms: bandwidth extension, half wave rectification,
adaptive bandwidth extension, low-complexity, magnitude in-
painting
1. Introduction
Bandwidth extension (BWE), a critical technology for im-
proving speech quality in applications like telecommunications
and voice assistants, has garnered significant research atten-
tion. Early signal-processing-based methods established foun-
dational frameworks through various approaches: spectral en-
velope modeling (e.g., Linear Predictive Coding [1]), statistical
mapping (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Models [2], Hidden Markov
Models [3]), and nonlinear signal processing techniques includ-
ing harmonic generation via octave extension [4], full/half-wave
rectification [5], chaotic prediction-based reconstruction [6],
and phase space reconstruction [7]. While these methods pi-
oneered high-frequency reconstruction by exploiting low-band
correlations, their reliance on statistical assumptions and sim-
plified signal models often limited high-band reconstruction ac-
curacy, particularly in complex acoustic environments.
Recent advances in deep learning have spawned diverse
neural-network-based bandwidth extension (BWE) models,
which primarily fall into two paradigms. Frequency-domain
approaches [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] learn spectral mappings,
exemplified by AERO’s complex-spectrum recovery mecha-
nism for simultaneous magnitude and phase reconstruction
[12]. Time-domain methods [15, 16, 17, 18, 19] directly process
waveforms through architectures like AudioUnet’s CNNs [18],
∗Corresponding author.
TF-Net’s hybrid time-frequency fusion [19], and SEANet’s
fully convolutional framework [17]. Despite their effectiveness,
both paradigms suffer from prohibitive computational costs and
model sizes, severely limiting practical deployment.
BAE-Net[13] features a dual-branch architecture compris-
ing a Magnitude Inpainting Network (MI-Net) and a Phase
Refinement Network (PR-Net).
The MI-Net handles high-
frequency magnitude inpainting for speech signals with vari-
able bandwidth, while the PR-Net optimizes the flipped phase,
enhanced by an information interaction module for cross-
component coordination.
Its lightweight variant, BAE-Net-
lite, prioritizes magnitude inpainting using the flipped phase
for waveform reconstruction. Despite lower computational de-
mands compared to other models, BAE-Net-lite remains chal-
lenging to deploy on resource-constrained edge devices.
In machine learning frameworks such as BAE-Net and AP-
BWE[20], high-frequency components in model inputs exhibit
limited information density due to direct upsampling from low-
sampling-rate speech signals.
Naturally, it occurs to us that
we can use some useful signals to fill this gap.
Previously,
a lot of work has been done to extrapolate high-frequency in-
formation through [5, 21, 22]. Among these, we adopt Half-
Wave Rectification (HWR)[5], a foundational nonlinear opera-
tor that selectively retains the positive or negative half-cycle of
the waveform. This choice is motivated by its algorithmic sim-
plicity, computational efficiency, and interpretability, making
it particularly suitable for preliminary exploration in resource-
constrained scenarios.
Against this backdrop, we integrate traditional half - wave
rectification into BAE-Net. By applying it to the input speech
signal, we can access additional valuable information that ex-
isting methods have overlooked. This new - found information
enriches the model’s understanding of speech signal character-
istics, leading to a more accurate speech signal reconstruction.
Through experiments, this novel utilization of half-wave rectifi-
cation has been proven to significantly improve the model’s per-
formance. Notably, it plays a crucial role in enhancing the de-
tails of high-frequency components, which are vital for achiev-
ing high-quality perceptual speech.
In addition, we have made substantial modifications to the
decoder part of the network. Specifically, we replaced the origi-
nal decoder with a simple linear layer combined with Weighted
Gaussian Mixture Models (WGMM). This redesigned decoder
not only effectively reduces the number of parameters but also
significantly decreases the computational complexity. The re-
duction in parameters is of great significance as it allows the
model to be more easily deployed on edge devices with limited
computational resources. For example, in our experiments, the
new decoder architecture has shown a significant reduction in
memory usage and computational time compared to the original

2. Methodology
2.1. Overview
The overall diagram of the proposed system is illustrated in Fig-
ure 1, which is based on BAE-Net and incorporates two main
improvements. First, we apply half-wave rectification to the
low-resolution (LR) input signal in the time domain to augment
high-frequency information. Second, we replace the decoder of
BAE-Net with a module based on the weighted Gaussian mix-
ture model (WGMM), called the WGMM Module, to output
the result of half-wave rectification multiplied by the Weighted
Density Function (wdf).

2.4. Training and Loss Function
Our model employs the same loss function and GAN structure
as BAE-Net to ensure effective recovery of the HR magnitude
spectrum and phase information. Specifically, the loss function
includes waveform loss, multiresolution STFT loss, adversarial
loss, and feature matching loss.
Waveform Loss: The waveform loss Lwav measures the dif-
ference between the generated waveform and the real waveform
to match the overall shape and the phase.
Multi-Resolution
STFT Loss: The multi-resolution STFT loss Lstft inspired by
[23, 24, 25] is introduced to improve the perceptually-related
auditory quality, which is a combination of the spectral con-
vergence loss and the logarithmic magnitude spectral L1 loss
with different FFT analysis parameters. In our experiments, the
number of FFT bins is set to {512, 1024, 2048}, the hop length
is set to {50, 120, 240}, and the window size is set to {240,
600, 1200}. Adversarial Loss: The adversarial loss Ladv is used
to stabilize the competitive relationship between multiple dis-

average least-square loss (RaLSGAN) proposed in [26]. The

3. Dataset & Implementation
Our experimental setup is designed to address speech super-
resolution tasks, inspired by the BAE-Net framework, with spe-
cific adaptations to meet our research objectives. We evaluated
our approach using the widely recognized VCTK data set [28],
which contains approximately 44 hours of clean speech sam-
pled at 48 kHz from 110 speakers. For training, we utilize data
from 108 speakers, reserving the remaining 2 speakers for test-
ing.
3.1. Data Preprocessing
Our methodology specifically targets bandwidth extension un-
der low-power edge computing constraints, where hardware
limitations typically enforce low sampling rates (4-6 kHz
range). This operational context necessitates particular atten-
tion to spectral recovery quality in sub-6kHz sampling scenar-
ios.
Input speech signals undergo STFT preprocessing us-
ing a Hann window with 512-sample frame length and 256-
sample shift. The 1536-point STFT configuration yields 769-
dimensional spectral features, optimizing frequency resolution
while maintaining temporal coherence. LR signals are gener-
ated from HR counterparts through a Butterworth low-pass fil-
ter (2-3 kHz cutoff, order:5-10). This processing chain creates
matched LR-HR pairs where LR signals represent band-limited
inputs and HR signals serve as reconstruction targets.

3.3. Evaluation Metrics
The performance of the model is evaluated through multiple
metrics.
The Log-Spectral Distance (LSD) is employed to
quantify spectral differences. For the objective and auditory
assessment of speech quality, the PESQ metric [30] is uti-
lized.
Moreover, deep-learning-based metrics such as DNS-
MOS P.808 [31] and NISQA [32, 33] are adopted for a com-
prehensive evaluation of speech quality.
4. Results & Analysis
4.1. Overall Performance Comparison
Table 1 demonstrates HWB-Net’s state-of-the-art efficiency-
quality tradeoffs. With merely 0.20M parameters, our model
achieves 3.35 DNSMOS score, outperforming the 19.41M-
parameter AERO system by 1.8% while reducing computa-
tional cost to 0.013G MACs (37.1% of BAE-Lite). The PESQ
improvement of 2.2% over BAE-Lite-Small (2.85 vs 2.63) com-
bined with superior NISQA performance (3.86 vs 3.59 in com-
parable configurations) confirms the effectiveness of our hybrid
waveform bandwidth extension design.
4.2. Component Ablation Study
As quantified in Table 2, the half-wave rectification (HWR)
module improves the DNSMOS score by 3.06% (3.27→3.37)
and slightly enhances NISQA (3.74→3.76) without additional
computational cost.
Subsequent integration of the softplus-
constrained WGMM achieves dual benefits: a 64.91% parame-
ter reduction (0.57M→0.20M) with minimal quality degrada-
tion (DNSMOS 3.37→3.35), while the 5.62% LSD increase
(0.89→0.94) reflects our systematic design trade-off. Notably,
this architectural refinement simultaneously elevates NISQA by
2.66% (3.76→3.86), demonstrating effective perceptual qual-
ity improvement despite spectral matching constraints.
The
WGMM architecture’s softplus-constrained linear superposi-
tion inherently limits exact spectral matching but implements
perceptually-guided component selection through:
(1) non-
negative weighting for noise floor suppression via attenuated
low-weight components, and (2) adaptive emphasis through rel-
ative weight magnitudes that theoretically enforce energy con-
centration in dominant spectral regions. This framework main-
tains robust quality metrics with PESQ at 2.85 versus baseline
2.83 and achieves superior NISQA performance (3.86 vs. 3.59-
3.74 in comparable models), demonstrating effective balance
between model efficiency and perceptual preservation.
5. Conclusions & Future Work
Our work advances speech bandwidth extension through two
key innovations: (1) The novel integration of half-wave rectifi-
cation in hybrid waveform models achieves measurable qual-
ity enhancement without computational overhead, establish-
ing HWR as an efficient perceptual optimization mechanism;
(2) The proposed WGMM architecture enables unprecedented
model efficiency (0.2M parameters, 0.013G MACs) through
strategic component selection while maintaining perceptual fi-
delity. This dual-mechanism framework demonstrates system-
atic prioritization of perceptual quality over spectral matching
precision, confirming its viability for resource-constrained real-
time applications. The balanced quality-efficiency tradeoffs set
a new benchmark for practical bandwidth extension systems.
Three promising directions emerge from this research: (1)
Hybrid signal processing integrating nonlinear high-frequency
extrapolation techniques with data-driven models to enhance
spectral diversity; (2) Distributional refinement through task-
specific mixture components, potentially replacing Gaussian
bases with heavy-tailed distributions (Laplacian/Exponential
mixtures) better suited for vocal tract excitation patterns;
(3) multi-scale probabilistic architectures using parallel Gaus-
sian mixtures with varied initial parameters {µk, σ2
