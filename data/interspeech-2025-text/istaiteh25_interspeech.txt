Beyond Similarity Scoring: Detecting Entailment and Contradiction in
Multilingual and Multimodal Contexts
Othman Istaiteh, Salima Mdhaffar, Yannick Est`eve
LIA, Avignon University, France
{firstname.lastname}@univ-avignon.fr
Abstract
Natural Language Inference (NLI) determines whether a
hypothesis entails, contradicts, or is neutral with respect to a
premise. While text-based NLI is well-studied, its multimodal
and multilingual extension remains underexplored. This paper
introduces a multilingual, multimodal NLI framework classi-
fying entailment, contradiction, and neutrality across text-text,
text-speech, speech-text, and speech-speech pairs in same- and
cross-lingual settings. A key motivation is improving transla-
tion assessment, where similarity-based approaches may miss
contradictions. The framework complements evaluation meth-
ods and helps identify inconsistencies by detecting entailment
and contradiction alongside semantic similarity. It also extends
text-based datasets with speech-text and speech-speech pairs
for multilingual multimodal inference.1 Experiments show the
model outperforms BLASER in distinguishing entailment from
non-entailment, achieving F1 gains of 0.19 in speech-speech
and 0.13 in speech-text.

1. Introduction
NLI determines the logical relationship between a premise and
a hypothesis. The premise is a given statement, and the hypoth-
esis is evaluated against it. The relationship can be entailment
(the hypothesis follows from the premise), contradiction (it con-
flicts with the premise), or neutral (it is unrelated or ambigu-
ous) [1, 2]. NLI is used in tasks like information extraction and
virtual assistants [3, 4]. Multilingual and multimodal NLI ex-
tends this capability to spoken and translated sentences, where
implicit meaning shifts and contradictions can impact evalua-
tion. In translation, bidirectional entailment [5] ensures that the
translated and original sentences support each other and detects
translations that contradict the original speech. Beyond trans-
lation, NLI plays a crucial role in speech recognition, where
phonetic variations and transcription errors can introduce con-
tradictions. This is particularly relevant in morphologically rich
languages like Arabic, where minor phonetic changes can alter
meaning and impact transcription accuracy.
Early NLI methods used syntactic and lexical features but
struggled with semantic relationships [1, 6]. The shift to deep
learning, particularly LSTM and attention-based mechanisms,
enabled more robust semantic modeling and significantly im-
proved inference performance [7]. Transformers advanced NLI
using pre-trained contextualized representations. Models like
BERT [8] and RoBERTa [9] improved performance by captur-
ing semantic relationships more effectively. This progress ex-
tended to multilingual models, such as mT5 [10] and mGPT

Specialized models have further addressed challenges
in cross-lingual representation learning:
No Language Left
Behind (NLLB) [12] enhances low-resource language perfor-
mance, while LaBSE [13] improves multilingual retrieval by
producing language-agnostic sentence embeddings. Together,
these advancements have broadened the scope of NLI across
languages and modalities.
Expanding NLI to speech introduces challenges like accent
variations, diverse patterns, and noise. Self-supervised mod-
els like Wav2vec 2.0 [14] and HuBERT [15] generate high-
dimensional representations that capture phonetic and linguistic
features. However, ensuring alignment between speech and text
embeddings is critical for multimodal NLI. SONAR [16] refines
speech encoders using NLLB, aligning speech outputs with text
embeddings at the sentence level.
Similarly, SAMU-XLSR
[17] leverages LaBSE for cross-lingual speech-text alignment.
These models align speech and text in a shared space, improv-
ing multimodal inference.
Cosine similarity compares sentence embeddings based on
their angular difference [18, 19]. Other metrics include Eu-
clidean distance, dot product similarity, and learned similarity
functions [20, 21]. BLASER 2.0 [22, 23], a three-layer dense
neural network, computes semantic similarity between speech
and text using a neural similarity function. It is trained with
supervised learning on cross-lingual semantic textual similar-
ity (XSTS) data [24], which provides human-annotated scores
from 1 (unrelated) to 5 (fully equivalent). This training aligns
BLASER’s similarity scores with human judgments.
In the
SeamlessM4T system, BLASER 2.0 ranks and retrieves match-
ing speech and text segments for speech-text alignment and
translation evaluation [22].
Although these metrics measure overall similarity, they
may assign high scores to sentences with high lexical overlap
but opposite meanings. In our own evaluation, BLASER gives a
4.79/5 similarity score to the sentences “The weather is sunny”
and “The weather is not sunny” despite their contradiction. De-
tecting contradiction prevents misleading similarity scores from
affecting retrieval and evaluation. Explicit entailment detection
filters mismatches, improving retrieval and avoiding contradic-
tory pairs in multilingual and multimodal settings.
This study presents a framework extending NLI to multilin-
gual and multimodal contexts using semantic representations.
It detects entailment and contradiction across input types: text-
text, text-speech, speech-text, and speech-speech, within same-
and cross-lingual settings. Unlike similarity-based methods, it
explicitly classifies entailment, contradiction, and neutrality. It
incorporates data generation for speech-text and speech-speech
NLI pairs, aiding multimodal datasets. This approach applies
to translation assessment, speech recognition, and multilingual
multimodal retrieval.

2. Methodology
This NLI framework supports multilingual and multimodal
inputs across text-text, text-speech, speech-text, and speech-
speech in same- and cross-lingual settings. Text-text spans 15
languages; speech-text and speech-speech include Arabic, En-
glish, and French. First, input pairs are encoded into a shared
semantic space for consistent cross-lingual and cross-modal
representation. This uses a single encoder for speech and text or
multiple aligned encoders for a unified representation. A fully
connected neural network with a softmax layer classifies rela-
tionships as entailment, contradiction, or neutral.
2.1. Feature Combination of Embeddings
The framework applies feature combination techniques from
the BLASER architecture to process premise and hypothesis
embeddings.
After encoding input pairs into a shared se-
mantic space, their embeddings are combined using element-
wise subtraction (to highlight differences in meaning), element-
wise multiplication (to capture interactions and shared seman-
tic information), and concatenation (to merge the original, sub-
tracted, and multiplied embeddings into a single vector). The
resulting feature vector encodes the premise-hypothesis rela-
tionship and has a dimensionality four times that of a single
input embedding.
2.2. Classification
The classification module uses the combined feature vector to
predict the premise-hypothesis relationship. A dense neural net-
work classifier with a softmax layer assigns probabilities to the
three NLI classes: entailment, contradiction, and neutral. By di-
rectly modeling these relationships, the framework detects en-
tailment and contradiction across different modalities and lan-
guages. It also distinguishes contradiction from entailment in
cases with high lexical overlap, where similarity-based models
like BLASER may struggle.
The classification network handles text-text, text-speech,
speech-text, and speech-speech inputs within and across lan-
guages.
It directly models logical relationships between in-
puts, providing a key advantage over regression-based similar-
ity models. Figure 1 illustrates the proposed framework, outlin-
ing the process from input encoding to classification. The blue
blocks represent the original BLASER architecture, while the
red blocks indicate the newly introduced components. This ca-
pability benefits translation assessment, where entailment com-
plements high semantic scores, and information retrieval, where
logical consistency is essential.
3. Experiments
We conducted experiments to evaluate the proposed frame-
work’s ability to classify entailment, contradiction, and neutral
relationships across text-text, text-speech, and speech-speech
inputs. The primary task was multiclass classification, distin-
guishing among the three labels. Additionally, we performed
binary classification, where entailment was treated as the pos-
itive class, and contradiction and neutral were combined into
a single negative class for comparison against BLASER. The
experiments also examined the impact of generated data and
compared performance on synthesized and natural speech.

3.1. Data Preparation
The data preparation consists of two parts. The first utilizes ex-
isting datasets, including SNLI [1], XNLI [25], and FLEURS
[26]. The second generates additional multimodal and multilin-
gual NLI data using synthetic techniques to extend the datasets
into new modalities and languages.
3.1.1. Datasets Utilized
This work uses three publicly available datasets in two cate-
gories:
Text Datasets: The SNLI and XNLI corpora are used for
NLI tasks. SNLI consists of English premise-hypothesis pairs
labeled as entailment, contradiction, or neutral. XNLI extends
NLI to 15 languages, with premise and hypothesis in the same
language. This work uses the English subset of XNLI for train-
ing and the multilingual subset for development and testing.
Speech Dataset: The FLEURS dataset contains aligned
text and speech data for 102 languages, with 11,000 samples
per language. Each sample includes a sentence translation and
its corresponding audio recording. This work focuses on En-
glish, French, and Arabic pairs.
3.1.2. Data Generation
We used a two-step process to extend NLI text data to a multi-
modal context, generating speech-text and speech-speech pairs
from existing text-text pairs. Figure 2 illustrates the process.

Text-text Pairs: We fine-tuned the Mistral 7B v3 model
[27] on the English portions of SNLI and XNLI. The model gen-
erated premise-hypothesis pairs for entailment, contradiction,
and neutral labels. Hypotheses for the English text in FLEURS
were paired with their Arabic and French translations, forming
English-English, English-Arabic, and English-French text-text
NLI pairs.
Speech-text Pairs:
English NLI-generated pairs were
aligned with the corresponding FLEURS speech, forming
English-English, English-Arabic, and English-French speech-
text NLI pairs.
Speech-speech Pairs: We used the VITS multispeaker [28]
implementation of the Coqui open-source framework [29] to
generate speech-speech pairs. The 109 available speakers were
split into training (77), validation (12), and testing (20), match-
ing FLEURS proportions. Using VITS, we generated speech for
NLI-generated pairs and paired it with corresponding FLEURS
speech, forming English-English, English-Arabic, and English-
French speech-speech pairs. The generated speech was also
paired with its corresponding text, adding more speech-text
data. Whisper v3 [30] generated transcriptions for the TTS-
generated speech, with an average Word Error Rate (WER) of
6.9%. To ensure quality, outputs with WER above 20% were fil-
tered out, removing 884 of 10,677 samples, resulting in a final
dataset with an average WER of 3.8%.
The SNLI and XNLI datasets, along with the generated
data, are balanced across the three NLI labels: entailment, con-
tradiction, and neutral. In contrast, the control set contains only
entailment examples, including text-text and speech-text trans-
lations, as well as text paired with synthetic speech from TTS.

3.2. Training Setup
The training process integrates SONAR embeddings for a uni-
fied representation of text and speech, ensuring alignment
across languages and modalities. Each sentence, regardless of
modality or language, is encoded into a 1024-dimensional vec-
tor. SONAR uses a single text encoder for all languages and
separate speech encoders for each language. This alignment
makes SONAR suitable for text-text, text-speech, and speech-
speech experiments.
The classifier retains BLASER’s three-layer dense neural
network but replaces the regression output with a three-node
softmax layer to classify sentence pairs as entailment, contra-
diction, or neutral.
The input vector is formed by concate-
nating the two 1024-dimensional sentence embeddings, along
with their element-wise subtraction and multiplication, captur-
ing their relationship. The resulting 4096-dimensional vector
is fed into a three-layer dense network with hidden dimensions
of 3072, 1536, and 3. The first two layers use the tanh activa-
tion function, while the last applies softmax. Training employs
a dropout rate of 0.1, a batch size of 2048, a learning rate of
1 × 10−3, and 1000 epochs, using cross-entropy loss.

3.3. Binary Classification: Baseline Comparison
BLASER serves as the baseline due to its semantic scoring
across multiple modalities, providing a meaningful comparison
for our framework, which explicitly models entailment, contra-
diction, and neutrality.
To evaluate the framework’s performance and enable a di-
rect comparison with the BLASER model, we created a binary
classification task. The original task consists of three classes:
entailment, contradiction, and neutral. We converted it to bi-
nary classification, treating entailment as positive and merging
contradiction and neutral into one negative class. This binary
setup allowed us to assess the model’s ability to distinguish en-
tailment from non-entailment relationships.
The BLASER model predicts semantic similarity scores on
a scale from 1 to 5. We used F1-Micro, calculating precision
and recall globally across all classes without assigning weight
to any class. F1-Micro is equivalent to F1-Binary for this binary
classification task, as it aggregates precision and recall across
both classes. Since BLASER is a regression model and does
not require a threshold, we applied a threshold of 3.9, selected
to maximize the F1 Score on the development set, enabling a
direct comparison with our approach.
Table 2 presents the results, showing that our model consis-
tently outperforms BLASER across all test splits in terms of F1-
Micro. These results highlight the framework’s effectiveness
in distinguishing entailment from non-entailment with greater
precision. Our model offers a complementary translation and
transcription evaluation tool, where a high semantic score must
align with a clear entailment relationship. This capability is
also valuable for information extraction, ensuring that extracted
content does not contradict the input sentence.
As a comparison by example, Table 3 demonstrates a case
where BLASER assigns a higher similarity score to a contra-
dictory pair than to a correct translation. While both hypothe-
ses are in French, only the first one (‘J’adore le chocolat.’) is
a valid translation of ‘I love chocolate,’ yet BLASER assigns
a higher similarity score (4.49) to the contradiction. In con-
trast, our model correctly classifies the first as entailment and
the second as a contradiction, demonstrating its ability to detect
meaning differences beyond surface similarity.

4. Conclusion
This paper introduced a multilingual and multimodal NLI
framework that classifies relationships across text-text, speech-
text, and speech-speech pairs in both same-language and cross-
lingual settings.
Using shared semantic representations, the
framework models entailment, contradiction, and neutrality,
overcoming the limitations of similarity-based models.
Experiments demonstrated that the proposed model out-
performs BLASER in distinguishing entailment from non-
entailment, with notable improvements of 0.19 F1 in speech-
speech classification and 0.13 F1 in speech-text classification.
Multiclass experiments highlighted the effectiveness of combin-
ing generated and natural data for improving multimodal NLI
tasks. The framework identifies contradictions, offering addi-
tional insights for translation and speech evaluation alongside
semantic similarity in retrieval and assessment. Additionally,
the generated dataset supports ongoing research in multimodal
NLI and cross-lingual inference.
However, performance on speech-text (F1 = 0.76) and
speech-speech (F1 = 0.77) in the three-class NLI classification
indicates room for improvement. While text-based evaluation
was conducted on real data, speech-text, and speech-speech
tasks relied primarily on generated text and synthesized speech.
Further validation of real-world spoken data would strengthen
the model’s applicability.
