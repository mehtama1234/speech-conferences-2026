Rapport-Building Dialogue Strategies for Deeper Connection: Integrating
Proactive Behavior, Personalization, and Aizuchi Backchannels
Muhammad Yeza Baihaqi1,2,3, Angel Garc´ıa Contreras2,1, Seiya Kawano4,2, Koichiro Yoshino3,2,1
1Nara Institute of Science and Technology, Japan; 2Guardian Robot Project, RIKEN, Japan;
3Institute of Science Tokyo, Japan; 4Kyoto Institute of Technology, Japan

seiya.kawano@riken.jp, koichiro@c.titech.ac.jp
Abstract
Proactive behavior, dialogue personalization, and backchannels
are essential elements for enhancing rapport, fostering engage-
ment, preventing conversation stalls, and supporting a natu-
ral dialogue flow. This study integrates these behaviors into
a rapport-building dialogue strategy using the CO-STAR and
few-shot frameworks to prompt large language models (LLMs)
within a human-robot interaction. The success of the integra-
tion was assessed through metrics such as conversation stall
frequency, dialogue similarity, and backchannel usage by the
robot.
We then observed the effect of integrating these ele-
ments on participant-observed behavioral outcomes, including
participant backchannel usage, self-disclosure, and subjective
questionnaire responses.
Experimental results indicated that
incorporating these behaviors into the dialogue strategy posi-
tively impacts human-robot rapport both behaviorally and sub-
jectively.
Index Terms: Backchannels, personalization, proactive behav-
ior, rapport-building dialogue strategy
1. Introduction
Rapport is defined as a state of mutual understanding, trust,
and connection between individuals during an interaction [1].
Building rapport through shared understanding, trust, and en-
gagement is essential for effective human-agent collaboration
[2]. Baihaqi et al. introduced an early rapport-building dialogue
strategy (RBDS) that employs specific rapport-promoting utter-
ances—such as praise, self-disclosure, and storytelling [3]. This
strategy is implemented within large language models (LLMs)
through a free-form dialogue approach, designed to facilitate
rapport during first meeting small talk.
However, without a specific strategy, the agent still faces
challenges in sustaining longer conversations with passive par-
ticipants. To prevent conversation stalls and effectively build
rapport, it is important to personalize the dialogue by proac-
tively collecting and utilizing the interlocutor’s preferences
while maintaining their engagement to construct responses [4].
Incorporating backchanneling, such as aizuchi (interjections in-
dicating active listening in Japanese conversation), can further
enhance interaction by fostering engaging conversation [5].
In this study, we sought to improve the RBDS by integrating
proactive behavior, personalization, and aizuchi. This enhance-
ment can be achieved by restructuring the prompt. By revamp-
ing the prompt structure with CO-STAR [6, 7], the agent gains
a clear understanding of context, objectives, and tone, allowing
it to take initiative in conversation and respond dynamically to
user cues. This structure empowers the agent to personalize di-
alogue by adapting its style and responses to the user’s interests,
creating a more engaging and tailored interaction. In the few-
shot examples, we explicitly included aizuchi usage to help the
LLM learn how to incorporate these interjections.
We integrated this dialogue system into a conversational
robot, CommU, and compared the proposed strategy with var-
ious LLMs, against the baselines. Finally, we evaluated the
impact of these behavioral modifications on human subjective
responses and behavioral outcomes to assess improvements in
rapport-building.
2. Related works
2.1. Proactive behavior and dialogue personalization
Proactive behavior is a crucial factor in enhancing interactions
and preventing negative outcomes [8]. In provider-customer
relationships, it is essential to build rapport and avoid service
failure [9]. Similarly, in coach-athlete relationships, proactive
behavior supports positive rapport and engagement [10].
In
dialogue, proactive behavior involves introducing new topics
through questions or statements to prevent dialogue stalls.
When making questions and statements, Humans are nat-
urally adjusted based on the interlocutor, making interactions
personalized and unique [11]. In investigative interviews, per-
sonalized conversations have been shown to foster higher rap-
port and increase self-disclosure [4]. Considering these effects,
we integrate proactive behavior and personalized dialogue into
the system’s responses to prevent conversational stalls and en-
sure alignment with the user’s interests.
2.2. Aizuchi in Japanese conversation
In Japanese conversation, aizuchi refers to a short verbal re-
sponse that listeners use to signal attentiveness, understanding,
and emotional responsiveness [12, 13, 14]. Aizuchi fosters a
sense of unity and alignment within the conversation, helping
to establish rapport and maintain relational harmony [5]. When
aizuchi is absent, it may give the impression of disinterest, po-
tentially weakening the rapport [12].
Aizuchi can be classified into several types [12].
Non-
lexical (NL) aizuchi, like “うん” (un - yes) or “ええ” (ee -
yes), uses to indicate attentiveness. Short responses (SR), such
as “そうですね” (sou desu ne - that is right), signal understand-
ing or agreement, while repetition (R) aizuchi involves echoing
keywords to affirm the speaker’s message. Combination aizuchi
blends these forms, as in “うん、そうですね” (un, sou desu
ne - yes, that is right), to convey deeper engagement.
Previous research has incorporated aizuchi in dialogue sys-
tems [15, 16], primarily focusing on non-lexical aizuchi and
short responses. In contrast, this study designs a dialogue sys-
tem that integrates various aizuchi types and investigates their
impact on human responses.

3. Proposed methods
3.1. Integrating proactivity, personalization, and aizuchi
into RBDS
The rapport-building dialogue strategy incorporated rapport-
fostering utterances from human interactions, such as praise,
storytelling, and jokes [3]. These elements were integrated into
a free-form dialogue approach by prompting the LLM before
the small talk, enabling the LLM to generate responses aligned
with the initial prompt throughout the dialogue.
The original RBDS instructed the LLM to use both open-
ended and short questions to maintain dialogue continuity.
However, this approach was ineffective, as the conversation fal-
tered when participants were passive. Furthermore, although
the LLM attempted to engage users using 5W+1H questions to
gauge user interests, it did not achieve the intended level of per-
sonalization. This was possibly attributed to the prompt’s com-
plexity and excessive commands, which made it difficult for the
LLM to discern the focal point, leading to a loss of context [17].
To elicit proactive behavior and personalize dialogue, we
revamped the prompt using the CO-STAR framework, which in-
corporates Context, Objective, Style, Tone, Audience, and Re-
sponse components [6, 7]. Instead of adding additional com-
mands that could complicate the prompt and reduce its clarity,
this structured approach provides a clearer focal point for the
LLM [18]. It also ensures that all key aspects are considered
when generating the LLM’s response, effectively aligning the
output with the goal [19, 20].
The prompt structure is as follows:
• Context: The context is set to engage users in small talk
about a specific dream travel destination, aiming to create a
positive and engaging conversation.
• Objective: The primary goal is to build rapport by delivering
a light, informal, and personalized conversation in Japanese,
ensuring it aligns with the user’s preferences and interests.
• Style: The conversation style is casual, friendly, and natural,
resembling the tone of a chatty travel blogger, which helps
make the dialogue relatable and approachable.
• Tone: The tone is light-hearted, enthusiastic, and empathetic
to ensure a relaxed atmosphere for the user.
• Audience: The target audience consists of young adults who
are interested in travel, preferring informal, inspiring, and en-
gaging conversations about the destinations.
• Response: The responses should integrate rapport-building
strategies and sustain engagement through follow-up ques-
tions and personalized details.
We also aimed to incorporate aizuchi backchannels into the
responses. To achieve this, we employed few-shot learning by
providing the LLM with two Japanese conversations that effec-
tively utilized aizuchi. This approach enabled the model to bet-
ter integrate these conversational cues.
3.2. Multimodal conversational robots
We embedded our dialogue systems into a robot developed by
Vstone, CommU [21]. The robot was equipped with automatic
speech recognition (ASR), text-to-speech (TTS), and predefined
gesture modules, enabling it to function as a dialogue robot.
3.2.1. Robot types
This study evaluates four dialogue robots designed to build rap-
port in small talk over 28 turns discussing travel destinations.
The robots are as follows:
• CSFS4o: Uses the CO-STAR and few-shot framework with
GPT-4o to elicit RBDS, focusing on proactive engagement,
personalization, and increased use of aizuchi backchannels.
• CSFS35: Implements the same frameworks as CSFS4o but
with GPT-3.5 Turbo. Comparing CSFS4o and CSFS35 al-
lows us to evaluate the impact of model differences.
• ORG35: Applies the original RBDS [3] with GPT-3.5 Turbo,
without specific strategies for proactive engagement, person-
alization, or aizuchi backchannels, allowing us to evaluate
whether the addition of these behaviors enhances rapport.
• ORG4o: Applies the original RBDS with GPT-4o, enabling
the assessment of whether improvements in dialogue quality
stem from the LLM’s enhanced capabilities or the inclusion
of proactive engagement, personalization, and backchannels.
GPT-4o offers advantages in context management and
prompt comprehension, enabling it to handle longer contexts
and interpret longer prompts more effectively [22]. It is also
perceived as more human-like, making its responses seem nat-
ural and expressive [23, 24]. Its broader knowledge base also
enhances its ability to generate creative and engaging responses.
In contrast, GPT-3.5 Turbo is a smaller model with less abil-
ity to handle complex prompts. It was found to be semantically
less rich, less emotional, and had less knowledge compared to
its successor [25, 26, 27], resulting in less engaging responses.
3.3. Metrics evaluation
To demonstrate the successful integration of proactive behavior,
personalization, and aizuchi, we used the following metrics:
1. Conversation stall: Conversation stall occurs when partici-
pants begin to provide brief responses, and the robot starts re-
peating similar responses due to a lack of new topics. Proac-
tive robot behavior is expected to mitigate these stalls.
2. Dialogue similarities: Personalized dialogues are expected
to have lower similarity due to user-specific responses. We
measured the similarity by vectorizing each conversation and
calculating the cosine similarity between dialogues.
3. Aizuchi usage of robot: We analyzed the frequency and type
of aizuchi used by the robot.
We also analyzed the impact of integrating proactive be-
havior, personalization, and aizuchi on participant engagement
using the following metrics:
1. Aizuchi usage of participant: The frequency and types of
aizuchi used by participants were also analyzed.
2. Effect on self-disclosure: Given that the experiment was
conducted in Japanese, self-disclosure was quantified by the
total characters participants uttered per dialogue.
3. Questionnaires: We administered pairwise rapport and user
experience questionnaires developed by Baihaqi et al. [3, 28].
Three rapport items were added to evaluate perceived proac-
tivity and personalization: “I felt the robot was proactive in
maintaining the conversation” (R8), “I felt the robot person-
alized the conversation for me” (R9), and “The robot appro-
priately introduced new topics as needed” (R10).

4.2. Experimental procedure
We employed a counterbalanced design, where each participant
interacted with each robot in a randomized order to minimize
potential order bias. After completing interactions with all four
robots, participants filled out the questionnaires. This experi-
ment was conducted from October 14 to 24, 2024.
5. Experimental results
5.1. Robot proactivity, personalization, and aizuchi usage
5.1.1. Frequency of conversation stall
The frequency of stalls for each robot is shown in Table 1.
CSFS4o had no conversation stalls. CSFS35 experienced four
stalls, with an average stall occurring at turn 20.29. ORG4o had
the most stalls, with seven, while ORG35 had only four stalls.
However, ORG35 showed the earliest average stall, at turn 15.
5.1.2. Dialogue personalization
The average dialogue similarities across the robots were as fol-
lows: CSFS4o (7.48%), CSFS35 (8.26%), ORG4o (11.97%),
and ORG35 (11.83%). The results indicate that robots utiliz-
ing CO-STAR and few-shot learning (CSFS4o and CSFS35)
exhibited lower dialogue similarities compared to ORG4o and
ORG35. Additionally, when examining unique words, CSFS4o
featured 1,548 unique words, while CSFS35 had only 1,105.
This suggests that CSFS4o generates more context-specific di-
alogues, whereas CSFS35 relies more on general responses.
5.1.3. Usage of aizuchi
Table 3 presents the distribution of aizuchi types used by each
robot. All robots mainly utilized the SR type of aizuchi. How-
ever, CSFS4o exhibited greater variety in aizuchi usage, partic-
ularly in SR+NL and NL+R combinations, which were rarely
used by the other robots. Overall, CSFS4o demonstrated the
highest total aizuchi usage (188), followed by CSFS35 (123),
ORG4o (87), and ORG35, which had the lowest usage (81).
5.2. Effect on experiment participants
5.2.1. Effect on participants’ aizuchi
An interesting phenomenon was observed in the usage of
aizuchi by the participants. As shown in Table 3, participants
tended to use aizuchi more frequently when interacting with
robots that also employed aizuchi more.
The highest num-
ber of aizuchi was observed during interactions with CSFS4o
(130 instances), followed by CSFS35 (109), ORG4o (87), and
ORG35 (80). Additionally, consistent patterns in aizuchi usage
were identified, with participants predominantly using SR-type
aizuchi. This observation highlights how an increased usage of
aizuchi by robots may influence participant behavior.
5.2.2. Effect on participants’ self-disclosure
We quantified self-disclosure with the total uttered characters.
On average, participants uttered the most characters during in-
teractions with CSFS4o (412.8), followed by CSFS35 (391.4),
and the least with ORG35 and ORG4o (386.1 and 359.9).
In Japanese conversation, even small differences, such as
the 52.9-character gap between CSFS4o and ORG4o, can carry
significant linguistic implications. For instance, 行きたい(ik-
itai) simply expresses “I want to go,” while the addition of just
one character in 行きたいよ(ikitai yo) adds emphasis, making
it sound more expressive, as in “I really want to go!” [29].
5.2.3. Questionnaire results
To analyze the UX and rapport questionnaire results presented
in Figures 1 and 2, we conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA.
Significant differences were observed across all aspects (p <
0.05 or p < 0.01), primarily attributed to ORG35’s lower per-
formance. For the overall UX scores, calculated as the mean
of N1-U7, the results were as follows: CSFS4o = 3.43, ORG4o
= 2.51, CSFS35 = 2.50, and ORG35 = 1.47. The analysis re-
vealed a significant effect: F(3, 57) = 21.8, p < 0.01, and
η2
p = 0.53. For the overall RS scores, calculated as the mean of
R1–R10, the results were as follows: CSFS4o = 3.49, ORG4o
= 2.44, CSFS35 = 2.53, and ORG35 = 1.44. The analysis re-
vealed a significant effect: F(3, 57) = 32.1, p < 0.01, and
η2
p = 0.63. These large effect sizes suggest that differences in
robot behavior had a substantial impact on UX and RS.
We conducted head-to-head comparisons of each robot’s
questionnaire using a post-hoc Bonferroni test, as shown in Ta-
ble 4. Overall, CSFS4o significantly outperformed the other
robots, especially in naturalness, satisfaction, engagement, and
certain rapport measures.
Compared to CSFS35, CSFS4o
demonstrated significant differences in UX (p < 0.05) and RS
(p < 0.01). Against ORG4o, both UX and RS differences were
significant (p < 0.01). Notably, compared to ORG35, CSFS4o
was perceived as significantly better in all aspects (p < 0.01).
We did not find any significant differences between ORG4o
and CSFS35 across all aspects. When comparing ORG4o with
ORG35, p < 0.05 statistically significant differences were ob-
served in satisfaction, dialogue interest, usability (U7), and sev-
eral rapport questions (R1, R3, R4, R5, R6). A p < 0.01 differ-
ence was found in engagement, usability (U6), and four rapport
questions, as well as overall UX and RS.

6. Discussion
Based on our experimental results, including the frequency of
conversation stalls, dialogue similarities, and aizuchi usage by
the robots, we infer that the elicitation of proactive behavior,
personalization, and aizuchi backchannels was successful. Both
proposed robots, CSFS4o and CSFS35, outperformed the base-
line (ORG35 and ORG4o), with CSFS4o—powered by GPT-
4o—showing particular superiority, exhibiting zero stalls, the
lowest similarity, a rich variety of words, and the highest and
most varied usage of aizuchi.
Both CSFS4o and CSFS35 effectively used aizuchi and
demonstrated proactive behavior by asking follow-up questions.
However, CSFS4o’s responses were notably more creative and
engaging. For example, when a participant mentioned an inter-
est in traveling to the United States to watch baseball, CSFS4o
not only acknowledged the statement but also asked a specific
follow-up: “How about New York or Boston? You can enjoy
watching the Yankees in New York and the Red Sox in Boston!”
This question was tailored to the participant’s interest, mention-
ing specific teams and adding a personal touch 1.
In contrast, CSFS35 also used aizuchi and asked a follow-
up question, but it was more general: “What other places would
you like to go?” While this encouraged continued conversation,
it lacked specificity. CSFS35’s open-ended question, focusing
on any other possible venues, was less personalized, likely due
to the limitations of the GPT-3.5 Turbo model. Although the
response helped reduce dialogue similarity, it also resulted in a
less deep interaction compared to CSFS4o.
Examining the effect on participants, both CSFS4o and
CSFS35 garnered higher attention. Participants used aizuchi
more frequently with CSFS4o, indicating greater engagement.
Participants also showed less hesitation to express themselves
when interacting with CSFS4o showed by longer responses.
Based on the questionnaire results, both UX and rapport
scores revealed CSFS4o’s superiority over the other robots. The
proactivity, personalization, and aizuchi significantly enhanced
naturalness, engagement, usability, and ultimately, rapport. In
interviews, participants noted the challenge in choosing them,
as CSFS35 offered proactivity and aizuchi but lacked creativ-
ity, while ORG4o was more creative, albeit with less proactiv-
ity and aizuchi. Some participants noted that ORG4o exhibited
a better user experience, while CSFS35 fostered rapport bet-
ter. However, we found no statistical support for this. Finally,
ORG35 performed the worst, with limited creativity, no proac-
tivity, minimal personalization, and minimal use of aizuchi.
7. Conclusion
In this study, we introduced a rapport-building dialogue strategy
that elicited proactivity, personalization, and aizuchi backchan-
nels through CO-STAR and a few-shot framework. Our exper-
imental results demonstrated the success of this approach, as
evidenced by lower conversation stalls, reduced similarity, and
an increased variety of aizuchi compared to the baseline. The
results showed that proactivity, personalization, and aizuchi sig-
nificantly influenced participants’ responses to the robots. Par-
ticipants exhibited more self-disclosure, used aizuchi more fre-
quently, and appeared more attentive, indicating enhanced rap-
port. Our questionnaire results revealed statistically significant
improvements in user experience, with participants reporting
more natural dialogues, greater satisfaction, higher engagement,
and, ultimately, a higher perception of rapport.
Future research could enhance rapport and UX by explor-
ing gesture strategies that complement RBDS. While this study
used predefined gestures, they were limited and less natural. Fu-
ture research in dynamic gestures that are contextually aligned
with verbal expressions will be beneficial.
