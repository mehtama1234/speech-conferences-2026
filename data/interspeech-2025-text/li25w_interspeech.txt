Lexical competition in the process of Cantonese tone merging: Diverse Impact
Mechanisms Across Different Individuals and Tone Pairs

Abstract
Lexical competition has been shown to exert an inhibitory
influence on phonemic mergers in phonetic evolution. This
study investigates the merging process of Cantonese tone pairs,
examining the lexical competition effect from an individual
perspective. Results reveal that lexical competition influences
tone production even in speakers without clear mergers,
helping maintain tonal contrasts while subtly altering pitch
distributions, which may signal future change. For participants
merging only one tone pair, the effect of lexical competition
varies: it consistently inhibits merging for the T4 (21)–T6 (22)
pair, but for the T3 (33)–T6 (22) pair, its influence manifests
in three distinct patterns—no effect, inhibition, or promotion
of merging. Conversely, in participants merging all three tones,
lexical competition shows minimal impact. This research
elucidates the diverse mechanisms through which lexical
competition shapes tone-merging processes.

1. Introduction
A key question in historical linguistics is why some sounds are
more prone to change while others remain stable over time [1].
Research has shown that internal linguistic factors, such as
word frequency, and lexical competition (e.g., minimal pairs,
neighborhood density) are crucial in promoting or inhibiting
phonetic changes [2, 3, 4, 5, 6].
Lexical competition plays a crucial role in phonetic
evolution. In French, for example, minimal pair nasal vowel
competitors reduce nasal coarticulation in Consonant-Vowel-
Nasal words, suggesting that lexical competition can inhibit
phonetic changes by enhancing phonemic distinctiveness [3].
Similarly, studies on natural spoken English found that
phonological contrasts between lexical items affect phonetic
change, such as in word-initial plosives, where minimal pairs
with voiced or voiceless plosives alter Voice Onset Time
(VOT)
[4][5].
A
corpus-based
research
confirms
that
phonemes distinguishing fewer minimal pairs are more likely
to merge, while those distinguishing many minimal pairs are
less likely to merge [6]. In Bardi, a language in northern
Western Australia, phonemes with fewer minimal pairs also
tend to merge [1]. Generally, lexical competitors lead speakers
to increase phonetic distinctiveness through strategies like
altering duration, VOT, and other articulatory features. A
study proposed a model explaining why high neighborhood
density(the presence of many competitors) aids spoken word
production, suggesting that lexical activation occurs at two
levels: lexical and segmental. Feedback from the segmental
level (i.e., similar words) boosts target word activation,
making it easier for the speaker to produce it [7]. However,
most of the above research has focused on the sound change of
vowels and consonants in non-tonal languages.
Recent studies have examined tonal change in Chinese
dialects, including Cantonese [8, 9], Hakka [10], and the
checked tones in Jin [11], focusing on production, perception,
and structural factors. However, they largely overlook lexical
competition, which may influence the maintenance of tonal
contrasts. One recent study on Cantonese provides preliminary
evidence
that
lexical
competition
helps
preserve
tonal
distinctions by affecting listeners’ acceptance of phonetic
variants [12]. Yet, this study only contrasted merged versus
non-merged tone pairs, without accounting for whether
individual
participants
actually
exhibited
tonal
mergers.
Moreover, the effect of lexical competition on production—
not just perception—remains underexplored. In addition, most
prior research has focused on group-level patterns, often
neglecting the role of individual variation in language change
[13]. Our previous work on Cantonese tonal mergers identified
five distinct merging patterns among younger speakers [14],
but did not investigate the cognitive or lexical factors that
might explain these individual differences.
We hypothesize that lexical competition, known to
influence mergers in non-tonal languages, also affects tonal
mergers with varying effects across individuals. The diverse
merger patterns in Guangzhou Cantonese offer an ideal setting
to study this. This paper will further explore the phenomenon
of tonal mergers between T3 (represented as 33 in the five-
degree tonal system), T4 (21), and T6 (22) in Guangzhou
Cantonese, examining how lexical competition factors affect
individuals with different tonal merger patterns.

2.2. Analysis
2.2.1.
Normalization
Production data were annotated and 11-point F0 contours were
extracted in Praat [16] by the first author, a native Cantonese
speaker. The non-contrastive T4–T6 pair “来(‘come’) – 睐
(‘look favorably upon’)” was excluded due to participants’
confusion over the tone of “睐.” Four participants were also
removed due to poor recording quality, leaving data from 49
participants. Of 6,419 word tokens, 5,914 were retained
(9.21% loss due to poor audio, misreadings, or creaky voice),
with 71.49% corresponding to T4. The first and last sampling
points were removed, and F0 values were normalized using
the lz-score method to reduce inter-speaker and gender-related
variability [17].

2.2.2.
Random Forest
To classify tone pairs, we adopted the Random Forest model
[18], an established method for tone classification [19]. Eight
participants
without

in

(≥80%

Another

balanced group sizes. Acoustic features (average pitch, slope,
curvature) were extracted for three tone pairs. The Random
Forest classifier, implemented in scikit-learn [20], was used to
detect merger patterns, with the number of trees (n_estimators)
optimized via 5-fold cross-validation. Model outputs were
cross-validated against perceptual labels and pitch contour
divergence before further analysis. The best model then
predicted pronunciation categories. Given the potential effects
of lexical competition, models were trained and evaluated
separately under competitive and non-competitive conditions.
Optimal tree numbers were: competitive – 20 (T3-T4, T3-T6),
40 (T4-T6); non-competitive – 80 (T4-T6), 140 (T3-T6).
3. Results
3.1. Overall Results
Using predictions from the Random Forest model, we
calculated the overall production accuracy for each tone pair
in both competitive and non-competitive states. Figure 1
presents boxplots of production accuracy across states. The
median accuracy for the T4-T6 and T3-T6 tone pairs was
around 75%, with a slightly lower accuracy for T3-T6 in the
non-competitive state. The highest accuracy, around 90%, was
observed for the T3-T4 tone pair.
Figure 1: Boxplots of overall production accuracy for
each tone pair (competitive vs non-competitive).
To investigate the effects of gender, competition, age,
and their interactions on production accuracy, this study
employed a linear mixed-effects model using the lmer()
function from the lme4 package in R (version 4.3.0) [21, 22].
The model formula was:
Accuracy ~ Gender + Competition + Age + Tone Pair +
Gender * Age + Tone_Pair * Competition + (1 | Participant)
The results indicated that competition state (β = 2.054e-02,
p < 0.05) and participant age (β = 1.514e-02, p < 0.05)
significantly affected production accuracy. The interaction
between tone pair and competition state also significantly
impacts (β = -3.917e-02, p < 0.01). No significant effects
were found for other variables (p > 0.05).

4.Discussion
This study investigates how lexical competition influences
tone merging across individuals, focusing on six patterns.
Although competition has an overall effect on production
accuracy (p < 0.05), its influence differs across tone pairs,
reflecting both common and divergent trends.
We first provide an overview of the common patterns in
tone distribution under different conditions. Two key trends
were identified across the groups: 1) T4 remains unaffected by
competition and stays stable at the bottom, with no significant
differences in average pitch, slope, or curvature (p > 0.05),
except for G4; 2) Lexical competition increases the average
pitch of T3 and T6 compared to the non-competitive condition
(p < 0.01, except for G2). This pattern holds both in the group
without tone mergers (G1) and in groups where tone mergers
occur (G2 to G5).
The stability of T4 is due to its falling tone characteristics,
requiring less articulatory effort [24]. Moreover, it remains
stable since T4 is positioned at the bottom of the Cantonese
tone system. In competitive conditions, the average pitch of
T3 and T6 increases, expanding the acoustic space between
these tones and aligning with the communicative clarity
principle.
Previous
studies
suggest
speakers
adjust
pronunciation to enhance word recognition, particularly for
phonetically similar words, by increasing dispersion for better
listener distinction [25][26]. This explains why T3 and T6
have higher average pitch in competition.
Next, we will analyze the effect of lexical competition on
different tone pairs and merging patterns. Starting with the T4-
T6 tone pair, under the premise that T4 stays at the bottom, if
the competitor T3 is absent, the decreased height of T6
compresses
the
acoustic
space
between
T4
and
T6,
encouraging a merger. This parallels vowel dispersion in
easily recognized words (fewer competitors) versus hard-to-
recognize words (more competitors), where harder words
show greater F1 and F2 spread [26]. The presence of lexical
competitors T3 prevents the merger of the T4 and T6 tone pair.
This
finding
is
consistent
with
previous
studies
on
phonological evolution in English and Bardi languages, which
also show that lexical competition can inhibit sound mergers.
We examine how lexical competition influences tone
merging in the T3-T6 pair by breaking down the consistent
patterns observed in G1 to G5 into three combinations. We
focus on shared characteristics, with group-specific details
discussed later.
1)T6_B, T6_C & T3_A: In this case, the average height of
T6_B and T6_C decreases under non-competitive conditions,
and the acoustic space between the T3-T6 tone pair increases
compared to the competitive state (T3_A & T6_A), which
inhibits further merging of the T3-T6 tone pair. Thus, the non-
lexical competitive state suppresses the merger of the T3-T6
tone pair.
2)T6_C & T3_C: Although the average height of T6_C
decreases, the decrease in T3_C is similar, and the acoustic
space remains relatively consistent between lexical and non-
lexical competitive conditions (T3_A & T6_A). Given the
relative nature of tone height, lexical competition has little
impact on this distribution.
3)T6_B & T3_C: Compared to the competitive state (T3_A &
T6_A), the acoustic space in this case is smaller, and the
presence of lexical competitors inhibits further merging.
Compared to lexical competition’s clear inhibition of the
T4–T6 merger, its effect on T3–T6 varies, likely due to tone
distribution. T3 and T6 occupy mid-low pitch regions with
greater variability than T4 [8]. Lexical competition inhibits
mergers depending on tone distribution: T4’s stability blocks
T4–T6 merging, while competition’s effect on T3–T6 varies
(promotion, inhibition, or none). Previous studies suggest that
vowel F1 and F2 distributions may be influenced by
“recognizability” [26], though others argue that consonantal
environments, not recognition ease, drive these patterns [27].
Similarly, the impact of lexical competitors on the T3-T6 tone
pair
requires
further
exploration,
considering
contextual
factors like vowel and consonant distributions.
Finally, we focus on the peculiarities of G3, G5, and G6.
G3 shows lower accuracy for the T3-T6 pair and a stronger
merging trend than G2. Under non-competitive conditions,
T3_C and T6_C decrease inconsistently, with T3_C showing a
greater reduction, narrowing the acoustic gap between T3 and
T6. The T4-T6 pair in G3 also shows a significant merging
trend (84.03% →73.27%, p < 0.05), suggesting a greater
likelihood of further merging compared to other groups.
Groups with deeper mergers may exhibit more tone system
variability, potentially linked to tone evolution shifts [28].
G5 shows lower accuracy and a stronger merging trend
for the T4-T6 pair than G4. The T3-T6 accuracy significantly
differs between competitive and non-competitive conditions (p
< 0.001), shifting from merged (73.37%) to non-merged
(82.18%). However, the tone pattern distribution (Figure 2)
shows no actual merger in the T3-T6 pair for G5. The T3-T6
distribution in G5 is skewed compared to G1, indicating that
the random forest algorithm reflects relative differences, not
absolute merging. This may be due to the features used (e.g.,
height, slope, curvature), which might not fully capture tonal
variations.
Future
studies
should
improve
methods
for
assessing tonal evolution.
G6
shows
the
most
pronounced
merging,
with
a
significant reduction in the acoustic space between the three
tones. The distribution patterns under competitive and non-
competitive conditions are less regular than in other groups,
and lexical competition has minimal impact on tone merging.
This research extends the role of lexical competition to
tone production, showing its complex influence varies by tone
pair, and merging pattern. The consistent inhibition of
merging in T4–T6 versus the varied effects in T3–T6 highlight
how lexical competition differentially shapes the direction and
dynamics of tone evolution depending on the specific merger
pattern.
5. Conclusions
In conclusion, this study examined how lexical competition
influences tone mergers among T3, T4, and T6 in Cantonese
using a production task. Lexical competition was found to
reshape tone distribution even in speakers without clear
merging, helping maintain distinctions across tone pairs,
though sometimes altering pitch distribution in ways that
could lead to future change. Among merging speakers,
competition consistently inhibited further T4–T6 merger,
while its effect on T3–T6 varied with tone distribution. In
cases of full three-way mergers, lexical competition had
minimal influence. These results underscore the interaction
between lexical competition and tone system dynamics. Future
research should further investigate how competition, phonetic
context, and segmental factors jointly contribute to the
trajectory of tone merger, tone evolution and refine methods
for quantifying tone merger degrees.
