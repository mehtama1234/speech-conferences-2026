Abstract
Recently, language model (LM)-based speech synthesis
models have shown remarkable naturalness and powerful zero-
shot capabilities. In this paradigm, discrete speech tokens play
a critical role. Prior work has proposed using automatic speech
recognition (ASR) tasks to enhance the semantic information
and alignment with text in tokens. However, the commonly
used byte-pair encoding (BPE) tokenizer in ASR task leads to
significant differences in the text token sets of different lan-
guages, making it difficult to exploit language-shared informa-
tion. This paper proposes to use the International Phonetic Al-
phabet (IPA) as the training target for ASR to learn language-
independent speech tokens. In addition, we propose to use a
timbre converter for speaker disentanglement in the speech syn-
thesis model. Our proposed approach effectively improves the
speaker similarity and expressiveness in both multilingual and
cross-lingual zero-shot speech synthesis.
Index Terms: speech synthesis, multilingual, speech token,
language model
1. Introduction
In recent years, neural text-to-speech (TTS) models have seen
significant advancements [1, 2, 3, 4]. These models are capable
of generating high-fidelity voices that are nearly indistinguish-
able from human speech for specific speakers. Alongside the
progress in audio representation learning techniques, such as
self-supervised learning (SSL) models [5, 6] and speech codec
models [7, 8], several studies have incorporated continuous or
discrete speech representations into speech synthesis models as
intermediate representations [9, 10, 11, 12].
Recent works have demonstrated that zero-shot TTS mod-
els have the ability to synthesize speech for any given text by
imitating the timbre, prosody, and style from just a few seconds
of prompt audio. These models, trained on large-scale data, ex-
hibit strong in-context learning capabilities and can be classified
into two main categories. One is based on the language model
(LM), which uses a speech tokenizer to extract discrete speech
tokens and an autoregressive language model to predict these
tokens [13, 14, 15]. Due to autoregressive sampling, LM-based
models can generate diverse and prosody-consistent speech.
The second category consists of non-autoregressive TTS mod-
els based on diffusion models, where flow matching is widely
used and has achieved high levels of naturalness [16, 17]. Addi-
tionally, some studies have combined these two generative mod-
els to achieve even better performance [18, 19].

Obviously, speech tokens are crucial in LM-based TTS
models, as they directly determine the performance of the mod-
els. While previous studies have applied discrete speech tokens
to LM-based multilingual TTS models [15], they typically used
unsupervised speech tokens [7, 8]. CosyVoice [19] proposed
inserting a quantization layer into a multilingual ASR model to
obtain discrete supervised semantic tokens, which complements
lacking semantic information and alignment with text, resulting
in better performance.
However, in the ASR tasks used by CosyVoice, the byte-
pair encoding (BPE) tokenizer was employed to obtain text to-
kens as training targets. Since the BPE token sets vary signif-
icantly across different languages, it becomes challenging for
the ASR model to learn shared language knowledge. As a re-
sult, the extracted speech tokens lack language-independent in-
formation, limiting the performance of multilingual LM-based
TTS models that rely on these tokens, especially in cross-
lingual scenarios.
To address this problem, this paper proposes using the Inter-
national Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) [20] as the training target for
the ASR model. IPA is a unified phonetic symbol system used
to represent the pronunciations of all languages. This enables
the ASR model to fully leverage the consistency of pronunci-
ation across languages and learn language-independent seman-
tic information. To obtain a language-independent speech to-
kenizer, we insert a vector quantization layer into the encoder
of ASR model with IPA as training targets. Then, we extract
language-independent speech tokens from the proposed speech
tokenizer and integrate them into an LM-based multilingual
TTS model, which autoregressively predicts the speech tokens
using a language model and employs a flow matching model to
generate mel spectrogram from these tokens, conditioning on
speaker embedding and mel spectrogram.
Furthermore, we propose an approach to achieve speaker
disentanglement during the training of the flow matching
model. This method uses a timbre converter to modify the tim-
bre attribute of the speech before extracting speech tokens and
then predicts the mel spectrogram extracted from the original
speech. In this way, the flow matching model is forced to rely
on speaker information from the provided speaker embedding
rather than the speech tokens, resulting in higher speaker simi-
larity and improved naturalness.
Our proposed TTS model is trained on a multilingual
dataset consisting of six languages.
Experimental results
demonstrate that the proposed method effectively improves the
speaker similarity and expressiveness of the synthesized speech
in both multilingual and cross-lingual zero-shot speech syn-
thesis tasks. Audio demos, codes and models are available at

2. Proposed Method
2.1. Language-Independent Speech Tokenizer
The Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) task involves tran-
scribing speech into text. Previous studies have explored lever-
aging ASR tasks to enrich the semantic information within
speech representations and improve their alignment with text.
For instance, Liu et al. [21] utilized the continuous hidden rep-
resentations of the ASR model as intermediate representations
for TTS models. In contrast, CosyVoice [19] introduced the
idea of inserting a quantization layer into the ASR encoder to
obtain discrete speech tokens.
As shown in Figure 1, our proposed language-independent
(LI) speech tokenizer is trained using an ASR task. It first down-
samples the waveform to 50 Hz representations using the same
CNN encoder as HuBERT [6]. The Token encoder then ex-
tracts contextual features from these representations, consisting
of the first six Transformer layers of the ASR encoder. Fol-
lowing this, a vector quantizer is inserted after the Token en-
coder to obtain discrete speech tokens, following the approach
of CosyVoice. We employ a vector quantizer with a Euclidean
codebook, updated using exponential moving average (EMA).
After quantization, discrete speech tokens are obtained. Before
feeding them into the subsequent networks, additional position
embeddings are added. The Aggregation encoder consists of
the remaining layers of the ASR encoder, and its output is fed
to the ASR decoder to predict targets in a teacher-forcing mode
during training with cross entropy (CE) loss.
In CosyVoice, the Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer was
used to generate text tokens as training targets for multilin-
gual ASR. However, the set of these text tokens varies signif-
icantly across different languages, which makes it challenging
for the ASR model to learn language-shared semantic infor-
mation. This variation negatively impacts the performance of
speech tokens and, consequently, the synthesis quality when ap-
plied to the downstream multilingual TTS task.
This paper proposes converting all text transcriptions into
the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) and using these IPA
transcriptions as the training targets for the multilingual ASR
model. IPA is a unified phonetic symbol system that represents
the pronunciations of all languages. In this way, we can ef-
fectively leverage the consistent pronunciation features across
different languages, enabling the model to learn a language-
independent speech token.
Note that the encoder of our ASR model is initialized us-
ing a pretrained multilingual HuBERT model to enhance per-
formance, given the limited computational and data resources.
Additionally, the original HuBERT models operate at a frame
rate of 50 Hz. Following previous work [22], we reduce this
to 25 Hz without introducing extra parameters. Since HuBERT
uses convolutional position embeddings, we modify the stride
of the position convolution before the first encoder layer to 2
and apply average pooling on the 50 Hz representations, result-
ing in a 25 Hz speech token.
2.2. Language Model-Based TTS Model with Language-
Independent Speech Token
As shown in Figure 2, our TTS model architecture follows the
design of CosyVoice [19]. A language model is used to predict
speech tokens from text. These tokens are then processed by
a flow matching model to predict the mel-spectrogram, condi-
tioned on both the speaker embedding and the reference mel-
spectrogram. Finally, a vocoder is employed to reconstruct the

In CosyVoice, a speaker embedding is fed into the language
model. However, during our preliminary experiments, we found
that this approach leads to a serious information leakage prob-
lem, particularly in cross-lingual scenarios, as also noted in
CosyVoice2 [23]. As a result, we remove the speaker embed-
ding from the language model, as shown in Figure 2. Addi-
tionally, to improve language control, we randomly prepend a
language prefix to the text during language model training with
a probability of 0.5.
Furthermore, to improve speaker similarity and the natu-
ralness of synthetic speech, we propose a speaker disentangle-
ment method during the flow matching training process. As
shown in Figure 3, we use a timbre converter to modify the
timbre attributes of the speech before extracting speech tokens,
and then predict the mel-spectrogram based on the unaltered
speech. This approach forces the flow-matching model to rely
on speaker information from the provided speaker embedding
and reference mel-spectrogram, rather than from the speech to-
kens, to achieve speaker disentanglement.

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets
3.1.1. ASR data
To train the ASR model described above, we used data from
40 languages in the Common Voice dataset [24], including Ara-
bic (ar), Belarusian (be), Bashkir (ba), Bulgarian (bg), Cata-
lan (ca), Chinese (zh), Czech (cs), Welsh (cy), Dutch (nl), En-
glish (en), Estonian (et), Basque (eu), Persian (fa), Finnish (fi),
French (fr), Georgian (ka), German (de), Hungarian (hu), In-
donesian (id), Italian (it), Latvian (lv), Lithuanian (lt), Marathi
(mr), Polish (pl), Portuguese (pt), Romanian (ro), Russian (ru),
Slovak (sk), Slovenian (sl), Spanish (es), Swahili (sw), Swedish
(sv), Tamil (ta), Turkish (tr), Ukrainian (uk), Urdu (ur), Uzbek
(uz), Uyghur (ug), Chuvash (cv), Tatar (tt). The duration of
each language ranged from 50 to 200 hours, for a total of 4879
hours. Each utterance had a corresponding text transcription.
3.1.2. TTS data
To evaluate the performance of the LM-based TTS model using
our proposed LI speech tokens, we used a multilingual dataset
consisting of six languages. The Chinese data was obtained
from Wenetspeech4TTS [25], with a duration of 945 hours,
while the English data came from LibriTTS [26], with a du-
ration of 585 hours. For the other languages, we used German,
French, Japanese, and Korean data from the Emilia dataset [27],
with durations of 1,590, 1,381, 1,715, and 217 hours, respec-
tively.
To demonstrate the language independence of the speech
tokens, Japanese and Korean in the TTS data were considered
unseen languages in the ASR task.
3.2. Experimental setup
3.2.1. ASR
To generate the IPA training targets, we employed the Phone-
mizer tool [28] to convert the transcriptions of the ASR data
into IPA sequences. However, this method was not applicable
to Chinese due to its low accuracy. Instead, we first utilized
pypinyin1 to convert Chinese characters into pinyin, and then
employed a pinyin-to-ipa tool2 to convert these pinyin into IPA
with tones. As a result, the size of the IPA set was 417.
As previously mentioned, the encoder of our ASR model
was initialized with a pretrained multilingual HuBERT model
named mHuBERT-147 [29]. We implemented an approach to

compress it to a token rate of 25 Hz without adding any extra
parameters. Upon completion of the training process, the Char-
acter Error Rate (CER) of the ASR model on the validation sets
of 40 languages was measured at 3.57%.
To verify the effectiveness of our proposed language-
independent speech tokens, we also trained another ASR model
with the same architecture and data, but using the BPE tokens
from CosyVoice instead of IPA. The size of BPE token set was
43,319, resulting in a CER of 10.24% on the validation sets of
40 languages.
3.2.2. TTS
We adopted the identical model configurations as those of the
original CosyVoice model.
Besides, to prevent information
leakage, we eliminated the speaker embedding from the Lan-
guage Model (LM). For the timbre converter, we used Open-
Voice [30]. Regarding the speaker embedding, we utilized the
campplus [31] model to extract it from speech waveforms.
In our experiments, we compared the following three mod-
els:
• Token: The TTS model trained using the speech tokens ex-
tracted from our ASR model, with BPE tokens as the targets.
• LIST: Our proposed model, which uses the speech tokens
extracted from our ASR model with IPA as the targets.
• LIST-tc: Our proposed model, enhanced with a timbre con-
verter for speaker disentanglement.
3.3. Experimental results
3.3.1. Evaluation sets
To evaluate the performance of the multilingual TTS models,
we prepared both an objective set and a subjective set for each
language. The objective set is used to assess various objective
metrics of the model. For Chinese and English, we directly
used the open-source benchmarks from SEED [18], which con-
tain 2,000 and 1,000 samples, respectively. For the other four
languages, we extracted 1,000 samples each from the test sets of
the Common Voice dataset. The subjective set is used for listen-
ing tests, as it includes high expressiveness and diverse styles.
Each language contains approximately 100 samples. Addition-
ally, to evaluate the model’s performance in cross-lingual sce-
narios, we constructed cross-lingual test sets by using the test
sets from different languages.
3.3.2. Objective evaluation
We used two objective metrics: 1) character error rate (CER),
which is used to evaluate intelligibility. For languages other
than Chinese, we used whisper-large-v3 [32] to transcribe the
generated speech. For Chinese, we used Paraformer-zh [33].
The CER is then calculated based on the transcriptions.
2)
speaker similarity (SIM), which is obtained by calculating the
cosine similarity between the speaker embeddings of the gen-
erated speech and the reference speech.
These embeddings
are extracted using a speaker verification model fine-tuned on
WavLM3.

3.3.3. Subjective evaluation
Subjective listening tests were conducted to evaluate the mean
opinion score (MOS) of the synthetic speech. For each lan-
guage, we randomly selected 20 samples from the subjective
evaluation set for zero-shot synthesis. Then, we conducted sub-
jective listening tests on the crowdsourcing platform Amazon
Mechanical Turk4. Approximately 15 native speakers partici-
pated in each language group. These native speakers first lis-
tened to a prompt reference audio, and then rated the synthe-
sized speech in terms of speech quality, expressiveness, and
speaker similarity to the prompt audio, on a scale from 1 to 5,
with intervals of 0.5.

4. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed a novel approach to obtain language-
independent speech tokens for multilingual TTS. We built an
ASR model using IPA as training targets and inserted a vec-
tor quantizer into the encoder to generate discrete speech to-
kens. The use of IPA enables the ASR model to learn useful
language-independent semantic information. Besides, we intro-
duced a speaker decoupling method during the training process
of the TTS model. This approach employs a timbre converter
to modify the speech before extracting speech tokens for flow
matching, resulting in improved speaker similarity and voice
quality.
