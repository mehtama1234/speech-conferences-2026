Discovering Directions of Uncertainty in Speech Inpainting
Kfir Cohen1, Lior Wolf 2, Bracha Laufer-Goldshtein1
1The School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Tel-Aviv University, Israel
2The School of Computer Science and AI, Tel-Aviv University, Israel

Abstract
Speech inpainting aims to restore missing segments in audio
signals. While both classical signal processing and deep
learning approaches have been developed for this task, they
typically generate a single output, despite the existence of
multiple plausible reconstructions. In this paper, we adapt
Neural Posterior Principal Component (NPPC) to recover the
principal directions of variation in the posterior distribution
of the original signal given its masked version. Our empirical
results demonstrate that these directions capture diverse and
meaningful variations in both speech content and style, while
more precisely capturing the predictive error compared to a
more costly Bayesian deep learning approach.

1. Introduction
Audio signals often contain corrupted segments where multiple
samples are missing, noisy, or unreliable. These distortions can
arise from various factors, including recording errors, packet
loss during transmission, or the degradation of storage media
over time. Audio inpainting aims to reconstruct these missing
or corrupted portions, restoring the signal’s integrity.
Early approaches to audio inpainting primarily relied
on signal processing techniques and sparse representations.
Adler et al. [1] introduced a foundational audio inpainting
framework, leveraging dictionary learning methods in the
time-frequency domain. Mokry et al. [2] proposed a proba-
bilistic framework based on nonnegative matrix factorization.
They treat the missing samples as latent variables, and derive
expectation–maximization algorithms for estimating the model
parameters in either the time or frequency domains. Another
traditional approach is based on autoregression. The Janssen
algorithm [3], widely regarded as the state-of-the-art method
for time-domain audio inpainting, has been effective for
reconstructing gaps up to 50 ms in length. Recent work by
Mokry et al. [4] extends this approach to the time-frequency
domain, potentially enhancing its applicability to longer gaps.
The rise of deep learning has introduced new possibilities
for audio inpainting. Ebner et al. [5] explored the use of
generative adversarial networks (GANs) for audio inpainting,
showing promise in generating longer segments of missing
audio. Federico et al. [6] proposed using multi-resolution
harmonic CNNs for audio inpainting. Recent works have
introduced diffusion-based audio inpainting [7, 8], capable
of handling gaps of various lengths. Another trend is to
incorporate visual information, leveraging accompanying video
data to guide the audio inpainting process [9, 10].
In many cases, especially for speech gaps of several tens
of milliseconds, a single definitive reconstruction may not ex-
ist; instead, multiple plausible completions can be considered
valid. However, previous studies have largely overlooked this
uncertainty.
Uncertainty quantification aims to assess model confidence.
Techniques such as Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout [11], deep en-
sembles [12], and test-time augmentations [13] estimate pre-
diction variability. Recently, quantile regression and confor-
mal prediction [14] gained attention for offering distribution-
free uncertainty bounds. Predictive uncertainty in audio tasks
remains relatively underexplored. Recent work has begun ad-
dressing this gap in areas such as speech enhancement [15] and
source localization [16, 17, 18, 19].
In this paper, we introduce a framework to quantify and
track uncertainty in speech inpainting. Building on [20], we
leverage NPPC to recover the principal directions of variation
in the posterior distribution of the clean signal given the
distorted input. Our experimental results show that these
directions capture diverse variations in both speech content and
style, manifested in hearing, transcription, and pitch. Moreover,
compared to a Bayesian deep learning approach, NPPC can
better reconstruct predictive error with reduced computational
cost. An illustration of our approach is provided in Fig. 1.
2. Preliminaries
Consider a clean speech signal represented by its short time
Fourier transform (STFT) X∈ CF×T , where F denotes the
number of frequency bins andT represents the number of time
frames. We observe a masked version of the signal,Xm, where
certain time frames are missing or severely corrupted. Let
m∈ [0, 1]T be a binary masking vector, where ones indicate
observed frames and zeros denote distorted ones. The masked
signal is given by
Xm = X⊙M, (1)
where⊙ denotes element-wise product, M = 1F· mT , and
1F is an all-ones vector of length F . Note that the location of
the masked part is assumed to be known.
Next, let x, xm∈ R2FT denote the vectorized STFT rep-
resentation, comprising the real and imaginary parts of X and
Xm, respectively, for all time frames and frequency bins. Given
a datasetD ={(xm, x)}N , a modelf (x;θ) with parametersθ
is trained to reconstruct the missing portions of xm. The recon-
struction error is defined as
e(xm, x;θ) =|x− ˆx|

3. Method

by generating a diverse set of plausible alternatives rather than a
single estimate, ˆx. This uncertainty is encoded in the posterior
distribution of x given xm. We aim to identify multiple
directions of variation that capture the principal modes of this
posterior distribution. However, a single estimate, ˆx, conveys
only the posterior mean, neglecting higher-order moments.

4. Experimental Setup
Dataset. We conducted our experiments over the LibriSpeech
dataset, using train-clean-360 for training and test-clean for
validation at a sampling rate of 16 kHz. The inpainting task
was designed to reconstruct masked audio segments with a
pre-determined duration of 128 ms, while the total input audio
duration was set to 2 s during training. To ensure meaningful
masking, we applied voice activity detection (V AD) to locate
speech regions and randomly positioned 128 ms masks within
these regions. This approach ensures that the model learns
to inpaint speech segments rather than silence or non-speech
regions. We performed all experiments on the normalized log-
magnitude spectrogram computed with a window size of 256
and a hop length of128. Various methods exist for phase recon-
struction based on the estimated magnitude [21, 22]. However,

5. Results
We examine the differences among the PCs in terms of the
obtained spectrograms as well as the variations in transcription
and pitch. Additionally, we compare NPPC to a Bayesian deep
learning approach in terms of the output Root Mean Squared
Error (RMSE) and the reconstruction of the error.
Example Spectrograms. Fig. 2 displays the spectrograms
of the original speech signal, the masked signal, and various
transformations along the PCs. Given the small gap, we zoom
in on the surrounding interval for clarity. The results indicate
that different directions lead to distinct spectrogram variations,
emphasizing different frequency regions. Our GitHub reposi-
tory provides corresponding audio samples, enabling listeners
to directly perceive the variations in speech content.
Transcription. Table 2 presents transcription examples
for different traversal directions. The results show a variety
of possible transcripts depending on both the direction and
the traversal distance. For instance, the word “meters” is
sometimes transcribed as “measures,” “meekers,” or “mirrors”,
while “Paris” appears as “parents” or “parrots”. Notably, in
some cases, the initial output is incorrect, but moving along the
PCs recovers the correct transcription.
Pitch. Fig. 3 compares the estimated pitch when moving
across different PCs directions. While variations in pitch
are observed along different PCs, they are generally less
pronounced than content variations. This may be because pitch
is largely speaker-dependent and can often be inferred with
high confidence from the unmasked regions of the input signal.
RMSE and Residual Error . As a baseline for a quanti-
tative comparison, we use MC Dropout [11]. MC Dropout is
a technique used in Bayesian deep learning to estimate model
uncertainty by enabling dropout at both training and inference
time. During inference, the model performs multiple forward
passes with different dropout masks, generating a distribution
of predictions instead of a single deterministic output. We
apply MC dropout to generate 50 output samples and perform
principal component analysis (PCA) on this set, extracting the
top 5 PCs.
The RMSE and residual error are shown in Fig. 4, averaged
across 50 test samples. We observe that both approaches
obtain comparable RMSE, while NPPC achieves slightly better
reconstruction error. This is while being 50× faster during
inference than MC Dropout, since NPPC requires a single
forward pass, while MC Dropout requires 50 passes.
6. Conclusions
In this paper, we emphasize the importance of modeling
uncertainty in speech inpainting, moving beyond traditional
approaches that generate a single deterministic output. We
adopt the NPPC method, which learns to output the principal
components of the posterior distribution. Our results demon-
strate that NPPC better captures inpainting errors than the
computationally intensive MC Dropout approach, while also
producing perceptually diverse outputs, as illustrated in the
accompanying audio samples. This can enable future systems
to reflect ambiguity and adapt outputs to user intent.
