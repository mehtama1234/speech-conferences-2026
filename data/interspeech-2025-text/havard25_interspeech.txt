Self-Supervised Models of Speech Processing for Haitian Creole
William N. Havard1,2, Renauld Govain3, Benjamin Lecouteux2, Emmanuel Schang1
1LLL, Université d’Orléans, CNRS, 45000 Orléans, France
2LIG, Université Grenoble Alpes, CNRS, Grenoble INP, 38000 Grenoble, France
3LangSé, Université d’État d’Haïti, Port-au-Prince, Haïti
william.havard@univ-orleans.fr, emmanuel.schang@univ-orleans.fr
Abstract
We develop tailored speech processing models for Haitian Cre-
ole positioning it as a high-resource language in terms of SSL
models of speech processing. We do so by pretraining monolin-
gual Wav2Vec2-Base, Wav2Vec2-Large and Data2Vec-Audio-
Base models from scratch, which are then finetuned on an Au-
tomatic Speech Recognition task. We compare the performance
of these models with models finetuned from larger multilingual
(XLSR-53, XLSR2-300m, MMS-1B) and monolingual French-
based models (LeBenchmark 1 to 7K). Our results highlight the
effectiveness of pretraining monolingual models from scratch,
demonstrating that they can achieve performance comparable to
or even surpassing larger models derived from large-scale mul-
tilingual pretraining. Our work provides essential resources for
robust Haitian Creole speech recognition and offers pretrained
models that can be adapted to other French-based Caribbean
Creoles, opening new avenues for linguistic research and prac-
tical applications in the region.
Index Terms: wav2vec2, data2vec, Haitian Creole, speech
recognition
1. Introduction
Haitian Creole (kreyòl, or kreyòl ayisyen) is the primary lan-
guage spoken in the Republic of Haiti, with approximately
11 million speakers. Being spoken in a context of diglossia,
Haitian Creole (HC) still faces challenges in achieving parity
with French, which is considered a more prestigious variety,
despite efforts within Haiti to grant it an equal status.
In this paper, we present our work on the development
of self-supervised speech processing models pretrained exclu-
sively on HC,1 which are further finetuned on a Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR) task. The monolingual models we
train represent a departure from prior approaches for this lan-
guage that exclusively relied on transfer learning from multi-
lingual models or from a typologically close high-resource lan-
guage, and more specifically here, from its lexifier language,
French. Our work grants Haitian Creole an equal status to other
high-resourced languages such as English [1, 2], French [3] or
Mandarin Chinese [4] which all have dedicated monolingual
models. In summary, our work shows that monolingual mod-
els perform competitively with similar-sized models trained via
transfer learning for ASR, despite using less training data.
Beyond the technical aspect, our initiative carries signifi-
cant symbolic weight. For too long, it has been asserted that
Creole languages were destined to disappear in favour of their

2. Background
The field of speech processing for Creole languages is rela-
tively sparse, compared to other languages such as English or
French, mainly due to the lack of accessible data. One of the
very first datasets created to train speech processing models for
HC, and more specifically ASR models, was collected by the
DARPA DIPLOMAT project in the late 1990s [6], and is com-
monly known as the HAITI-CMU dataset. More recently, a
larger collection of speech was collected for the IARPA BA-
BEL program, which is accessible through the Linguistic Data
Consortium [7] (see section 3 for more details on these two
datasets). To the best of our knowledge, other than the two pre-
vious datasets we mentioned, no publicly available datasets of
transcribed Standard Haitian Creole exist, which severely lim-
ited the work done for this language. This data scarcity is not
limited to the spoken realm but also to textual approaches, and
it is only recently that initiatives to develop textual resources
specifically targeting Creole Languages emerged.
Over the years, most work on Haitian Creole (HC) has pri-
marily focused on Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) [8, 9].
However, in most work, ASR is often treated as a component of
a larger speech translation pipeline [6, 10], where transcriptions
serve as input for machine translation models in a cascaded ap-
proach. Beyond ASR, research on spoken HC has also explored
keyword spotting [11, 12], driven by datasets such as the IARPA
Babel Haitian Creole Language Pack [7], which was specifi-
cally created for this purpose. Research targeting other French-
based Creoles is similarly limited. Recently, ASR models have
been developed for Guadeloupean Creole, which is related to
HC and mutually intelligible [13, 14, 15]. Regarding Mauritian
Creole – spoken in Mauritius, which is neither related to HC
nor mutually intelligible – the field appears more active, with
notable contributions from [16, 17, 18] and part of the work by
[13], although research remains relatively niche.
Despite recent personal initiatives to create modern speech
processing models for HC using transfer learning of pretrained
multilingual models like Whisper [19], XLS-R [20] or MMS
[21], reproducible scientific efforts to develop monolingual
models comparable to WAV2VEC2 for English or LEBENCH-
MARK for French are, to our knowledge, nonexistent. The only
exception is the work of [22] which is limited in scope and do-

In summary, there is a significant gap between the state-
of-the-art speech processing models available for languages
like English or French and those for HC. This gap is largely
due to the lack of accessible data, which prevents the devel-
opment of comparable models. Additionally, past speech pro-
cessing efforts for HC, primarily ASR models, have remained
largely confidential and inaccessible.
Our work aims to ad-
dress these challenges by (1) surveying the existing datasets of
spoken HC, (2) training state-of-the-art self-supervised mono-
lingual WAV2VEC2 and DATA2VEC models, and (3) finetuning
these models on an ASR task while making them freely avail-
able to the creolist community, our primary target audience.
3. Haitian Creole Speech Corpora
This section surveys the datasets of spoken Haitian Creole used
in our experiments. Table 1a offers a concise comparison of
each dataset, highlighting their size, train/validation/test split
partitioning, and gender distribution among speakers.
ATLAS LINGUISTIQUE
D’HAÏTI (ALH) The ATLAS
LINGUISTIQUE D’HAÏTI (Linguistic Atlas of Haiti) [23] con-
sists of a collection of 499 audio recordings collected in Haiti
between 1978 and 1987 for the purpose of creating a linguistic
atlas, and totalises 356h of audio. The recordings were orig-
inally done on audio cassettes with tape recorders, and were
then digitalised by the French National Library (Bibliothèque
Nationale de France, BNF) in 2010. Each recording is on av-
erage 45 minutes long and features one or several interview-
ers eliciting words or phrases from their native collaborators.
This data has been made publically and legaly available by the
BNF and the Faculté de Linguistique Appliquée de l’Université
d’État d’Haïti and is accessible through the COCOON platform
[24]. This corpus consists entirely of raw speech. Each record-
ing is enriched with detailed metadata, including the exact lo-
cation of collection and speaker information (e.g. age, profes-
sion, literacy). The recordings span the entire country, ensuring
a large diversity of accents and vocabulary, by more than 200
speakers.
CORPUS OF NORTHERN HAITIAN CREOLE (CNHC)
The CORPUS OF NORTHERN HAITIAN CREOLE [25] consists
of 10 recorded and transcribed interviews conducted in Cap-
Haïtien (Northern Haiti) to study dialectal variation in relation
to standard Haitian, and totalises 9h of audio. The transcriptions
use non-standard spellings to more faithfully represent speak-
ers’ pronunciations. For example, standard/non-standard varia-
tions include: “Pòwoprens/Pòtoprens” (Port-au-Prince, capital
city of Haiti); “eske/eskeu” (question word); or “de/deu” (two).
While we do not have the exact number of speakers, we estimate
it to be between 10 and 20, all from the Cap-Haïtien region.
HAITI-CMU (H-CMU) The HAITI-CMU dataset con-
sists of recordings that were originally collected for the DARPA
DIPLOMAT project [6] in the late 1990s and totalises 20h of
speech. The dataset solely consists of read speech, using sen-
tences stemming from various sources (novels, speeches, reli-
gious material, etc.), resulting in more than 7.5k sentences for
152 speakers. The recordings were collected in various loca-
tions (U.S. & France) with speakers from different backgrounds
and regions of Haiti. Some of the speakers having lived or hav-
ing been brought up in Metropolitan France, or Overseas France
islands where neighbouring Creoles (Guadeloupean, Martini-
can, Guianese) are spoken, this might have affected their accent.
All the recordings are transcribed using the standard Haitian
Creole spelling conventions.
They have been collected in a
noise-free environment resulting in high-quality recordings.
IARPA-BABEL
HAITIAN
CREOLE
(BABEL)
The
IARPA-BABEL HAITIAN CREOLE dataset [7] consists of
recordings in Haitian Creole that were gathered for the U.S.
government in the course of the BABEL program.
The
recordings were collected in 2012/2013 and mainly consist
of conversational speech recorded over the phone.
Some
recordings were also collected using scripted material, where
speakers were asked to read specific sentences and/or discuss
specific topics. The whole dataset consists of more than 11k
recordings totalising more than 202h of speech across more
than 1k speakers.
Most recordings were transcribed using
standard Haitian Creole spelling conventions, but some were
left untranscribed. To ensure maximal diversity, speakers come
from different region of Haiti, and span a wide age range, and
is balanced between male and females. The recordings were
done in various environmental conditions, from very noisy
(e.g. street) to very calm environements (e.g. home or office).
VOXLINGUA HAITIAN (VOXLG) VoxLingua [26] is a
speech dataset that consists of audio recordings scraped from
YouTube, in order to train language identification models. The
whole dataset consists of recordings for 107 languages. In this
work, we only use the recordings that were classified as be-
ing in Haitian Creole, totalising 96h of speech. The distributed
recordings are already preprocessed [26] in order to exclude
spoken sections from noise and non-spoken sections using a

RADIO HAITI (RADIO HAITI) While the datasets previ-
ously mentioned are known to the speech and language pro-
cessing community, and have already been used in other publi-
cations, RADIO HAITI is a new dataset, which is used for the
first time in this publication. RADIO HAITI consist of record-
ings aired by Radio Haiti-Inter between 1957 and 2003, which
were made available by Duke University through the David M.
Rubenstein Rare Book Manuscript Library [27]. The dataset
comprises over 5, 000 recordings totaling 2, 400 hours of spo-
ken content. About half of the recordings only consist of speech
in Haitian Creole, while the other half is mostly in French or a in
mix of French and Haitian Creole, and, to a rarer extent English
or Spanish. In this work, we only use a subset of this dataset,
specifically the portion in Haitian Creole. This portion consists
of about 2.9k recordings totalising more than 1.3k hours. This
is, to the best of our knowledge, the largest ever collection of
speech in Haitian Creole. Although most recordings were made
in a studio, ensuring noise-free, high-quality audio, the dataset
also includes interviews and interventions conducted over the
phone or in public spaces, introducing a wide range of record-
ing conditions.
Each recording is paired with metadata that
specifies the main topics discussed and the key speakers, typ-
ically the presenters and, when relevant, the interviewees. Al-
though it is impossible to determine the exact number of speak-
ers (e.g. speakers who intervene over the phone or during inter-
views are not mentioned in the metadata), the recordings we use
feature at least 1, 000 unique individuals. Similarly, while we
noticed the speakers are from different backgrounds and have,
for some, a distinct accent (e.g. from the Cap Haïtien region),
we do not have metadata indicating where the speakers come
from. Finally, the metadata specifies the airdate of the record-
ings (at least the year). Most of the recordings that are used in
this publication were aired from the 1980s to the early 2000s.
Overall, our work uses more than 2000h of recordings in
Haitian Creole.
While we cannot give an exact number of
speakers, we estimate it is over 3, 000 unique speakers. The
recordings cover a wide range of environmental conditions,
from fieldwork recordings done in the field (ALH) or in calm
environments (CNHC), to read (H-CMU, BABEL) speech and to
more natural conversational speech (BABEL, VOXLG, RADIO-
H). Concerning gender balance, although we lack this informa-
tion for all the datasets we use, when it is available, we observe
a fairly balanced distribution with 744 males and 645 females.

spoken sections from silences and non-human speech sounds.

concatenation, preserving temporal order so as to reach a mean

Text Prepocessing. We use a vocabulary of 28 symbols
(27 characters and an additional space character 〈|〉).5 When
encountering foreign graphemes in loanwords or proper nouns
(e.g. 〈qu〉as in “Inuqua”) we converted them to their closest
equivalent (e.g. 〈k〉as in “Inuka”). Digits were fully spelled
out (e.g. “21”: “venteyen”) using the num2words package
that we adapted for Haitian Creole,6 and abbreviations were
fully spelled out (e.g. “HTML”: “ach te èm èl”) using a cus-
tom script. All the annotations of the IARPA Babel specifi-
cations (e.g. to signal noise, hesitations, etc.) were removed.
Finally, the transcriptions were lowercased, all the punctuation
was removed and characters normalised using Unicode’s NFKC
standard [36]. Finally, we separated clitic pronouns (“t”, “m”,
“n”, “y”, “l”, and “w”) which are phonetically bound (e.g. “w”

6. Conclusion
In this work, we presented three pretrained models of speech
processing for Haitian Creole based on the WAV2VEC2-BASE,
WAV2VEC2-LARGE, and DATA2VEC-AUDIO-BASE architec-
tures. We further finetuned these models on a speech recog-
nition task and show they obtain competitive results compared
to large-scale multilingual and monolingual models. This is
the first time models of this scale are developped for Haitian
Creole, establishing it as a high-resource language in terms of
SSL models of speech processing. Our work provide the nec-
essary tools for robust speech recognition, and source models
for transfer learning to other French-based Caribbean Creoles,
and contribute to grant Haitian Creole equal status alongside
French. We hope that creolists will seize upon these models to
transcribe data on a large scale. They have already been used
to transcribe 1, 600 hours of RADIO HAITI recordings, enabling
unprecedented study of linguistic phenomena in Haitian Creole.
