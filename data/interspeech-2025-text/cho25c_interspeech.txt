A Demonstration of High-Fidelity Human to Non-Human Voice Conversion

Abstract
Non-human monsters in massive multiplayer online role-
playing games (MMORPGs) are essential for immersive game
environments and significantly enhance player engagement.
However, producing high-quality monster sounds demands con-
siderable time and financial resources. AI-driven voice conver-
sion offers a potential solution, but existing models rely on stan-
dard human voice training and human-centric audio features,
limiting their ability to generate realistic non-human voices.
This study presents a human-to-non-human (H2NH) voice con-
version model designed to address these challenges.
The
model effectively generated high-quality non-human sounds by
recording the voices of participants and converting them in real-
time into selected monster vocalizations.
Index Terms: non-human voice conversion, monster sound
1. Introduction
The global gaming market expanded rapidly during the COVID-
19 pandemic as demand for stay-at-home, non-face-to-face en-
tertainment increased [1]. Recent massive multiplayer online
role-playing games (MMORPGs), such as Lineage W, now fea-
ture high-quality 3D graphics and 48 kHz high-fidelity sound to
improve realism. Medieval fantasy–themed games, inspired by
Dungeons & Dragons [2], often incorporate fictional monsters
such as dragons and orcs to enrich the virtual world. Achieving
such immersive environments requires extensive creativity not
only from graphic designers shaping a monster’s appearance but
also from sound designers crafting its voice—an effort that in-
curs significant costs. This study proposes a voice conversion
model that transforms human voices into non-human timbres,
reducing production costs. Section 2 examines the characteris-
tics of monster sounds. Section 3 reviews recent voice conver-
sion technologies and their limitations. Section 4 introduces the
proposed model, while Section 5 details the implementation and
demonstration of the model. Section 6 presents the conclusions.
2. The Monster Sound
Monster sounds in games occupy a unique position between
voice and sound effects. While they convey linguistic infor-
mation, making them voice-like, they also require extensive fil-
tering to achieve textures that define the identity of the monster,
classifying them as a specialized type of sound effect. Addition-
ally, they incorporate elements rarely addressed by conventional
speech technology, such as unvoiced sound (e.g., breathing) and
extreme vocal expressions like screaming. Therefore, produc-
ing high-quality monster voices demands exceptional perfor-
mances from voice actors and extensive postproduction work,

The production process generally involves three stages: (1)
defining the timbre of the target monster, (2) hiring a profes-
sional voice actor capable of approximating that timbre, and (3)
applying effects and modulation to refine the recorded voice.
The timbre of a monster is largely determined by its origin, size,
and sex. Employing skilled voice actors is costly because the
vocal range required is broader than standard narration and im-
poses greater strain. Finally, the recorded “seed voice” under-
goes post-processing to achieve the final monster timbre, typ-
ically using specialized tools, such as Dehumaniser1, a Digi-
tal Audio Workstation (DAW) plug-in. Producing high-quality
monster sounds requires trained personnel to operate these tools
effectively. Advanced voice conversion is anticipated to reduce
these time and financial costs.
3. Related Works
Voice conversion for ordinary speech has advanced to the point
where defenses against adversarial attacks must be considered
[3].
Additionally, techniques such as singing voice conver-
sion using latent diffusion models [4] achieve highly accurate
timbral reproduction, with users frequently sharing redubbed
singing videos on social media for entertainment. These mod-
els rely on speech features that capture human vocal character-
istics and are trained exclusively on human-speech datasets to
ensure generalization. However, monster voices, such as those
inspired by birds or tigers, span a wider frequency range than
human voices and contain intricate details shaped by various

4. Proposed Method
The model employed a 44.1 kHz sampling rate, STFT with a 20
ms window and 5 ms hop length, and Mel spectrograms cover-
ing 0–22.05 kHz to accommodate a broad frequency range and
preserve fine temporal details. A reference encoder extracted
the global style from Mel spectrograms, while self-supervised
perturbations captured linguistic features across pitch and for-
mant variations. Given the challenges in reliably estimating f0
for non-human sounds, frame-level energy is the main prosodic
cue.
The proposed architecture consisted of a CVAE-based pos-
terior encoder, decoder, prior encoder, and flow module, with
two key enhancements: incorporating a 5 ms hop-length lin-
ear spectrogram input and applying the style vector exclusively
within the prior and flow components. The prior encoder inte-
grated style embeddings, energy inputs, and SSL-derived lin-
guistic features, estimated latent distribution parameters, and
refined the sampled latent variable using a flow module.
Training employed a combined generator and discrimina-
tor losses, including KL divergence, feature matching, LS-GAN
adversarial loss, and reconstruction loss. The discriminator was
trained separately using adversarial loss. The model incorpo-
rated FDRL with multiple hop lengths and a DAC discriminator
to enhance temporal resolution to better handle the transient-
rich nature of non-human audio. Figure 1 shows the monster
sound converted into an Orc voice from the original human
voice using the proposed model.

5. Demonstration
The proposed model is packaged to run independently within
a Linux virtual machine environment, enabling users to inter-
act with it via a Windows-based web interface on the local
host. Figure 2 illustrates the Web UI used for the demonstra-
tion. Users begin by selecting a reference monster voice from
available target voices. Subsequently, they record their voices as
the source input using a Web Audio API–based AudioWorklet,
facilitating real-time browser access and playback for review.
Once the recording is completed, clicking the “convert” but-
ton transmits both the selected reference monster voice and the
recorded source voice to the H2NH voice conversion model
running on localhost. The converted voice is returned to the
web interface upon the transformation and played immediately.
This web-based implementation provides an intuitive and user-
friendly experience, requiring no complex setup while deliver-
ing instant feedback on transformation results. Consequently,
users can enjoy an engaging and interactive experience in this
demonstration.
6. Conclusion
This study introduces an efficient voice conversion model that
streamlines the production of high-quality monster voices in
MMORPGs, addressing the substantial costs and specialized
processes associated with conventional methods. The human-
to-non-human (H2NH) voice conversion model uses an ex-
panded frequency range and enhanced temporal resolution to
accurately capture and transform the intricate characteristics of
non-human voices. A real-time demonstration system was im-
plemented to validate the ability of the model to convert the
voice of a speaker into a realistic monster-like timbre. The pro-
posed approach can significantly reduce the time and expenses
required to produce monster voices in game development. Fu-
ture research will focus on extending the capabilities of the
model to a broader range of monster and speech domains, to
develop a general-purpose voice conversion system that main-
tains consistently high performance.
