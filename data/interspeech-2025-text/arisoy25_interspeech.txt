Evaluating Large Language Models in Data Generation for Low-Resource
Scenarios: A Case Study on Question Answering
Ebru Arisoy1, Merve Unlu Menevse2, Yusufcan Manav3, Arzucan Ozgur2
1Electrical and Electronics Engineering, MEF University, Turkey
2Computer Engineering, Bogazici University, Turkey
3Allianz Partners, Germany

arzucan.ozgur@boun.edu.tr
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are powerful tools for
generating synthetic data, offering a promising solution to data
scarcity in low-resource scenarios.
This study evaluates the
effectiveness of LLMs in generating question-answer pairs to
enhance the performance of question answering (QA) models
trained with limited annotated data. While synthetic data gener-
ation has been widely explored for text-based QA, its impact on
spoken QA remains underexplored. We specifically investigate
the role of LLM-generated data in improving spoken QA mod-
els, showing performance gains across both text-based and spo-
ken QA tasks. Experimental results on subsets of the SQuAD,
Spoken SQuAD, and a Turkish spoken QA dataset demon-
strate significant relative F1 score improvements of 7.8%, 7.0%,
and 2.7%, respectively, over models trained solely on restricted
human-annotated data. Furthermore, our findings highlight the
robustness of LLM-generated data in spoken QA settings, even
in the presence of noise.
Index Terms: spoken question answering, large language mod-
els, data generation
1. Introduction
Recent advancements in dataset augmentation techniques have
seen the integration of LLMs to enhance the quality and diver-
sity of training data. LLMs are prompted to generate synthetic
data, which can then be utilized to fine-tune a model, eliminat-
ing the necessity for manual data collection in large amounts.
This approach not only reduces the time and cost associated
with data curation but also allows for the generation of highly
customized datasets tailored to specific tasks.
LLMs have proven to be effective synthetic data genera-
tors across diverse domains [1], including commonsense rea-
soning [2], information retrieval [3], code-switching Automatic
Speech Recognition (ASR) [4], and QA [5, 6, 7]. While LLMs
help address data scarcity in many tasks, their use for dataset
augmentation also presents challenges, such as introducing fac-
tual inconsistencies through synthetic examples.
To address
these challenges, hybrid approaches that combine human ex-
pertise with LLM-driven annotation have been proposed to op-
timize data annotation processes [8, 9].
In this paper, we explore the potential of LLMs for QA data
generation, particularly in low-resource scenarios and spoken
QA tasks. Question generation has been shown to significantly
enhance QA model performance [10, 11, 12, 13]. Traditional
question generation approaches often involve fine-tuning gen-
erative language models [14, 15, 16], which typically require

extensive annotated data. However, in scenarios with limited
or no training data, alternative strategies include cross-lingual
knowledge transfer [17, 18, 19], few-shot learning [20], effec-
tive fine-tuning strategies [21] and leveraging LLMs for syn-
thetic data generation [5, 6, 7].
Our research utilizes LLMs to distill knowledge into a QA
model trained with limited annotated data.
Specifically, we
generate question-answer pairs from provided passages using
LLMs and integrate this synthetic data to enhance both text-
based and spoken QA models. Unlike approaches that rely on
deploying large-scale LLMs for direct downstream tasks, our
method demonstrates that small, efficient models can achieve
substantial performance improvements when augmented with
LLM-generated synthetic data. This finding is particularly rel-
evant for applications with limited computational resources.
To simulate low-resource scenarios, we employ small parti-
tions from SQuAD [22] and Spoken SQuAD [23], as well as
a low-resource Turkish spoken QA dataset. While synthetic
data generation has been widely explored for text-based QA,
its impact on spoken QA remains under-researched [21]. Our
study systematically investigates how LLM-generated data can
enhance spoken QA models, demonstrating significant perfor-
mance gains even in presence of ASR noise. Furthermore, by
focusing on a less-supported language, i.e., Turkish, we high-
light the potential of LLMs to handle multilingual challenges
and improve QA performance in low-resource languages.
Our work distinguishes itself from prior studies that uti-
lized LLMs for QA data generation, which primarily focused on
few-shot learning approaches [5, 6] or on creating spoken QA
datasets from acoustic data [7]. Instead, we use off-the-shelf
models, investigate their data generation performance for spo-
ken QA as well as text-based QA and evaluate their effective-
ness on publicly available and non-public datasets to mitigate
the potential risk of data leakage [1] – an important concern
when assessing LLMs on publicly available datasets. While
our research shares a similar emphasis on enhancing spoken
QA performance as in [21], our primary focus is on utilizing
LLMs for data generation and extending this capability to low-
resource languages beyond English.
2. Method

tasks. The data generation framework is illustrated in Figure 1.

question-answer pairs. In this stage LLM is tasked with pro-
ducing a specific number of questions (represented as “NBR” in
Figure 1) along with their corresponding answers from the given
paragraph. Since we are generating data for reading/listening

comprehension tasks, the answers are expected to be short, con-
secutive words within the same paragraph. However, as LLM
prompting does not consistently produce the desired output, we
may not always obtain the expected number of questions for the
given paragraph, and the answer may not necessarily be a span
of words within the paragraph. To address the former issue, no
specific action is taken, potentially leading to a reduced number
of questions. To tackle the latter issue, we introduced Stage-2
in data generation. After obtaining the question-answer pairs
from the LLM, we verify if the answer is indeed a consecutive
span of words within the paragraph. If this criterion is met,
the question-answer pair is included in the generated data along
with the corresponding paragraph. This verification process is
applied to all generated questions. If the answer fails to meet
the criteria, the process proceeds to Stage-2.
In Stage-2, the LLM is provided with the same paragraph as
in Stage-1, along with the question whose answer did not pass
the verification process. In this stage LLM is prompted to locate
the answer within the given paragraph. The generated answer
undergoes the same verification process as in Stage-1. If the
answer meets this criterion, the question-answer pair is included
in the generated data for the given paragraph. However, if the
answer does not satisfy the verification process, the question
is skipped. As a result, we may end up with fewer question-
answer pairs than desired. This 2-state procedure is applied to
all paragraphs in the unannotated data.
The prompts used to invoke the LLM are illustrated in
Figure 1. These prompts are specifically designed for ques-
tion generation from clean text.
When generating questions
from ASR transcriptions, the prompts are adapted to inform the
LLM about the potential presence of inaccuracies due to speech
recognition errors.
3. Experimental Setup
This section explains the data, models and setups utilized in
both question-answer generation and QA tasks.
3.1. Data
In our experiments, we used SQuAD [22], Spoken SQuAD [23]
and Turkish lecture [24]1 QA datasets.
SQuAD contains
100,000+ question-answer pairs across 23.2K paragraphs for
training, development, and test partitions. Spoken SQuAD con-
tains ASR transcriptions of SQuAD paragraphs, along with cor-
responding question-answer pairs. Spoken SQuAD excludes
pairs where the answer couldn’t be found in the ASR transcrip-
tions, resulting in approximately 37K and 5.3K question-answer
1This dataset is non-public and was used with permisson.
pairs for training and development sets. Additionally, the devel-
opment set is available in three versions – No Noise, Noise V1,
and Noise V2 – each with different word error rates (WERs)
of 22.7%, 44.2%, and 54.8%, respectively. The Turkish lecture
dataset was collected from lecture recordings of law courses.
It consists of approximately 18 hours of video recordings and
their corresponding reference and ASR transcriptions. The tran-
scriptions were segmented into short passages and manually an-
notated to generate QA pairs, resulting in 878, 286, and 287
pairs for the training, development, and test partitions, respec-
tively. Unlike the SQuAD datasets, this dataset contains longer
answers, averaging 15 words. Turkish lecture dataset is also
used for spoken QA by utilizing the ASR transcriptions of the
lecture recordings.
The Turkish lecture dataset represents a typical low-
resource QA scenario.
For data generation, we utilized ref-
erence transcriptions from approximately 12 hours of lecture
recordings within the same domain. Since the transcriptions
lacked predefined paragraph boundaries, we created pseudo-
paragraphs by segmenting the text into fixed-length chunks,
which served as unannotated Turkish data. To simulate low-
resource conditions for the (spoken) SQuAD datasets, we
prepared annotated data subsets containing 1K, 3K, and 5K
question-answer pairs, with each larger subset including the
smaller ones. For LLM-based QA generation, we selected raw
paragraph subsets that were disjoint from the annotated data,
aiming to generate approximately 1K, 5K, and 10K question-
answer pairs – matching the sizes of the original datasets.
3.2. Question-Answer Generation
To generate QA pairs from the SQuAD datasets using LLMs,
we employed four models: GPT-4o-mini [25, 26], Llama-3.2-
3B [27], Llama-3-8B [27], and Qwen2.5-7B [28]. All models
were run on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. Due to com-
putational constraints, we limited our experiments to models
with at most 8B parameters. We followed the framework de-
picted in Figure 1 for each model. Each paragraph was used to
prompt the LLM to generate a number of questions equivalent to
those in the original dataset, ensuring the generation of 1K, 5K,
and 10K synthetic question-answer pairs. The prompts primar-
ily followed the structure shown in the figure. Due to cost and
response limitations, we used GPT-4o-mini only for generating
1K and 5K question-answer pairs. The 10K question-answer
pairs were generated exclusively using the open-source LLMs.
Although Turkish is not officially supported by all the open-
source LLMs mentioned, we used them to assess their multi-
lingual capabilities and performance on a non-public dataset.

3.3. Question Answering
For the QA task in English, we utilized pretrained encoder-
only models such as BERT [29] and its variants (RoBERTa,
ALBERT [30]), as well as ELECTRA [31]. For the QA task
in Turkish, we employed BERTurk and the multilingual XLM-
RoBERTa [32]. These models were fine-tuned using both the
annotated data subsets and synthetically generated QA data.
Following the findings of [21], we adopted a two-step fine-
tuning strategy with the generated data. In this approach, the
QA model is first fine-tuned on the generated data and then fur-
ther fine-tuned on the manually annotated data. This strategy
has the advantage of preventing the model from predominantly
learning repetitive or inaccurate questions and answers that may
arise from automatically generated data.
The QA system was implemented using the HuggingFace
transformers library [33]. During fine-tuning, we set the batch
size to 4 and experimented with learning rates of 2e-5 and 3e-5.
The best epoch was selected based on F1 performance. Since
the test partition of SQuAD is not publicly available, we eval-
uated the QA performance on the official SQuAD and Spoken
SQuAD development sets. Hyperparameters were tuned on a
separate partition selected from the training set.
4. Results
While LLMs offer a convenient method for generating syn-
thetic data, they may introduce biases and factual inconsisten-
cies in question-answer pairs. Therefore, assessing the quality
of the automatically generated data is essential. In this study,
we evaluated the quality of generated examples using both di-
rect and indirect approaches. Although human evaluators pro-
vide reliable direct assessments, the process is costly and time-
consuming. To mitigate this, we also leveraged an LLM as an
evaluator for direct assessment. For indirect assessment, we
utilized the generated data in the downstream QA task and eval-
uated the effectiveness of the generated data.
4.1. Direct Evaluation of the Generated Data
To evaluate the automatically generated question-answer pairs,
we used GPT-4o-mini as an evaluator for data generated by
Llama and Qwen LLMs. Since only raw paragraphs were used
for data generation and the original question-answer pairs were
excluded, we employed metrics that do not require ground truth,
such as faithfulness and answer relevancy, to assess the qual-
ity of the LLM-generated examples. Faithfulness measures the
factual consistency of an answer with its context, while answer
relevancy evaluates how well an answer aligns with the gener-
ated question. Due to the nature of reading comprehension task,
faithfulness scores close to 1.0 are expected. As a result, we
focused on evaluating answer relevancy by generating a set of
artificial questions based on the response. These questions were
designed to reflect the content of the response, and the answer
relevancy score was computed as the average cosine similarity
between the embeddings of the input question and the gener-

English experiments were performed by fine-tuning the
encoder-only models described in Section 3.3 using both an-

4.2.1. QA Results
For the baseline English QA systems, we fine-tuned the
encoder-only models described in Section 3.3 using only an-
notated data subsets. The highest F1 score was achieved with
the ALBERT-xxlarge model, which has approximately 206M
parameters. To evaluate the impact of LLM-generated data on
smaller models, we also experimented with the ALBERT-large
model, containing around 17M parameters. While larger mod-
els generally outperform smaller ones, smaller models offer
benefits such as reduced computational costs and greater fea-
sibility for deployment in resource-limited environments. Af-
ter fine-tuning with just 1K annotated samples from the train-
ing partitions, ALBERT-large and ALBERT-xxlarge achieved
F1 scores of 78.0 and 89.2 on SQuAD (see Table 2), and 64.0
and 77.6 on Spoken SQuAD (see Table 3), respectively.

ALBERT models fine-tuned with question-answer pairs gen-

scores of models fine-tuned with a combination of 1K anno-

Previous experiments have shown that LLM-generated data ef-
fectively improve QA performance, especially when annotated
data is scarce (e.g., 1K question-answer pairs), highlighting the
importance of LLMs in low-resource scenarios. Here, we in-
vestigate whether this approach remains effective as the amount
of annotated data increases. Figure 2 presents F1 scores for
ALBERT models fine-tuned on 1K, 3K, and 5K annotated
question-answer pairs, as well as 10K generated data for Spo-
ken SQuAD. The 1K annotated data results were previously re-
ported in Table 3. As shown in the figure, increasing the amount
of annotated data reduces the gap between baseline models and
those trained with additional generated data. This effect is es-
pecially pronounced for ALBERT-xxlarge, while for ALBERT-
large, the improvements remain notable. These findings sug-
gest that LLM-generated data plays a more critical role in low-
resource scenarios, especially when using smaller models.

4.3. Turkish QA Results
For the baseline Turkish QA system, we fine-tuned the mod-
els described in Section 3.3 using the Turkish lecture QA
dataset. The highest F1 score was achieved with the multilin-
gual XLM-RoBERTa-large model (550M parameters). Addi-
tionally, we evaluated a smaller model, BERTurk (110M pa-
rameters).
XLM-RoBERTa-large and BERTurk achieved F1
scores of 83.3 and 80.4 in text-based QA and 82.9 and 77.9
in spoken QA. Table 5 presents F1 results for the models
fined-tuned with both LLM-generated and annotated data, with
columns showing text-based and spoken QA results, respec-
tively.
The LLM-generated dataset for Turkish contains ap-
proximately 1.5K question-answer pairs. The results demon-
strate that leveraging generated data is effective for both text-
based and spoken QA in Turkish, even in limited-resource set-
tings. Unlike English, Turkish is not officially supported by
many LLMs. Despite the low answer relevancy scores reported
in Table 1, achieving relative improvements of over 4% in text-
based QA and over 2% in spoken QA is promising for low-
resource languages and highlights the multilingual capabilities
of off-the-shelf LLMs.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we demonstrated that LLM-generated synthetic
data significantly enhances the performance of QA models, par-
ticularly in low-resource settings. Our results confirm that even
without fine-tuning, LLMs can effectively generate data to im-
prove both text-based and spoken QA tasks, including for lan-
guages they do not officially support, highlighting their multi-
lingual capabilities. Importantly, our approach leverages small,
efficient models that remain viable alternatives in an era domi-
nated by large-scale LLMs. Our findings reinforce the value of
small models, as they offer computational efficiency while still
benefiting from LLM-driven data generation. Future work can
focus on further improving synthetic data quality and extending
this approach to other low-resource languages beyond Turkish.
