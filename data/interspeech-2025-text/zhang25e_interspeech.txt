Effect of noise floor in room impulse response on speech perception under
spherical harmonics-based spatial sound reproduction
Yunqi C. Zhang, Dhruv Jagmohan, Hong Kit Li, C.T. Justine Hui, Yusuke Hioka
Acoustics and Vibration Research Centre, Department of Mechanical and Mechatronics
Engineering, University of Auckland, New Zealand
clara.zhang@auckland.ac.nz, justine.hui@auckland.ac.nz, yusuke.hioka@ieee.org
Abstract
The current study investigates the effect of noise floor in mea-
sured room impulse responses (RIR) on the reproducibility
of speech perception under spherical harmonics-based spatial
sound reproduction. Subjective listening test measuring the in-
telligibility of speech in noise was conducted under the spatial
sound reproduction implemented using practically measured
RIR with varying level of noise floor. The same test was also
conducted in the real rooms where the RIR were measured. The
comparison of the experimental results from the spatial sound
reproduction and the real room suggests using measured RIR
with low noise floor contributes to reproducing speech percep-
tion in real rooms accurately when the room is highly reverber-
ant. It also has an effect to improve the reproducibility when the
sound sources are located at 5 m but not at 2 m. Truncating RIR
to further remove the noise floor mostly did not help improve
the reproducibility regardless of the acoustics of the room.
Index Terms: spatial sound reproduction, spherical harmonics,
speech intelligibility, room impulse response, noise floor, repro-
ducibility
1. Introduction
Spatial sound reproduction virtually replicates the auditory per-
ception of sound in an arbitrary acoustic environment such as
sound source location and spatial extent of the space [1]. As
more equipment and tools become available to implement spa-
tial sound reproduction, its application areas have also been
extending rapidly.
Apart from various commercial applica-
tions, thanks to its high reproducibility of auditory perception,
there have been growing number of reports in hearing research
that use spatial sound reproduction to examine humans’ audi-
tory perception in varying acoustic environment [2, 3, 4]. This
emerging research methodology enables studying how humans
perceive speech in practical acoustic environments in a con-
trolled manner.
Many of such hearing studies using spatial sound repro-
duction adopted spherical harmonics-based (also known as
Ambisonics) techniques [2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].
When spherical
harmonics-based spatial sound reproduction is implemented,
the acoustical properties of the space are often specified by us-
ing room impulse responses (RIR), which encapsulates the in-
formation about the propagation of sound in the space. While
RIR can be either numerically simulated based on the physical
model of the space or practically measured in the real space [6],
Ahrens et al. [10] found that speech perception in the real space
was replicated most accurately when practically measured RIR
were utilised. However, in practical spaces where surrounding
environment is normally uncontrollable, quality of RIR mea-
sured can be often compromised due to various ambient noise
in the space. RIR measured in such a noisy environment typi-
cally suffers from non-decaying “noise floor” [11], which may
produce infinite reverberation effect [12] in the stimuli rendered
from the spatial sound reproduction. Using such low quality
RIR for implementing spatial sound reproduction would even-
tually degrade the reproducibility of speech perception in the
original space and invalidate/limit the value of the experimental
results collected under spatial sound reproduction. While some
previous studies investigated the reproducibility of speech per-
ception using spatial sound reproduction [10, 13, 14], there has
been no report of studies examining the effect of the noise floor
in RIR on the reproducibility of speech perception.
Various techniques have been studied for minimising the
noise floor in measured RIR. An approach to address the noise
floor is using measurement methods known to be robust to spe-
cific type of ambient noise [15, 16]. Another approach is post-
processing the measured RIR, which removes the noise floor
from the measurements [17], such as truncating the RIR to re-
move the parts impacted by the noise floor [18]. However, most
of these techniques have been developed for improving the ac-
curacy of estimating acoustical parameters, e.g. reverberation
time, which are typically calculated from measured RIR. The
effect on speech perception when the processed RIR are used
for spatial sound reproduction is yet unknown.
The current study investigates the effect of noise floor in
measured RIR on the reproducibility of speech perception un-
der spherical harmonics-based spatial sound reproduction. The
study particularly aims to answer the questions: i) how noise
floor in measured RIR used for spatial sound reproduction af-
fects the reproducibility of speech perception in the original
space, and ii) if removing noise floor by truncating RIR would
improve the reproducibility of speech perception. The study
intends to shed light on this niche but non-trivial challenge in
using spatial sound reproduction for hearing research.
2. Methodology
Subjective listening tests were conducted to evaluate the speech
intelligibility under spherical harmonics-based spatial sound re-
production (called sound reproduction test) using RIR with
varying level of noise floor. To obtain baseline performance,
the same listening tests were also performed in the actual rooms
where the RIR were measured (called Real room test). To eval-
uate the performance in terms of replicating spatial extent of
sound in the original rooms, the experimental design included
the reverberation time in the room, distance and angles of the
target speech from the listener, as varying parameters. This sec-
tion provides the summary of spatial sound reproduction im-
plemented including how the RIR with varying level of noise
floor were generated, the design of the listening tests, and the

2.1. Spatial sound reproduction
For implementing spatial sound reproduction, the current study
followed the set up used in the previous study [8] and employed
the techniques available in the SPARTA plugin1, which is an
open-source package for implementing spherical harmonics-
based sound reproduction. The system takes RIR measured by
a spherical microphone array as input, which are encoded to
the representation in the spherical harmonics domain by the Ar-
ray2SH plugin [19]. These RIR represented by spherical har-
monics are then decoded using the AmbiDEC plugin. The de-
coded RIR are convolved with arbitrary audio files to generate
the stimuli (see Section 2.3) to be rendered from the 32-ch loud-
speaker array installed in the anechoic chamber of the Acoustics
Laboratory at the University of Auckland.
2.2. Room acoustics and impulse response measurement
The current study utilised two rooms, seminar room and chapel
both located at the University of Auckland, for measuring the
RIR to replicate their acoustics in the sound reproduction test
as well as conducting the real room test. In each room, the
distance between the positions of the speaker and listener was
varied between 2 m and 5 m. Hence, overall speech intelligi-
bility was measured in four acoustics (2 rooms × 2 distances)
with distinct speech clarity (C50) [1], which are known to cor-
relate well with speech intelligibility in rooms. The acoustical
properties of the rooms are summarised in Table 1.
The RIR were measured in each acoustic condition. A 32-
ch spherical microphone array (MH Acoustics Eigenmike) was
placed at the centre of each room and oriented at a fixed angle
defined as 0°. A loudspeaker (Genelec 8020D) was placed at
four different angles: -45°, 0°, 45° and 180°, either 2 or 5 m
away from the microphone array. Figure 1 shows the positions
of the loudspeaker and microphone array along with the dimen-
sion of the rooms. The RIR were measured at 48 kHz sampling
rate using the open software tool ScanIR [20, 21] by playing a
swept sine signal from the loudspeaker. The length of the mea-
sured RIR was 2 s for the seminar room and 2.5 s for the chapel.
Overall, three types of RIR were examined in this study.
The two were RIR with different level of noise floor acquired by
varying the repetition of the swept sine signal for synchronous
averaging [16], a well-known technique to lower the noise floor,
between 6 and 40 resulting in decreasing the level of noise floor
by approximately 15 dB. These RIR with low and high level of
noise floor are called Low (noise floor) and High (noise floor),
respectively. In addition, the Low RIR truncated at the inter-
section time, the time instant where the noise floor exceeds the
energy decay [18], was also examined. This RIR is called Low
truncated. Figure 2 shows the energy decay curves [1] of the
RIR measured at 0° in each acoustic condition encoded to the
0-th order spherical harmonics (i.e. omni-directional), with the
positions of the intersection time.

2.3. Stimuli
The test stimuli consisted of target speech and babble noise ren-
dered simultaneously to simulate speech in noise. The target
speech was selected from the Bamford-Kowal-Bench (BKB)
sentence lists [22] in the SPANZ corpus [23], while the noise
was sourced from the NOISEX-92 corpus [24], both resampled
to 48 kHz. The sound sources were presented at the sound levels
of 50(±1) dBA for speech and 53(±1) dBA for noise, both mea-
sured at the listener’s position, resulting in a target-to-masker
ratio (TMR) of -3 dB. In both real and sound reproduction tests,
the position of the target speech was fixed at 0°, while that of
the noise was varied across the four spatial angles specified in
Figure 1. Four repetitions were made by varying the speech
sentence for each condition, and the target speech and babble
noise shared the same distance from the listener (2 m or 5 m).
2.4. Participants
The listening tests recruited in total 54 native New Zealand En-
glish listeners in Auckland, New Zealand, who are categorised
into four groups. Participants in Group 1 (N = 16, mean age =
21.3, sd = 0.77) undertook the test using Low and Low truncated
RIR whereas those in Group 2 (N = 16, mean age = 21.7, sd =
1.66) undertook the test using High RIR. Participants in Group
3 (N = 11, mean age = 25.5, sd = 3.06) undertook the test in
the real seminar room and those in Group 4 (N = 11, mean
age = 21.8, sd = 2.86) undertook the test in the real chapel. All
participants self-reported no hearing impairment at the time of
participating the experiment.
2.5. Procedure

sentences they listened to under noise through the graphical
user interface displayed on a monitor located in 0◦. While the
speech sentences and their order were kept consistent for all
participants, the combination of parameters (noise source an-
gles and source distances) applied to each speech sentence was
randomised between participants except the source distance of
the real room test due to logistical difficulty. For the sound
reproduction test, participants were seated at the centre of the

2.6. Marking and statistical analysis
The participants’ responses were marked according to the mark-
ing schedule used in the previous study using spatial sound re-
production for hearing research [8]. The resultant proportion
correct scores normalised from 0 to 1, called proportion correct
hereafter, were statistically analysed separately for each room
by linear mixed effect models using the lme4 package in R [25]
with the noise source angle, source distance and environment
(virtual sound environments realised by three types of RIR plus
the real room) as fixed effects and participant ID as a random
effect. The model fitting was conducted by the step function
from lmerTest [26], by including interactions between multiple
factors when the fitness of the model was improved. Post-hoc
analysis for pairwise comparison was implemented by emmeans
package [27] with correction of p-values by Tukey method.

3. Results and discussion
For the seminar room, a significant three-way interaction was
found between the source distance, noise source angle, and en-
vironment (χ2(31) = 293.2, p < .0001). For the chapel, sig-
nificant two-way interactions were found between the source
distance and noise source angle (χ2(3) = 13.88, p < .01), and
between the source distance and environment (χ2(3) = 26.12,
p < .0001).
Figure 3 shows the predicted models of proportion correct
in each room with respect to the noise source angle split by
source distance. Table 2 shows the pairwise contrast between
the proportion correct scores of the real room test and sound re-
production test (top three blocks) as well as between the scores
of the sound reproduction test using different RIR (bottom three
blocks). A score from the sound reproduction test being not
significantly different from that of the real room test suggests
that the spatial sound reproduction was able to reproduce the
speech perception in the real room. Similarly a non-significant
pair between the environments using different RIR means there
was no improvement or degradation of reproducibility by the
change of RIR. The results show that spatial sound reproduc-
tion using Low RIR successfully reproduced the speech percep-
tion in the (real) chapel, but not in the (real) seminar room. The
slightly higher noise level in the seminar room Low RIR com-
pared to the chapel Low RIR seen in Figure 2 may have caused
the difference. Notable improvement of reproducibility using
Low RIR instead of High RIR was observed when the distance
was 5 m but not 2 m in both rooms. Because the level of direct
sound received by the listener is reduced as the source distance
increases, the perception at 5 m would be more sensitive to the
reverberation effect caused by the noise floor. Further experi-

One of the key acoustical characteristics of spaces that
spatial sound reproduction is able to reproduce is the source
angle.
A well-known phenomenon in speech perception in
noise attributed to source angle is spatial release from masking
(SRM) [28], where speech intelligibility improves when the tar-
get speech and noise sources are arriving from different angles.
The reproducibility of SRM can be measured by looking at the
difference of speech intelligibility between the cases where the
noise source angle is 0° and other angles. Table 3 shows the
results of pair-wise comparison between noise source angles.
SRM is observed from the pairs with a significant difference
(p-values in boldface). Care needs to be taken to interpret the
responses at ±45◦, under the seminar2m condition in the real
room test (orange lines in the left plot of Figure 3(a) and the
values underlined in Table 3) because the responses from these
angles very likely suffered from ceiling effect [29] by observ-
ing 64 % of participants achieved the proportion correct being
one (answered all sentences correctly). By taking this fact into
account, it is very likely that SRM was also observed in these
cases but not represented by the significance. Overall, in the
seminar room, the results suggest the environments with the
Low RIR successfully reproduced the SRM observed in the real
room. This is not the case with High RIR at 5 m. Previous stud-
ies [8, 30] found SRM is reduced under highly reverberant envi-
ronments. The noise floor in the High RIR would have made the
virtual sound environment much more reverberant than the real
seminar room, resulting in the SRM to be diminished. Trun-
cated RIR also reduced the reproducibility by making the envi-
ronment less reverberant than the real room. In the chapel, on
the other hand, no interaction between the noise source angle
and the environment was observed, suggesting spatial sound re-
production replicated the SRM in the real chapel regardless of
the RIR used. As the environment was already highly reverber-
ant, the change of reverberation caused by the noise floor would
not have had significant effect on speech perception.
4. Conclusion
The current study examined the effect of noise floor in RIR on
the reproducibility of speech perception under spatial sound re-
production. The study conducted a listening test both in the real
room and under spherical harmonics-based sound reproduction
implemented by using RIR with different level of noise floor.
The study found using RIR with low noise floor contributed to
reproducing speech intelligibility in real room accurately but
only when the room was highly reverberant. It also improved
the reproducibility regardless of the room when sound sources
were located at 5 m from the listener. The effect of SRM was
also replicated when RIR with low noise floor was used. Trun-
cating RIR did not show notable benefits to the reproducibility.
