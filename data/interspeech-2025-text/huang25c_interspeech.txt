VoiceNoNG: Robust High-Quality Speech Editing Model without Hallucinations

Abstract
Voicebox and VoiceCraft are the current most representative
models for non-autoregressive and autoregressive speech editing,
respectively. Although both of them can generate high-quality
speech edits, we identify their limitations: Voicebox is not good
at editing speech with background audio, while VoiceCraft suf-
fers from the hallucination-like problem. To maintain speech
quality for varying audio scenarios and address the hallucination
issue, we introduce VoiceNoNG, which combines the strengths
of both model frameworks. VoiceNoNG utilizes a latent flow-
matching framework to model the pre-quantization features of a
neural codec. The vector quantizer in the neural codec provides
additional robustness against minor prediction errors from the
editing model, which enables VoiceNoNG to achieve state-of-
the-art performance in both objective and subjective evaluations
under diverse audio conditions. Audio examples and ablations
can be found on the demo page1 and supplementary material2.
Index Terms: neural speech editing, flow-matching model,
speech infilling
1. Introduction
In recent years, speech editing technology has rapidly gained
prominence, offering substantial benefits across various domains.
This technology enables users to seamlessly modify and en-
hance audio recordings [1], addressing issues such as slips of
the tongue, mispronunciations, or transient background noise.
Consequently, YouTubers and filmmakers can edit their speeches
without re-recording, significantly boosting productivity and re-
ducing costs. Below we first introduce recent speech editing
techniques, and then summarize the contributions of this paper.
1.1. Speech editing techniques
In the early stages of speech editing, the process primarily in-
volved cut-and-paste techniques to replace the speech segments
that needed to be changed with the target speech segments. How-
ever, this approach often resulted in unnatural intonation and
noticeable boundaries. Several works tried to address this issue
with voice conversion and text-to-speech (TTS) techniques fol-
lowed by manual manipulations with a user interface to ensure
natural boundary transitions [2, 3]. Recently, as speech synthe-
sis technology has become more advanced, methods for speech
editing have generally been evolved into two main approaches:
non-autoregressive speech infilling (inpainting) [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9],
and autoregressive causal generation [10, 11, 12]. For the train-
ing of speech editing models, the models take masked audio
along with its transcript as input, and the goal is to recover the

is simply masked, and the edited speech is generated according
to the new target transcript. Similar to speech synthesis, the
sequential dependency of autoregressive generation often results
in better speech quality, while the parallel generation of non-
autoregressive speech editing models allows for significantly
faster inference. An overview of the input and output of current
speech editing models are shown in Figure 1.
Among all these speech editing models, Voicebox [6]
and VoiceCraft [12] are the two most representative non-
autoregressive and autoregressive models respectively. Voicebox
is a flow-matching-based generative speech model trained to
perform speech infilling task by conditioning on both past and
future audio contexts with text transcript, making Voicebox a
versatile platform for various speech tasks, including zero-shot
TTS synthesis, speech editing, transient noise removal, and style
conversion. Furthermore, its flow-matching framework allows
it to directly learn to transform noise distributions into audio
data distributions, leading to more stable training, better gener-
alization, and faster sample generation compared to traditional
diffusion-based methods. Therefore, Voicebox outperforms pre-
vious state-of-the-art models while achieving up to 20 times
faster performance in zero-shot TTS generation [6].
On the other hand, VoiceCraft [12] is an autoregressive
model that fall under the Neural Codec Language Model
(NCLM) category. NCLMs first convert audio into discrete
tokens by a neural codec, which allows the model to learn better
sequential token dependency through a next-token prediction
task, and the predicted tokens can be further converted to high-
quality audio using the neural codec decoder. For speech editing,
the challenge lies in how to condition the causal generation on
bidirectional context to infill the masked neural speech codec to-
kens according to the text transcript. VoiceCraft is therefore built
upon a two-step token rearrangement procedure, comprising a
causal masking step and a delayed stacking step, to enable au-
toregressive generation with bidirectional context with efficient
multi-codebook modeling for high-quality generation.

1.2. Contributions
Despite both Voicebox and VoiceCraft are successful in some
speech editing conditions, we discover that they are not robust
enough to generate promising speech editing under various sce-
narios. Therefore, in this paper, we first identify the main reasons
for their limitations and propose a new SOTA speech editing
method, VoiceNoNG, which combines their key strengths and
eliminates those weaknesses. In summary, this paper makes
several significant contributions:
1) We identify that the hallucination-like problem makes
VoiceCraft difficult to follow the target transcription accurately.
This leads to a higher word error rate (WER), indicating harder
to follow the target transcription accurately.
2) We find that Voicebox struggles to generate good edited
speech with background audio, and we identify the HiFi-GAN
[13] vocoder might be the main reason for this problem.
3) We propose VoiceNoNG, a speech editing model that
owns the key strengths of Voicebox and VoiceCraft, achieving
SOTA performance in objective and subjective evaluations.
4) We find that the vector quantizer module in the neural
codec is crucial for developing a robust speech editing model.
2. Proposed VoiceNoNG
A good speech editing model should precisely follow the tar-
get transcript while ensuring naturalness by maintaining the
speaker’s characteristics and preserving the background audio
(e.g., noise, music, etc.) in line with the surrounding context.
In this section, we first discuss the drawbacks of Voicebox and
VoiceCraft, and then explain how we address these issues.
2.1. Issues of Voicebox and VoiceCraft
In our preliminary experiment, we observe that Voicebox is not
good at generating speech edits with background audio. We
suppose there are two reasons. First, Voicebox is trained on Lib-
riLight [14], which is a clean and large-scale audiobook corpus
without background. Second, Voicebox utilizes a HiFi-GAN
vocoder with Mel spectrograms as the audio feature, which has
shown not generalizing well to various non-speech audio scenar-
ios such as sound or music [15, 16]. A spectrogram example
is shown in Figure 2, with audio provided on our demo website
(section 2 of the website) and comprehensive experiments to
verify this as presented in Section 3.
On the other hand, although VoiceCraft can obtain a bet-
ter subjective preference, its WER is worse than the baseline
(please refer to Table 4 of [12]). After carefully checking its
generated speech, we identified that this higher WER is due
to attention errors (hallucinations), which make it difficult for
VoiceCraft to accurately follow the target transcription. Specifi-
cally, when the target transcript is complex (multiple instances
of the same token), VoiceCraft sometimes generates speech
with missing or repeated words. Recently, [17] also highlights
that autoregressive-based TTS models have this kind of attention
error, which often generates speech with repeating words, miss-
ing words, and misalignment. An example of attention errors is
presented in Table 1, with audio samples provided on our demo
website (section 3 of the website).

2.2.1. Utilizing Descript Audio Codec (DAC)
Inspired by VoiceCraft, which trained their own EnCodec [18]
as their speech tokenizer, we noticed that there are lots of recent
works showing great success in generating speech and audio
with neural audio codecs [16, 11, 12]. Therefore, we adopt
the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) [19] as the acoustic feature
for VoiceNoNG, which can be further converted back to audio
with its paired audio codec decoder. More DAC details will be
described in section 3.2.
Besides being trained on a diverse range of audio types and
sources, another reason for using DAC is its ability to compress
the features into a discrete latent space through a vector quan-
tizer (VQ). This provides additional robustness against minor
prediction errors from the editing model [20]. A small error,
as long as it is not larger than the token decision boundary, will
still be mapped to the correct token during VQ. This will be
verified in Section 3.6.
2.2.2. Latent flow-matching model
Flow-matching-based non-autoregressive models [21, 16, 6, 22]
and NCLM-based autoregressive models [23, 12, 11] are the
two main model categories of high-quality speech generation
for various tasks. However, since the hallucination-like problem
exists for NCLM-based autoregressive speech models, we’re
more willing to use the Voicebox-like flow-matching framework

3. Experiments
3.1. Training and testing sets for speech editing
Unless otherwise noted, we use GigaSpeech as the training cor-
pus, as its diverse audio conditions enable our model to better per-
form background-preserving speech editing. For evaluating dif-
ferent speech editing models, we utilize the RealEdit dataset [12],
a pioneering benchmark that features 310 manually-crafted ex-
amples reflecting real-world editing scenarios. This dataset in-
cludes tuples of original audio, original transcript, and edited
transcript, meticulously crafted to ensure grammatical correct-
ness and semantic coherence. RealEdit consists of 100 utterances
from the LibriTTS dataset [25], 100 from YouTube (sourced
from the GigaSpeech test set [26]), and 110 from the Spotify
Podcast dataset [27]. The edits span various types—insertions,
deletions, and substitutions—across different lengths, from short
(1-2 words) to long (7-12 words), with single or multiple spans.
For each speech editing model, we generate edited speech
for every tuple from the RealEdit dataset and then evaluate these
generated speeches using the metrics introduced in the following
subsections. During the objective evaluation, speech was gener-
ated with five different random seeds, and we reported the mean
scores along with the standard deviation (shown in parentheses).
3.2. Speech editing model setup
Our proposed VoiceNoNG combines the strengths of both Voice-
box and VoiceCraft, enabling our model to perform background-
preserving speech edits without the hallucination issues. To ver-
ify that our modifications lead to the desired improvements, we
compared our speech editing model with four other models: two
VoiceCraft models (with sizes 330M and 880M), and two Voice-
box models (using Mel-spectrograms as the acoustic feature,
trained on LibriLight and GigaSpeech respectively). The results
for VoiceCraft are based on the official code. Since no code and
model checkpoints are available for Voicebox, we reproduced
the results. Our Voicebox architecture settings are identical to
those reported in the original paper, which features 330M param-
eters. For a better comparison, our VoiceNoNG shares the same
architecture as Voicebox, except for the dimensions of the input
and output acoustic features are changed according to DAC.
To delve into more details, DAC comprises three mod-
ules: an encoder that encodes the input waveform into a fixed-
dimension vector sequence (pre-quantization feature), a residual
VQ module that discretizes the encoded representations into to-
kens, and a decoder that decodes the quantized representations
(post-quantization feature) back into the waveform. In our ex-
periments, we use the pre-quantization feature as our acoustic
feature and apply an extra CE loss calculated against the ground-
truth token labels to enhance the token prediction accuracy of
VoiceNoNG. To demonstrate the impact of this setting, we also
conduct an ablation study to highlight the benefits of modeling
pre-quantization features and incorporating the CE loss.
3.3. Objective evaluation for speech intelligibility: WER
WER is an appropriate metric for evaluating TTS and speech
editing since it provides a quantitative assessment of how ac-
curately a system generates speech according to text transcript.
Table 2 displays the WER results for various speech editing
models using Whisper-large-v3 [28] as the ASR model. The
WER is computed by comparing the transcribed text to the target
transcript provided by the RealEdit datasets.
As indicated in the table, all flow-matching-based methods
achieve lower WER than VoiceCraft, which is consistent with the
findings presented in the VoiceCraft paper [12] and Table 1 that
this is primarily due to the hallucination problem of VoiceCraft3.
Additionally, the results demonstrate that when the training cor-
pus for Voicebox is switched from LibriLight to GigaSpeech,
the WER decreases across all types of audio sources, and further
reducing the WER is achieved by changing the acoustic feature
from Mel-spectrogram to DAC (our proposed VoiceNoNG).
We also perform an ablation study for VoiceNoNG to il-
lustrate the advantages of modeling pre-quantization features
and adding CE loss.
When VoiceNoNG generates the pre-
quantization features, it must be further processed through the
VQ and DAC decoder to reconstruct the waveform, which offers
additional robustness against prediction errors as noted in Sec-
tion 2. In contrast, if VoiceNoNG outputs the post-quantization
features (similar to [24]), only the DAC decoder is required
for waveform reconstruction, which therefore yields the highest
WER on average. Incorporating CE loss helps improve the token
prediction accuracy, thereby also reduces the WER.
3.4. Objective evaluation for speech quality: SQUIM-(SI-
SDR)
To assess the quality of the generated speech, we utilize a non-
intrusive estimation of the scale-invariant signal-to-distortion
ratio (SI-SDR), referred to as SQUIM-(SI-SDR) from [29]. The
results of the estimated quality scores are presented in Table 3. It
is evident that the SI-SDR for the original speech from LibriTTS
is higher than that of the speech from YouTube and Spotify. This
finding corresponds with the characteristics of the datasets, as
the speech from YouTube and Spotify contains some background
audio. Although the SI-SDR scores offer less clear information
regarding the generated speech for YouTube and Spotify, we still
provide the scores for reference.
In the case of LibriTTS, we can observe that the speech
quality produced by VoiceNoNG is the best and closely resem-
bles the original speech, while the quality score for the speech
generated by Voicebox is the lowest, likely due to the limitations
of the Mel-spectrogram and the vocoder used. We also observed
that if VoiceNoNG utilized post-quantization features as the
acoustic feature, the SI-SDR would significantly decrease. Fur-
thermore, the standard deviations for Voicebox (Giga, Mel) and
post-quantization are considerably larger, which aligns with our

3.5. Subjective evaluation
Figure 3: Subjective scores of different speech editing methods.
To subjectively evaluate the audio quality of various speech
editing methods, we conducted a listening test using a 5-point
scale. Participants listened to audio files, which could either be
real or edited, and rated them on a scale where 5 represented
highly natural and unaltered audio, while 1 indicated a strong
belief that the audio had undergone partial editing (mainly fol-
lows [12]; please refer to Figure 5 in the supplementary material
for detailed instructions). 8 original speeches were randomly
selected from LibriTTS, YouTube, and Spotify, respectively. The
corresponding edited speeches from VoiceCraft, Voicebox, the
proposed VoiceNoNG, and VoiceNoNG with post-quantization
were then chosen, resulting in a total of (8×3)×5 = 120 utterances
for each listener to evaluate. 15 listeners participated in the study.
The experimental results are shown in Figure 3, revealing that
the naturalness of speeches edited by VoiceNoNG and VoiceCraft
is comparable to that of the original speech (we ask the listeners
to focus on audio quality while ignoring semantics, grammar,
etc., therefore the hallucination issue of VoiceCraft may not be
reflected). In contrast, the performance of VoiceNoNG with post-
quantization exhibits a significant decline, warranting further
analysis in the next section.
3.6. Robustness benefits from VQ module
Figure 4: Relationship between varying levels of noise added
to pre-quantization and post-quantization DAC features and the
PESQ scores of the resulting resynthesized speech.
As shown in the previous experiments, the performance of
VoiceNoNG declines when post-quantization features are used
as learning targets. To validate the advantages of predicting
pre-quantization features with VQ, we intentionally added Gaus-
sian noise to these features to simulate the prediction error
incurred by the editing model. LibriTTS subset from the
RealEdit dataset was chosen for this experiment. In Figure 4, we
present the PESQ scores [30] for resynthesized speech generated
from the noisy DAC features at varying noise levels (the noise
strength is first normalized based on the dynamic range of the
input features). This figure illustrates the robustness benefits of
VQ, showing an approximate improvement of 0.2 PESQ scores
under moderate noise conditions.
4. Ethical Impact
The same capabilities that make speech editing valuable for legit-
imate purposes can be exploited to create sophisticated Deepfake
audio [31], posing serious threats to public trust. Therefore, the
research community is increasingly focused on building robust
detection systems to identify manipulated speech [32]. Detailed
experimental setup and results of anti-spoofing are described in
our supplementary material, which demonstrates that the self-
supervised pre-trained detector achieves near-perfect (i.e., 97%)
utterance-level accuracy, even in more challenging conditions
where non-edited segments are also resynthesized from the DAC.
5. Conclusion
In this study, we first examine the limitations of current advanced
speech editing models. Voicebox produces lower-quality speech
in the presence of background audio (e.g., noise, music), while
VoiceCraft struggles to accurately follow text input, a common
hallucination-like issue with autoregressive models. To address
these challenges, we introduce VoiceNoNG, which leverages
the strengths of both models and achieves SOTA performance
in both objective and subjective evaluations (our demo page
shows VoiceNoNG can keep the accent and even successfully
generate background music for movie editing!). We also identify
the vector quantizer module in the neural codec as crucial for
achieving a robust speech editing model.
