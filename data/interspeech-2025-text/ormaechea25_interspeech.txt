Abstract
This study explores the synthetic data generation capabilities
of LLMs for French spontaneous speech simplification (S3), a
low-resource NLP task. We introduce the exo-refinement ap-
proach, which builds on the self-reflect workflow but differs by
using separate models for generation and evaluation. To address
the limitations of single-model refinement, it integrates external
feedback from distinct LLMs as judges, refining outputs based
on 3 task-specific dimensions. Comparing expert-based simpli-
fications from gold transcriptions to LLM synthetic outputs, re-
sults show that mistral-large outperforms all benchmarked mod-
els, including the MUSS baseline, and mistral-small achieves
competitive performance with few refinements. SARI results
confirm that iterations improve simplicity gain without compro-
mising semantic meaning, as shown by COMET scores. These
findings support exo-refinement as a scalable method for syn-
thetic data generation and future S3 model development.

1. Introduction
The generative capabilities of the latest LLMs present a promis-
ing avenue for NLP tasks where parallel data is scarce. This has
driven research on LLMs as text generation artifacts and have
spurred papers exploring prompt engineering techniques [1, 2]
and workflows for generating and evaluating synthetic data [3,
4, 5, 6]. These efforts aim to develop methods to produce syn-
thetic data that closely mirrors real-world data in terms of rep-
resentativity, trustworthiness and diversity.
This paper benchmarks various state-of-the-art LLMs
through a data creation pipeline exploring diverse prompting
strategies.
These are integrated into an iterative generation-
evaluation workflow to identify the most effective data creation
setup for a low-resource NLP task1: spontaneous speech simpli-
fication (S3) for French. S3 aims at reducing the linguistic com-
plexity of an utterance while preserving its original meaning
and grammaticality [7], thus making spontaneous transcripts
more accessible for several target audiences (e.g., people with
cognitive impairments, non-native speakers, etc.), but also pro-
viding an intermediate representation in NLP pipelines involv-
ing speech, such as subtitle [8] or pictograph translation [9].
To address the challenges of generating high-quality
synthetic simplifications, we build upon the self-refinement
generation-evaluation workflow and propose an approach where
the tasks of generation and evaluation are handled by separate
models, which we coin as exo-refinement. This approach en-
ables the infusion of feedback from a distinct LLM evaluator

into the LLM generator, addressing the limitations of relying
on a single model for both roles [10]. Our goal is to examine
how successive iterations within an exo-refinement approach in-
fluence the quality of the generated candidate simplifications,
either aligning them more closely to human-crafted versions
or rather causing a decline in quality. Identifying the optimal
configuration (in terms of model pair, generation strategy and
number of iterations) may allow us to scale our data generation
workflow and produce a high-quality large-scale synthetic set.
Our contributions are as follows: i) we introduce a varia-
tion of the self-refinement workflow, termed exo-refinement, to
generate synthetic simplified outputs from gold transcripts; ii)
we release a small-scale set of paired spontaneous transcripts
with 4 expert-based simplifications; and iii) we present the first
dedicated solution to automate S3, which far outperforms state-
of-the-art text simplification models when applied to speech.
2. Related work
2.1. Spontaneous speech and simplification
S3 is an emerging task and draws inspiration from a well-
established NLP field, i.e., text simplification (TS). TS has been
widely studied across various written domains such as news ar-
ticles, Wiki-based content and healthcare documents [11, 12].
Much like its written-centric counterpart, S3 seeks to re-
duce linguistic complexity while retaining the original mean-
ing and grammaticality. However, most studies have focused
on English, leaving simplification under-explored in other lan-
guages like French, where sentence-level simplification remains
sparsely studied [13, 14].

While S3 may seem like a mere change in input modality
with respect to TS, complexity manifests differently in speech,
which varies from written language in ways that extend the
transmission mode, impacting lexical, syntactic and morpho-
logical aspects [16, 17]. Unlike written texts, which are planned
and finalized, spoken texts are an ongoing (and thus unfin-
ished) process. Spontaneous utterances diverge from the sen-
tence structure typical of a written modality, since the discourse
thread is less reliant on complete sentences due to cognitive
constraints in real-time production. Instead, speech unfolds in

after the other as it progresses. This leads to disfluent traces like

spontaneous speech, given its dialogic nature. Thus, meaning
is often not confined to the segment level but derives from the
shared interaction between speakers, making the simplification
process eventually dependent on prior context.
2.2. LLM-based synthetic data generation
Recent research highlights LLMs as an efficient and practical
tool for generating synthetic data to train AI models, especially
when human-crafted data is pauce. This is due to two features:
i) extensive pre-training [22], which equips them with factual
and linguistic knowledge to produce coherent human-like text;
and ii) instruction-following capabilities, which confer models a
wide adaptability over textual generation, making them suitable
for domain-specific data generation [23, 24], even with limited
or no task-specific seed exemplars [25].
LLMs provide extensive customization by allowing users
to create and specify attributes in prompts through natural lan-
guage instructions. However, LLMs lack controllability over
outputs, due to their inherent non-determinism and the biases
learned during pre-training.
This can lead to skewed syn-
thetic datasets [10, 26], factual inaccuracies (i.e., hallucina-
tions) which can ultimately lessen their reliability in generating
trustworthy data. Diversity also poses challenges, given that a
low one may result in highly monotonous outputs that fail to
capture the varied nature of real-world data [22], while a high
one can lower accuracy and factual correctness, compromising
the trustworthiness of the outputs [5]. Balancing these factors
is crucial, as both extremes risk degrading downstream model
performance and potentially lead to model collapse2.
This underscores the need to distinguish high-quality exam-
ples from repetitive ones. While human assessment remains the
gold standard, it is notoriously costly in terms of time and bud-
get [27]. This has opened the door to a new paradigm: LLM-
assisted evaluation, where machines assess outputs, providing a
scalable approach to approximate human preferences [28]. Re-
cent literature presents contrasting views. Optimists see LLM-
assisted evaluation as a fast and inexpensive surrogate for hu-
man assessment [29], highlighting LLMs’ ability to refine out-
puts through self-refinement, with models being able to rectify
errors and produce improved outputs based on their own feed-
back [3, 4]. Skeptics, however, warn about LLMs’ assessment
capacities, given their tendency to exhibit biases, often related
to specific positions or verbosity [28]. Suboptimality can also
be caused by self-bias, leading to overfitting [30], failure to cor-
rect mistakes [31] and data diversity harm, especially in smaller
models [10]. This suggests the need to integrate external feed-
back into these workflows to mitigate the impact of such effects.

3. Data
3.1. CEFC speech dataset
S3 remains an underexplored task, compromising the availabil-
ity of parallel data. In order to evaluate the quality of LLM-
based simplifications, we resorted to CEFC (Corpus d’ ´Etude du
Franc¸ais Contemporain), a reference corpus for contemporary
French spontaneous speech [15], and gathered representative
spontaneous utterances. CEFC contains ≈270 hours of acoustic
data from ≈2, 500 speakers across diverse contexts (e.g., lec-
tures, medical consultations, everyday life interactions, among
others). Dialogues are distributed across 12 subcorpora and are
segmented into audio clips, each representing a conversation.
3.2. Expert-based simplifications from spontaneous speech
The CEFC corpus includes sentence-aligned segments with au-
dio and morphosyntactic annotations, but lacks ground truth
simplifications.
To address this, we launched a simplifica-
tion campaign involving 4 expert translators, all native French
speakers with backgrounds in translation studies and current po-
sitions in the same field. To manage the large source corpus
and the high cost of obtaining large-scale manual simplifica-
tions, we extracted a small representative subset of CEFC us-
ing proportional allocation sampling. The population, namely
CEFC, was divided into 12 distinct strata, each corresponding
to a subcorpus. From each stratum, we extracted 10 consec-
utive segments from 2 conversations, resulting in a set of 240
gold transcripts (12 strata × 2 conversations × 10 segments) to
be simplified by the enlisted professionals (see Table 1).

The simplification task was hosted on the LimeSurvey plat-

4. Method
4.1. Model specialization through exo-refinement
Our approach, coined exo-refinement, builds on the self-
refinement paradigm with a key modification: the generation
and evaluation tasks are conducted by distinct models. This
variation allows us to explore the potential of incorporating ex-
ternal feedback in the form of a separate model with advanced
reasoning capabilities, addressing issues in prior research that
arise from the use of a single LLM for all roles [10, 28]. We in-
vestigate whether an iterative generation-evaluation loop, inte-
grating an LLM-assisted evaluation, approximates human intu-
ition and leads to progressively closer alignments with human-
crafted simplifications or, conversely, degrades prior outputs.

We thus propose a model specialization of the two above-
mentioned tasks:
1. Generation, which involves both the initial generation and
the further refinements, based on the feedback provided by
the evaluation (or judge) LLMs. Generation LLMs were in-
structed to create outputs using various prompting strategies.
2. Evaluation, which involves 3 independent judges that assess
3 core dimensions of simplified texts, with a top evaluator
reviewing their scores and making a final decision to accept
or reject the proposed simplification.

4.2. LLMs as generators
Instruction-tuning enables LLMs to generalize to new tasks us-
ing natural language prompts. However, given the novelty of the
examined task, we hypothesized that instructions alone might
not suffice to convey its nuances.
We thus explored several
prompting techniques to extend the range of candidate simpli-
fications for each input. To generate French outputs with mul-
tilingual LLMs, we used English prompting templates, as re-
cent studies [32, 33] show English prompts paired with non-
English examples yield better performance, which likely stems
from Anglocentric data representation during pre-training. The
prompts designed3 were: i) zero-shot, where only the task
context and generation instructions are provided; ii) one-shot
and iii) few-shot, where one or several task-specific demonstra-
tions are used to implicitly inject human guidance [22] into the
prompt; iv) multi-step, which couples multiple steps, each de-
pendent on the previous output, guiding the model through the
specificity of the task [34]; v) chain-of-thought, where human-
created rationales encourage the model to break down reasoning
into a sequence of steps that lead to a conclusion; vi) chain-
of-thought with self consistency, which generates multiple rea-
soning paths for the same query to select the most consistent
answer [35]; and vii) tree-of-thought, which explores multiple
reasoning pathways simultaneously [2], enabling the model to
evaluate different approaches in parallel in order to make a final
decision.

4.3. LLMs as judges
As discussed earlier, we defined 3 independent LLM-based
judges to assess 3 classic dimensions of simplified texts using a
criteria-based evaluation, namely:
• Simplicity gain and meaning preservation, which were evalu-
ated using a 3-point Likert scale (1 being the worst and 3 be-
ing the best), and were conceived as ranking tasks. The eval-
uation was conducted in a reference-guided manner: compar-
ing the simplified outputs against the original utterances.
• Grammaticality, which was treated as a reference-free clas-
sification task, and assessed using a binary label: 1 for non-
grammatical and 0 for grammatical.
For the evaluation templates, we used a uniform structure:
i) we first provided the judge LLM with a task description; ii)
we later defined each score value; and iii) we outlined the out-
put template, including a numerical score and its reasoning. The
decision to proceed to a new refinement is made by a top evalu-
ator, who after reviewing the reasoned scores from the judges,
provided a final judgment to accept or reject the simplification.

4.5. Compared methods
S3 lacks task-specific resources that enable its automation us-
ing existing models. Consequently, we made use of MUSS [13]
as a baseline, the latest reproducible model for sentence-level
French TS. MUSS is an unsupervised TS model trained on para-
phrasing data, using controllable parameters to adjust length
and lexical attributes. We used it in inference mode to produce
candidate simplifications from the 240 input utterances.
5. Results and discussion
In this section, we analyze candidate simplifications generated
from the exo-refinement approach and compare them to the col-
lected expert simplifications using SARI, BLEU5 and COMET6
metrics. To this respect, if a candidate simplification is accepted
before the 5-iteration limit, the last output is copied into the next
ones. This ensures scores are compared using the same number
of outputs across all iterations.
Table 3 shows the best results obtained for the 3 metrics
across all model configurations.
As observed, mistral-large
stands out as the best performing model, achieving the high-
est SARI score in the fourth iteration and the highest BLEU
score in the first one, while also accomplishing it with one of
the lowest number of generated outputs, second only to mistral-
small (see the leftmost plot of Figure 2). Additionally, it falls
only 0.1 points behind the leading model (i.e., gpt-4o) in terms
of COMET. In light of these results, we also see that the pro-
posed approach consistently surpasses the established baseline
by several points, with the sole exception of open-mistral-7b,
whose best SARI result remains below MUSS. Furthermore,
SARI consistently boosts after the initial generation, highlight-
ing the value of an iterative approach for the task at hand.
In contrast, Table 3 reveals the opposite trend for BLEU:
the highest scores are obtained in the first iteration, followed by
a slight decline in subsequent ones. This indicates that the out-
puts generated in later cycles diverge lexically from the initial
guess, resulting in reduced surface overlap. This pattern reflects
the acknowledged inverse SARI-BLEU relationship: as sim-
plicity gain increases, meaning preservation tends to decrease.

6. Conclusions
In this paper, we present an iterative generation-evaluation ap-
proach to examine the simplification capabilities of a set of Ope-
nAI and Mistral LLMs. Outputs were compared to expert sim-
plifications, and results showed that mistral-large outperformed
all other models tested, including the MUSS baseline. More-
over, mistral-small required the fewest refinements and reached
very competitive performance compared to the other LLMs.
Techniques such as zero-shot and multi-step yielded the highest
results in terms of SARI, BLEU and COMET, demonstrating
the potential to scale this approach for large-scale data genera-
tion and the further development of an S3 downstream model.
We acknowledge several limitations in this study.
We
benchmarked only a subset of recent LLMs, and experiments
used gold transcriptions rather than speech signals. As further
work, we plan to integrate this approach with an ASR system.
Also, experiments were conducted on a small corpus, given the
limited S3 resources and the need to balance workload with
quality in the expert simplification task. Finally, while auto-
matic scores indirectly correlate with human correctness via
ground-truth simplifications, LLM-as-a-judge scores may re-
quire human calibration for a more comprehensive assessment.
