Abstract
Anomaly sound detection is an important audio task, with
various applications in industrial monitoring, healthcare, secu-
rity and surveillance, and other domains. Existing methods are
often designed with various assumptions for specific types of
anomalies and are not suitable when applied to the challenging
real-world scenarios, such as domain shifts and data scarcity
issues.
In this work, we propose a few-shot training-free
method, leveraging pre-trained audio models to extract patch-
based spatial-temporal representations for few-shot anomaly
detection and segmentation. We show that our proposed ap-
proach is flexible and applicable even under the extreme low-
shot regimes, and can at times outperform models trained with
the full datasets. Furthermore, our method is more robust in
tackling domain shifts, with the need of only few-shot data
points to quickly adapt to various conditions, therefore more
suitable for deployment in real-world applications.
Index Terms: anomaly sound detection, machine condition
monitoring, few-shot learning, training-free methods
1. Introduction
Anomaly sound detection (ASD) is an important and practical
task with numerous applications, including monitoring machine
operating conditions, conducting end-of-line testing in manu-
facturing pipelines [1–4], and healthcare [5, 6]. Audio can be
a complementary or alternative sensory modality that is cost-
effective, non-contact and ubiquitous (unlike, e.g. vibration,
torque, temperature or most bio-sensing) and robust to low vis-
ibility and occlusion (unlike, e.g., vision).
Conventional reconstruction-based [7, 8] methods usually
utilize an autoencoder (AE) architecture, requiring certain num-
ber of normal samples to fit a distribution to identify anomaly
outliers. In the field of vision, recent generative models like
GANs and diffusion models are also utilized to reconstruct nor-
mal data distribution for anomaly detection [9,10]. These meth-
ods require lots of domain-specific data and fail to generalize to
new domains. Deviation-based methods (e.g., DevNet [11]) re-
lax the constraint of only training with normal samples, mixing
few anomaly samples with larger normal sets to obtain better re-
sults. Furthermore, clustering [12, 13] and classification-based
techniques [14, 15] turn the original outlier detection problem
into a binary classification task, treating normal samples from
different sources (e.g., different machines) as anomalies to es-
tablish a more compact decision boundary. This approach typi-
cally requires significant amounts of labeled data. There is also
growing interest in leveraging large language models (LLMs)
toward building foundation models for anomaly detection, es-

such methods directly to audio, as common natural language de-

available. Also, unlike vision, the background component is not
occluded by the source of the anomaly, but additively combined.
Thus, few shot solutions, that can easily adapt to new domains

As highlighted in the discussion and results for Task 2 of
the 2024 DCASE challenge [20, 21], existing approaches are
often impractical and unfeasible for real-world applications due
to data scarcity issues. For example, in the case of machine
anomaly detection in industrial applications, the performance
of models may decrease for machine types not seen in the train-
ing data. Also, the assumption of collecting normal and partic-
ularly abnormal samples from every single machine type is im-
practical [22]. In such scenarios, pre-trained audio models on
large-scale data are often utilized to address generalization is-
sues across various data distributions [23–25]. Therefore, few-
shot ASD approaches have recently become important research
topics [26–30]. These works leverage few-shot learning [31,32]
for adapting pre-trained normal models to new domains with
few anomaly samples.

for the ASD task. We demonstrate the effectiveness and gen-
eralizability of our proposed methods across diverse datasets
including machine anomaly and healthcare domains. Our con-
tributions are: 1) We propose a few-shot, training-free ASD ap-
proach that requires minimal data samples and is applicable to
a wide range of real-world conditions. 2) Our approach demon-
strates robustness to domain-shifts and cross-domain general-

2. Few-shot Patch-based Difference Method
We aim to rapidly adapt to domain shifts (e.g., changes in
machine configurations) without requiring extensive data col-
lection and model fine-tuning, while maintaining high perfor-
mance and robustness against variations. Therefore, we formu-
late the problem as few-shot anomaly detection, relying on only
a limited number of available normal samples. Our method
is inspired by WinCLIP [16], a vision-based anomaly detec-
tion framework that computes anomaly scores by leveraging vi-
sual differences. Differently, we dispense the language com-
ponent entirely to account for the non-availability of textual
descriptions for audio anomaly, relying solely on intermediate
patch-level audio features from a pre-trained transformer en-
coder. This way, our approach is not tied to a specific encoder,
any transformer-based encoder trained on large-scale data can
be integrated into our framework. Another closely related work
[33] also utilizes hidden representations from a pre-trained au-
dio transformer for anomaly detection. However, their method
targets a low-shot setting (approximately 200 or more normal
samples), whereas our approach focuses on a more challenging
few-shot regime (fewer than 10 normal samples). In this study,
we use the audio spectrogram transformer (AST) [34] encoder,
which is trained based on the vision transformer (ViT) architec-
ture [35]. AST is a widely adopted transformer-based general
purpose audio encoder in various research studies [36,37].

free inference pipeline focusing on the exposure of local differ-

lic and private datasets to showcase the generalizability of the
proposed approach across various domains. For this, we con-
sider a total of five datasets (three public and two private) to
comprehensively evaluate the model robustness and generaliza-
tion across different domains and conditions. Table 1 shows
the detailed data statistics, namely: MIMII [38]: The dataset
comprises four machine types recorded at different SNRs
and machine IDs; DCASE2024-Task2-Development (DCASE-
Dev) [38, 39] and DCASE2024-Task2-Additional (DCASE-
Eval) [21,39]: These two datasets are from DCASE2024-Task2
Challenges, containing various machine types, recorded in both
source (SRC) and target (TGT) domains, with balanced test
set2; Kadia: This is a Bosch proprietary dataset collected in the
manufacturing line. The anomaly comes from misalignment of
the honing tool-tip, causing a squealing sound; Asthma [40]:
This is another private dataset in the healthcare domain, featur-
ing normal coughing and coughing with wheeze. The samples
containing wheezing are classified as anomalies. This data was
collected during patients visit to their healthcare provider.
For MIMII and the two private datasets, we follow the parti-
tioning strategy adopted from [38]. To create a balanced test set,
we use all of the anomaly samples along with the same number
of randomly selected normal samples. The remaining normal
data is used for training. Notice that we randomly craft a small
subset (i.e., 10 shots of normal and abnormal samples) from
MIMII as the validation set to study the role of hyperparame-
ters for the proposed approach (see Sec. 4.1). For the DCASE
datasets, we respect the official train and test splits.
Baseline: We consider autoencoder (AE) [41] as our baseline
method, which is the most conventional approach for bench-
marking anomaly detection systems [8, 21]. The AE model is
trained to reconstruct normal samples, and the reconstruction

4. Results and Discussions
4.1. Hyperparameter Study
There are two main hyperparameters to consider for the pro-
posed AST-PB method: 1) the choice of the intermediate net-
work layers L (i.e., from 1 to 11) that are used in calculating the
anomaly map Aq; and 2) the percentage quantile (e.g., 0.05, 0.1,
..., 0.95) of the anomaly score decision threshold. We use the
small crafted MIMII validation set (Sec. 3) to tune these hyper-
parameters. Firstly, we observe that utilizing all L layers yields
the most robust results. Secondly, Figure 2 shows the impact
of decision threshold (via percentage quantile) on AUC perfor-
mance of the validation set. We observe that different machines
do require different thresholds to achieve their best performance
(see the solid dots per machine). Although we recognize that
tuning hyperparameters could improve performance, we adhere
to the official benchmark setup in the DCASE Challenge for the
rest of the experiments. For fair comparisons, we fix the hyper-
parameters obtained from MIMII validation knowledge (i.e., all
L layers and a 0.05 decision quantile) across all experiments
and datasets. We also provide the ideal results as a reference,
denoted as AST-PB (10HT), which would be achieved if we
were able to further tune the quantile for each machine individ-
ually. Here HT stand for hyperparameter tuning.

4.3. Cross-Domain Robustness
Table 3 and Table 4 show the detailed results in SRC and
TGT domains from DCASE-Dev and DCASE-Eval, respec-
tively. Firstly, looking into the SRC domain results, we ob-
serve comparable or sometimes superior performance between
the AE (N) results and AST-PB (N). However, for this dataset,
AE (all) demonstrates better performance on average. Look-
ing closer, there are several machine types that AST-PB demon-
strates better performance, such as GearBox, ToyTrain; while
worse at ToyCar. Similar findings hold for both DCASE-Dev
and DCASE-Eval sets.
We speculate that the variance in performance may be at-
tributed to the characteristics of the anomaly types (e.g., pe-
riodic vs. aperiodic/impulsive, stationary vs. non-stationary),
where AST-PB and AE may each have their own advantages in
handling different types. It would be an interesting future di-
rection to take different characteristics of anomaly types into
consideration and design targeted specific score function (S).
Another hypothesis is that different machines or anomaly types
require different set of hyperparameters, as shown in Figure 2
as we see a consistent improvement of AST-PB (10HT) results
if we are able to perform hyperparameter tuning properly.
Additionally, the results from the TGT domain could pro-
vide us with a better understanding of the performance of these
approaches, as TGT simulates real-world scenarios where ma-
chine operation could constantly change, and only a few-shot
samples are available (i.e., 10 shots in the DCASE setup) for
unsupervised domain adaptation. We note substantial perfor-
mance improvements of the proposed AST-PB approach over
the AE baselines, even when compared to the one trained on the

4.4. Does Adding More Shots Help?
We found that adding more shots beyond 10 does not seem to
benefit AST-PB as shown in Figure 3, the average performance
tends to saturate while the STD decreases. We consider that
it might because the comparisons between all the sub-patches
between the support sets and query is getting noisier when the
number of comparisons grows. This suggests that the quality
of support set samples is more favorable than their quantity,
with a few representative prototypes being more critical. An-
other interesting future direction would be to explore intelligent
sampling strategies for selecting a better support set, such as
importance sampling [42].

4.5. Anomaly Sound Segmentation
One additional advantage of the proposed approach is that it
can localize spatio-temporal anomalies as we compute anomaly
score per sub-patch. The overlay in the bottom of Figure 4
shows the most different patches when compared to the support
sets. We can clearly see that the anomaly pattern in about 7s is
captured. The overlay particularly emphasizes the energy sweep
across the entire frequency region, with these regions contribut-
ing significantly to the final anomaly score.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we proposed a few-shot patch-based difference ap-
proach for anomaly sound detection and segmentation. Our ap-
proach relaxes the assumptions regarding normal and anomaly
samples during training, and can adapt to new domains with
minimal samples. For future work, we aim to explore sampling
methods for selecting support sets and find representative sub-
patches for different machine anomalies. We also plan to intro-
duce a data-driven network to predict anomaly scores based on
the similarity maps, and design losses for pre-training and fine-
tuning AST encoders to further emphasize sample differences.
