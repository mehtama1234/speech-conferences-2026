Position also matters! Separating Same Instruments in String Quartet using
Timbral and Positional Cues
Yuetonghui Xu1, Yiwen Wang2, Xihong Wu2, Xiaobing Li1, Feng Yu1
1Department of Music AI and Music IT, Central Conservatory of Music, China
2School of Intelligence Science and Technology, Peking University, China
{22a057, lxiaobing, yfeng}@mail.ccom.edu.cn, pku wyw@pku.edu.cn, wxh@cis.pku.edu.cn
Abstract
String quartets inherently face challenges such as funda-
mental frequency overlap, similar timbres, and complex acous-
tic environments, making existing source separation methods
relying solely on timbral characteristics less effective. This pa-
per proposes a novel approach that enhances separation perfor-
mance by encoding timbral and spatial positional cues to guide
a neural network for source separation. To evaluate our method,
we created a dataset of string quartet recordings with realistic
reverberation with two different seating arrangements. Experi-
ments demonstrate that our approach outperforms current state-
of-the-art (SOTA) methods in source separation, achieving su-
perior performance even in seating arrangements different from
training, thus highlighting the importance of our timbral and
positional encoding in improving separation quality and gener-
alizability.
Index Terms: music source separation, classical music, string
quartet, conditional source separation
1. Introduction
Music source separation (MSS) has made significant progress
with the advent of deep learning, enabling the extraction of in-
dividual sound sources from complex audio mixtures [1, 2, 3, 4,
5, 6]. However, the current development in this field has been
heavily influenced by the limitations of existing datasets, such
as MUSDB18-HQ [7], MedleyDB [8], and FMA [9], which pri-
marily feature modern pop and rock music styles. As a result,
most music source separation research is focused on the Sony
De-mixing Challenge [10], where the goal is to separate indi-
vidual stems—such as vocals, bass, drums, and other instru-
ments—after they have been mixed and subjected to various
non-linear effects like gain, delay, and reverb. In practice, it
has been proven feasible using advanced deep learning archi-
tectures, such as BS-Roformer [6], Demucs [3], and Band-split
RNN [11]. However, these methods typically rely on separating
stems based on distinct timbres, thus losing their effectiveness
when it comes to separating instruments with similar timbres.
In chamber music, due to the demands of composition,
there exists multiple instances of homogeneous musical instru-
ments(e.g., more than one violin) and instruments from the
same family (e.g., the violin family, including violins, violas,
and cellos). These instruments share similar physical structures,
leading to close timbres and significant spectral overlap, which
greatly increases the difficulty of source separation [12]. More-
over, unlike the linear summation inverse operations used in pop
or rock music separation, the natural reverberation in concert
halls introduces a nonlinear mixing process [13, 14, 15]. This
nonlinearity further complicates the source separation task.
Separating sources in chamber music settings offers several
promising practical applications. It can enhance the quality and
efficiency of live recordings by improving post-production ca-
pabilities, allowing selective re-recording of specific parts, and
reducing overall recording costs. Additionally, it holds the po-
tential for transforming traditional stereo recordings into im-
mersive 3D surround sound experiences by accurately extract-
ing individual sound sources [16]. From a social welfare per-
spective, it could also benefit individuals with hearing loss by
enabling personalized adjustments of instrument volumes, as
demonstrated in projects like the Cadenza initiative [17]. These
practical applications highlight the need for more advanced sep-
aration methods tailored to the unique acoustic challenges of
chamber music, such as those presented by string quartets.
Motivated by above, in this paper, we focus on string quar-
tet(consisting of two violins, a viola and a cello), the typical sit-
uation of chamber music, to explore source separation with sim-
ilar timbre instruments under complex reverberant conditions.
As shown in Figure 1, although the arrangement of these instru-
ments is uncertain, the position candidates are fixed, which pro-
vides valuable positional cues that can be leveraged to improve
separation performance. Specifically, we incorporate timbral
and positional cues to guide a neural network for source separa-
tion, achieving results of each instrument in the string quartet,
which as far as we know, has not been investigated yet. Our
main contributions are as follows:
• We introduce a new dataset: String Quartet with Reverbera-
tion Set (SQR Set) by collecting data for string quartets, com-
prising data synthesized using a Sound Library (for training
and validation) and individual instrument audio recordings
(for testing), and simulated real performance seating scenes
using real-world sampled B-format hall reverb with two kinds
of seating arrangements.
• We develop a source separation system that can separate ho-
mogeneous musical instruments by incorporating timbre and
positional cues into a modulation network, demonstrating its
effectiveness in handling complex acoustic environments.
• We train our proposed method only on one seating arrange-
ment and test it on another. The experimental results demon-
strate that the separation performance of our method remains
almost unaffected. In contrast, other state-of-the-art (SOTA)
methods used for comparison showed almost no applicability
in this scenario. This validates the effectiveness and general-
izability of our method.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2 reviews related work in music source separation. Section 3
presents our proposed method, detailing timbral and positional
information dynamic embedding. Section 4 describe the ex-
perimental setup and evaluate the performance of our method.
Section 5 concludes the paper.

2. Related Works
2.1. Music Source Separation
Music source separation aims to extract different sound sources
from a single audio mixture. Current methods for music source
separation can be broadly categorized into three approaches
based on how the model relates to the sources:
• Individual Model for Each Source: This approach trains a
separate model for each source, resulting in multiple models
corresponding to the number of sources. While this method
can be highly effective for specific sources, it is computation-
ally expensive and not scalable for many sources. Examples
include BS-RoFormer [18] and Band-Split RNN [4].
• Shared Model for All Sources:
A single model sepa-
rates all sources simultaneously, outputting multiple tracks
in one pass. This method is more scalable and efficient but
may struggle with distinguishing between sources when their
characteristics are highly similar. Demucs [1] and its subse-
quent improvements [19, 3] exemplify this approach.
• Shared Model with Conditional Control for Each Source:
In this approach, a single model is employed for all sources,
but it outputs different source tracks by applying specific
conditions. This method allows for flexibility and improved
separation quality by leveraging prior knowledge or context
about the sources. Examples include MSI [20] and LaSAFT
[21].
2.2. Chamber Ensemble Source Separation
Research on source separation for chamber music is relatively
limited.
Lin et al.
propose a conditional source separation
method using the timbre of the target instrument as a query
based on the URMP dataset [20, 22]. However, this method
cannot separate multiple instruments of the same type within
a recording and is limited to single-channel data. Sarker et al.
introduce EnsembleSet, a dual-channel dataset for chamber mu-
sic generated from MIDI files [23], which retains spatial place-
ment information of instruments. However, subsequent studies
merge this dual-channel data into a single channel, failing to
fully utilize spatial cues, leading to reduced separation perfor-
mance in overlapping pitch and similar timbre scenarios [24].
Additionally, both studies only address the separation of two
sound sources and do not explore more complex multi-source
scenarios.
3. Proposed Method
In Section 2.1, we outline three types of sound source separation
methods. The first approach, which involves training a separate
model for each instrument, costs many resources. The second
approach uses a shared model for all sources but lacks the ability
to effectively encode positional information and suffers from
performance degradation as the number of outputs increases.
In contrast, the third approach allows dynamic modulation of
the network by incorporating positional encoding as a control
condition, enabling the network to produce the corresponding
instrument audio for a specific target position. Therefore, we
adopt the third method for our network design in this study.
3.1. Main Architecture
As shown in Fig. 2, our network is based on a 7-blocked U-Net-
like architecture [25] designed for multi-instrument separation
using positional information. The input is a complex spectro-
gram with separate real and imaginary parts, preserving phase
information [26] to leverage both magnitude and phase cues for
accurate source separation.
The encoder begins with a 1 × 2 convolution block to ad-
just the input channel dimension, followed by ReLU for non-
linearity. It includes multiple down-sampling layers that reduce
the resolution of feature maps, 2D convolutional layers to cap-
ture local patterns, and batch normalization layers to stabilize
training. The decoder mirrors the encoder with up-sampling
layers to restore feature map resolution and skip connections to
retain spatial information across scales. The final 1×2 convolu-
tion block outputs the target spectrogram, preserving both real
and imaginary components. Down-sampling and up-sampling
layers handle resolution changes, while smaller convolutional
filters capture fine temporal details. For the convolution blocks,
we adapt TFC-TDF blocks inspired by [26], as is shown to be
able to extract time-frequency features. We set number of inter-
nal layers to 5, with kernel size 3 × 3 densely connected, and
the growth rate [27] is set to be 24.
3.2. Timbral and Positional Cue Embedding Module
The timbral and positional cue embedding module encodes the
timbral and spatial information of each instrument in the mix-
ture. As shown in the right part of Fig 2, we number the po-
sitional candidates and timbral candidates in the string quartets
respectively. The position number corresponds to the order in
the array [’pos1’, ’pos2’, ’pos3’, ’pos4’] (0, 1, 2 or 3), and the
timbre number corresponds to the order in [’V iolin’, ’V iola’,
’Cello’] (0, 1, 2). We pass the values of the positional and tim-
bral information numbers through an embedding generator to
generate a 32-dimensional embedding vector, and then multiply
the generated weights by 0.5 and add them together. Then the
result is used to modulate the weights of the Gated Point-wise
Convolutional Modulation(GPoCMs) introduced in section 3.3
at various levels of the decoder. This process conditions the
network to focus on the target source. The Condition Generator
produces a set of condition weights, β1, β2, . . . , βC, which are
applied to the decoder’s feature maps via GPoCM, dynamically
adjusting the network’s focus based on the positional cues of
the target source.
3.3. Gated Point-wise Convolutional Modulation
Inspired by [28], the Gated Point-wise Convolutional Modula-
tion (GPoCM) is introduced in our work, which extends the idea
of Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) by incorporating a
gating mechanism that enhances the flexibility and expressive-
ness of the modulation process [29]. GPoCM integrates gating
mechanisms with inter-channel operations to dynamically con-
trol the feature maps at different decoder layers, based on the
positional cues embedded by the Condition Generator.

4. Experiments
4.1. Dataset
Given that there is currently a lack of string quartet datasets
with clean stems and real hall reverberation, we introduce a
new dataset: String Quartet with Reverberation Set(SQR Set),
with each track is stereo, sampled at 44100 Hz, consisting of
the mixture and four sources: V iolin1, V iolin2, V iola and
Cello. This dataset simulates the spatial situation of two com-
mon string quartet seating arrangements in the presence of real
hall reverberation. Our dataset has been uploaded to zenodo:
https://zenodo.org/records/14793971.
Stems Collection:
It is difficult to obtain clean stem
ground truth for live recorded chamber music string quartets,
as bleeding from other instruments cannot be avoided when
recording simultaneously.
Thus we find string quartet parts
from EnsembleSet[23] (synthetic data) and URMP[22] (real
recorded data). EnsembleSet is a dataset for chamber music
rendered from MIDI files using a highly realistic orchestral
sample library by Spitfire Audio called ”BBC Symphony Or-
chestra”(BBCSO), having 18 unique multi-mic recordings and
2 professional mixes. Here we utilize its ”Mix 2” parts and take
the average value of two channels to generate the single chan-
nel data. URMP has mono recordings of isolated instruments in
anechoic conditions.
Seating Arrangement: The string quartet consists of two
violins, a viola, and a cello. When performing in string quar-
tets, musicians usually sit in a semi-circle so that all of the mu-
sicians can see each other. Being able to see each other helps
the musicians hear each other so they can better keep time and
know where everyone is. However, the order in which they sit
has changed over time[30]. Through the 19th century, the musi-
cians were organized in the order of first violin, cello, viola, and
second violin, as shown in Fig 1-(a). In contemporary times, the
most common seating arrangement for string quartets is shown
in Fig 1-(b).
Hall Reverberation Simulation: To simulate real hall
reverberation, we take these one-channeled audio and cre-
ate stereo versions in halls using convolution reverb by am-
bisonic b-format impulse response ”s1r2.wav” from the Openair
Database1.
Train, Validation and Test Set:
Our training set in-
cludes data generated only using 65 songs from the Ensemble-
set with seating arrangement type #2, having a total duration
of 17002.73 seconds. The validation set is made with the rest
6 songs from the Ensembleset with type #1 and #2 and dura-
tion is 1618.9 * 2 = 3237.8 seconds. The test set is formulated
using URMP with type #1 and #2 and duration is 781.233 * 2
= 1562.466 seconds. To ensure fairness to each track, we per-
formed RMS normalization on the volume of the four stems in
the same song during data synthesis.
4.2. Experimental Setup
For our model, we train it using Adam with learning rate
lr=0.0005 to minimize the mean squared error between the
ground-truth STFT output and the estimated with max epochs
of 1000. We don’t use any data augmentation methods. For
validation and test, we use SDR[31] as the evaluation metric
and evaluate the mean absolute error of the target signal and
the estimated. We use the median SDR(unit: dB) value over all
the test set tracks for each run and report the mean SDR over
three runs. For all models in our study, we use FFT window
size of 2048 and hop size of 1024 for spectrogram. For the con-
dition module, we tried different combinations of information
injection places. We find that we give timbral information to

4.3. Results
We perform an ablation study to validate the effectiveness of
our proposed method. The group ”dedicated” means U-Nets
for the single source separation, trained separately. The U-Net
structure here is the same as the U-Net setting in our proposed
method, but the ”Condition Module” and ”GPoCM” parts are
not added. The ”one-conditioned” group has a similar condi-
tional control model to our proposed method, except its condi-
tion vector is generated by one condition. Sepcifically, we en-
code the four tracks by the track name, that is, the four tracks are
numbered 0, 1, 2, and 3 respectively according to the order of
the names of the four tracks in the array [’V iolin1’, ’V iolin2’,
’V iola’, ’Cello’]. Results of our ablation study are shown in
Table 1. The ”validation” group means the results are evalu-
ated on the validation set and the ”test” group on the test set.
For each model, we evaluated them with the same seating ar-
rangement situation as training(type #2, no mark) and the dif-
ferent(type #1, marked with ”*” in the model column). All three
methods work well when the data distribution is the same as the
training dataset. For all methods, results on the validation set
are better than those on the test set. This is due to the difference
in data distribution between real recordings and synthesized au-
dio. By observing groups marked with ”*”, we find the per-
formance of ”V iolin2” and ”Cello” in the ”dedicated” model
trained independently for each instrument dropped significantly,
because the positions of these two instruments changed, while
the remaining ”V iolin1” and ”V iola” drop not that severe. The
”one-conditioned” model performance drops but is better than
the dedicated one, indicating that conditional encoding based
on the sub-tracks has a certain effect. Our method appears to be
more robust.
Also, we compare our proposed method with existing state-
of-the-art music source separation models2: BS-Roformer [6]
and HTDemucs [3] as mentioned in section 2.1. Each of the
contrast method has been trained for 1000 epoch and we con-
firm that has been fully converged by observing the change in
loss. For BS-Roformer, we train one model for each source with
batch size of 10 and learning rate of 0.00005. Results are shown
in Table 2. Similar to the results in Table 1, SOTA methods
achieve good results in the same scenario as training, but they
require three to four times the number of parameters of ours and
their performances degrade significantly after the data type and
acoustic scene change. Our method performs better when the
scene changes3.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we propose a method for source separation for
string quartets using timbral and positional cues in a condi-
tional modulation network which overcomes the limitations of
existing methods. We propose a new dataset of string quartet
recordings with realistic reverberation with two different seat-
ing arrangements. Experimental results demonstrate the effec-
tiveness of this method in separating individual instruments and
highlight the value of spatial cues in music source separation.
