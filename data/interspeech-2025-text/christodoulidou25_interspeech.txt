A semi-automatic pipeline for transcribing and segmenting child speech
Polychronia Christodoulidou1, James Tanner2, Jane Stuart-Smith2, Michael McAuliffe3, Mridhula
Murali4, Amy Smith4, Lauren Taylor4, Joanne Cleland4, Anja Kuschmann4
1Department of Theoretical & Applied Linguistics, Aristotle University of Thessaloniki, Greece

4Department of Psychological Sciences & Health, University of Strathclyde, UK

Abstract
This study evaluates both automated transcription (WhisperX)
and forced alignment (MFA) in developing a semi-automated
pipeline for obtaining acoustic vowel measures from field
recordings from 275 children speaking a non-standard, En-
glish dialect, Scottish English. As expected, manual correction
of speech transcriptions before forced alignment improves the
quality of acoustic vowel measures with respect to manually-
annotated data, though speech style and recording environment
present some challenges for both tools. Adaptation of the MFA
pre-trained english us arpa acoustic model towards the chil-
dren’s speech also improves the quality of acoustic measures,
though greater improvement was not found by increasing train-
ing sample size.
Index Terms: child speech, automated speech processing, non-
standard English, WhisperX, force alignment (MFA)
1. Introduction
Until recently our understanding of child speech production has
rested on results from relatively small speaker samples, not least
due to difficulties of collecting, curating and analyzing large
child speech corpora [1, 2, 3, 4].
Obtaining reliable acous-
tic phonetic measures from many child speech recordings also
encounters numerous challenges. Not only does child speech
show more acoustic and speech production variability than adult
speech [1, 5, 6], but preparing recordings for measurement, i.e.
transcribing and segmenting child speech, can be time consum-
ing [7], and is compounded by many factors including speech
style [2] and spoken language variety [3].
Automated speech processing is commonly used for the
acoustic phonetic analysis of large adult speech corpora [8], and
is now being extended to child speech [2, 3, 7]. Two kinds of au-
tomated processing, automated transcription and forced align-
ment, can reduce data processing time and increase efficiency
of working with large speech corpora, though are not without
issues: a fully-automated pipeline usually incurs the removal of
a large proportion of likely erroneous tokens [3, 8], and tools
may be less suitable for low resource populations [9, 10].
Automated transcription tools offer a way to speed up the
initial phase of transcribing child speech. Recent automated
language assessment tools developed to provide metrics from
spontaneous speech interactions often in noisy settings have
used cloud-based systems such as Amazon Transcribe, without
providing transcripts [11, 12]. Open-AI’s Whisper, and more
recently WhisperX [13], have the advantage of running locally,
which avoids ethical issues around sharing child speech, and are
now also being fine-tuned for child speech [14, 15]. To date au-
tomated transcription as a precursor for phonetically analysing
child speech has not been evaluated.
Forced alignment, i.e. the automated phone labelling and
segmentation of speech sounds [3], requires minimally an or-
thographic transcript of the speech recording, a pronunciation
dictionary specifying the phone labels for each word, and some-
times a pre-trained acoustic model specifying acoustic parame-
ters for the phone labels for the variety. Evaluation of forced
alignment has been extensively undertaken for adult speech;
to date four studies have systematically evaluated forced align-
ment for child speech (US English: [2, 7], Australian English:
[16], NZ English: [3]). Using metrics which assess alignment
quality through the degree of overlap between ‘gold standard’
manually-annotated boundaries and those inserted by aligners,
forced alignment of child speech has shown to vary by child
age, speech style, whether training data includes the child’s di-
alect, and sound type. To date the Montreal Forced Aligner
(MFA) [17] shows the best performance for US [2] and non-
standard NZ English [3]. Post-alignment manual correction of
segment boundaries is recommended before acoustic measure-
ment [2, 3, 16], though [7]’s results for sibilants based solely on
aligned boundaries are encouraging.
This paper aims to evaluate both automated transcription
(WhisperX [13]) and forced alignment (MFA [17]) in a semi-
automated pipeline used to prepare child speech recordings for
acoustic vowel analysis. The context is a reference dataset of
robust acoustic measures for clinicians and researchers, drawn
from field recordings from a cohort of 275 children, who speak a
non-standard English dialect. We consider the impact of two in-
terventions during data processing: 1. adjustment of automated
transcripts before alignment (Stage 1, Sec. 3), and 2. adaption
of acoustic models towards child speech (Stage 2, Sec. 4).
2. Method
The Variation in Child Speech (VariCS) [18] project examines
variability in children’s speech from a cross-sectional and lon-
gitudinal perspective. It aims to establish typical ranges for key
acoustic measures of respiration, phonation, resonance and ar-
ticulation, advancing understanding of developmental speech
patterns and their variability. Speech data is being collected
from 275 children in Scotland, UK, aged five to twelve years,
at four time points (‘Sweeps’) over 18 months [19]. In each
sweep, children complete a set of speech tasks, including story
retelling, picture description, sentence repetition, single-word
production, sound prolongation and rapid syllable repetition.
Data collection was conducted in the field, in 23 primary
schools across seven councils in Scotland. Recordings were
made using a bespoke iPad app that guided children through
the activities, and were saved as 32-bit mono WAV files with a
44.1KHz sampling frequency. To minimise background noise
and maintain a constant mouth-to-microphone distance, chil-

The data for this study uses the single-word production task
collected during Sweep 1. We aimed to collect productions of
20 familiar, easily-imaged words (teeth, feet, sheep, cake, fish,
bed, bat, cat, hat, cup, sock, coat, boat, soap, boot, car, ring,
hill, lip, sing) chosen to capture Scottish English vowels in a
CVC structure, plus certain consonants, and two nonwords to
consider nasalisation (meem, beeb). We used a picture-naming
task, repeated three times to investigate variability across rep-
etitions. We anticipated the task would elicit single words. In
fact, the speech is neither highly-controlled (though some chil-
dren only produced the depicted words without other speech or
prompts), nor spontaneous (though some children asked ques-
tions and/or responded to fieldworker prompts). This stylistic
variation, together with other child production features [5, 6],
including pitch and voice quality characteristics affecting res-
onance, as well as other noises, e.g. coughs, sniffs, laughter,
with noises from the field recording environment, and field-
worker prompts occurring in 37% recordings (287/783), adds
further challenges for automated processing. The speech style
thus falls between ‘controlled’ and ‘spontaneous’ speech [2, 3],
but is typical of eliciting child speech for acoustic analysis in a
speech research/therapeutic context for which greater efficiency
from automated processing is highly desirable [4].
After informal testing on 21 recordings from 7 children
from a single council area, we found the optimal transcription
tool to be WhisperX [13, 20], with the default model (small)
and language setting to ‘English’, previously trained on 438k
hours of adult English [15]. No improvement was found with
the large model setting [13], adding the target words using
--initial prompt did not improve output, and although
diarization helped for recordings with audible prompts, it was
not useful for non-prompted speech, and so was not adopted.
We aimed for a processing pipeline: WhisperX, then MFA,
then likely manual checking. After a trial of adjusting auto-
mated transcripts for 22 children, the Stage 1 evaluation (Sec. 3)
established that robust acoustic vowel measures needed adjust-
ment of all WhisperX output before alignment. Adjusting the
WhisperX output was carried out by four phonetically-trained
assistants, took ∼150 hours and entailed: removing/correcting
incorrect transcriptions including hallucinations; adjusting ut-
terance boundaries to reflect children’s speech (sometimes the
words were produced like a continuous list, at others, each word
was a separate utterance); and separating fieldworker prompts
from child speech into distinct tiers. Detailed annotation of non-
speech/‘speech-like’ noises could not be made at this scale.
An informal log of adjusting the WhisperX output for 783
recordings from 273 children for Sweep 1 (∼13 hours) showed
that WhisperX had transcribed all speech (child/fieldworker)
for 73% (568/783), though not always correctly and with some
instances of ‘hallucinations’ where text was made up.
The
log also noted the level of adjustment, and showed that: 70%
needed ‘a little’ or ‘no’ adjustment (a few words: 56%, none:
13%), 24% needed ‘some’ (several words), and a small propor-
tion required ‘a lot’ (5%) or completely retranscribing (1%).
The adjusted WhisperX .json output files were then con-
verted into Praat TextGrids using a bespoke Python script [21].
Earlier trialling had shown that utterance-level timestamps gave
better input for MFA than WhisperX word-level timestamps.
Following adjustment of most of the WhisperX dataset (654
recordings from 227 children), the Stage 2 evaluation (Sec. 4)
ascertained that an adapted version of the MFA english us arpa
acoustic model would substantially improve measurement ac-
curacy. Our choice of MFA for automatic segmentation was
informed by its performance for adult non-standard English
speech, as well as recent studies for child speech [2, 3]. We se-
lected english us arpa for pronunciation dictionary and acous-
tic model because Scottish English is nominally rhotic, and it
gave best results for another non-standard English dialect [3].
Before each alignment we validated the corpus and manually
expanded the pronunciation dictionary for the children’s pro-
ductions by adding phonemic transcriptions for Out of Vocabu-
lary items [3, 6].
The role of the two interventions was evaluated by inspect-
ing our intended acoustic measures structured by known pho-
netic/phonological patterning [9]. Note that the measures pre-
sented here, were either extracted from manual segmentation,
or directly extracted from force-aligned boundaries without fur-
ther manual intervention.
Extraction of acoustic measures and processing, for both
Stages, followed these steps. First, a Praat script was used to
extract the following features: 1. vowel duration, defined in the
manual analysis as the interval from start to end of the vowel’s
formant structure with normal voicing (excluding breathy or
creaky voicing), and 2. the average values of F1 and F2 frequen-
cies from the central 60% of the vowel. Automatically-inserted
boundaries were used to define the vowel for measures extracted
from MFA-aligned speech. The data were then cleaned and re-
fined in R [22]: for instance, fieldworker speech was removed,
any alternative forms of the target words, e.g. mean for meem,
were excluded if the target word was produced, and the final
occurrence of any consecutive repeated words was retained.
3. Stage 1: Adjusting WhisperX
The impact of adjusting WhisperX transcripts on quality of
forced alignment was examined using a sample of 64 recordings
(total duration: 3695s; mean: 58s; 1408 words/vowel phones)
from 22 children (aged 5 to 10, mean age: 6.5 years).
All
speakers except one produced three recordings, corresponding
to three repetitions of the target words. The sample was not
balanced in terms of gender (10 boys, 12 girls), age (8 five-
year-olds, 5 six-year-olds, 3 seven-year-olds, 2 eight-year-olds,
3 nine-year-olds, and 1 ten-year-old), or local dialect area (4
dialects were included). This diversity aimed to assess the per-
formance of forced alignment across the varied dataset.
WhisperX demonstrated a good level of accuracy in tran-
scribing this child speech, with occasional hallucinations. For
every target word except soap, WhisperX provided at least one
alternative form. But WhisperX was also disrupted by noise in-
terference (e.g. background and child noises, whispered field-
worker prompts) and by a particular hyperarticulation found in
the speech of many children, extra-long stop closures.
As expected, MFA could align both raw and adjusted Whis-
perX transcripts, and was particularly effective for cases with
minimal interference (e.g., without environmental or children’s
noises/soft prompts or very long stops). However, as for Whis-
perX – and perhaps unsurprisingly given the lack of annotation
of non-speech/speech-like noises – MFA could become derailed
by noise interference, also when aligning previously-adjusted
WhisperX transcripts. The extra-long stop closures also caused
problems for MFA when aligning both raw and adjusted Whis-
perX output; see example in Figure 1.
The performance of MFA was evaluated against manually-
segmented files under two conditions: 1. direct application of
MFA to unadjusted WhisperX output (raw), and 2. application
of MFA to adjusted WhisperX output (adjusted). Vowel dura-

also modelled vowel F1 and F2 with LME, with predictors for
method and vowel, and their interaction, with a random inter-
cept for speaker. We used model comparison via likelihood ra-

4. Stage 2: Adapting MFA acoustic models
Following adjustment of WhisperX output for 654 recordings
from 227 children, we next tried to improve the quality of MFA
alignment by shifting the english us arpa acoustic model to-
wards the children’s variety. A small ‘holdout’ dataset of 9
recordings was manually segmented (age: 5-9 years, mean:
7.2 years; gender: 2 boys/7 girls; total duration: 418s, mean:
46s; 197 words/vowel phones). mfa adapt [26] was used to
make three new acoustic models: 1.
from the 64 record-
ings/22 speakers analysed in Stage 1 (adapt22); 2.
from
654 recordings/218 speakers, with default --mapping-tau
= 20 (adapt218); 3.
from 654 recordings/218 speakers,
with --mapping-tau = 10, giving greater weight to the in-
put speech (adapt218 tau10).1 Here, we compare formant
measures from manual segmentation against those from MFA
alignment from each acoustic model, with the expectation that
model adaptation would improve with more speakers.
Given the small number of recordings, we evaluated differ-
ences between the acoustic models by comparing the Euclidean
distance of formant measures from english us arpa and each
new adapted model, against those from manual segmentation.
The greater the Euclidean distance, the further the vowel token
derived from the acoustic model is from the manual reference
within the F1×F2 vowel space.
To ascertain the influence of model adaptation, we explored
the degree of error in formant values between acoustic models
by comparing the Euclidean distance, relative to manual seg-
mentation (N = 788). Figure 3 (left) illustrates the distribution
of Euclidean distances by acoustic model. To get some sense
of how accurate each acoustic model was in approximating the
manually-annotated formants, we modelled the relative size of
the Euclidean distance, where distance values ≤50 were se-
lected as sufficiently similar to the manually-annotated values,
based on the visual exploration of the distributions and vowel
spaces. This measure (≤50 vs >50) was modelled as a logis-
tic regression using lme4 [27] as a function of acoustic model
({arpa, adapt22, adapt218, adapt218 tau10}), and
log-scaled duration, and random by-speaker and by-vowel in-
tercepts. We report the estimated marginal difference ( ˆ∆) be-
tween acoustic models using emmeans [24]. We find that the
probability of a similar distance value is significantly different
across acoustic models (χ2(3) = 24.92, p < 0.0001). Specifi-
cally, we find that the arpa model performs significantly worse
than the other adapted acoustic models ( ˆ∆adapt22 = −1.03,
z = −3.6, p < 0.002; ˆ∆adapt218 = −1.2, z = −4.16, p <
0.0002; ˆ∆adapt218 tau10 = −1.21, z = −4.15, p < 0.0005),
though there are no significant differences between the other
acoustic models (Figure 3, right).
5. Discussion
This study reports an evaluation of two key aspects of using au-
tomated speech processing to improve the efficiency of prepar-
ing 275 children’s field recordings for acoustic analysis [4].
Specifically, we considered the impact of 1. adjusting auto-
mated WhisperX transcripts before forced alignment with MFA
(Sec. 3), and 2. adapting a pre-trained MFA acoustic model
towards the children’s Scottish English dialect (Sec. 4).
WhisperX showed good performance, providing transcripts
for 70% of the recordings, and little to no adjustment needed
for 73% [20]. As expected, adjustment of WhisperX output be-

though with greater variability and more centralized qualities

We also found that both WhisperX and MFA were disrupted
by features from the field recording and elicitation task, namely
additional noises, and consistent hyperarticulation in this child
speech. These findings indicate that this speech style, which is
often single words, but may include spontaneous speech too,
from field recordings from children speaking a non-standard
dialect, does indeed pose challenges for automated processing
tools. And this is not surprising: not only are WhisperX’s En-
glish small model and MFA’s english us arpa acoustic model
trained on adult speech, but the style of training data for both
tools is overwhelmingly fluent connected speech, mainly but not
entirely spontaneous for Whisper [28], and read speech (Lib-
riSpeech English) for MFA. It is unclear whether [20]’s Flexible
and Automatic Speech Aligner, adapted for noisy child speech
data, might perform better for our data, given that they evalu-
ate performance via Word Error Rate, rather than the quality of
extracted acoustic measures.
At the same time, we were able to improve MFA’s seg-
mentation by adjusting the pretrained acoustic model (en-
glish us arpa) towards the children’s non-standard dialect. The
quality of the vowel formant measures was enhanced with re-
spect to manual segmentation [7, 16], and this was achieved
with only 22 children in the training data. Of note, the substan-
tial increase to a 218-child training dataset did not give further
improvement. This likely is due to all speakers largely saying
the same words. More linguistically-varied datasets might need
more data to achieve similar performance.
Obtaining robust reference vowel measures from the Sweep
1 dataset required a final phase of manual checking of MFA out-
put [2, 3], which is now completed (a further ∼150 hours). We
note first that the entire manual input is a fraction of estimated
time for manual segmentation of speech corpora (up to ∼800
hours/hour of speech [3, p.183]). Second, the vowel formant
measures direct from forced alignment of raw WhisperX output
are promising (Figure 2). This suggests that future child speech
formant measures could be drawn from an automated approach,
as commonly done for adult large-scale speech analysis [8, 2],
i.e. by pruning erroneous data against reliable baseline mea-
sures obtained by the semi-automatic pipeline developed for
this large child speech corpus.
