GTA: Towards Generative Text-To-Audio Retrieval via Multi-Scale Tokenizer
Minghui Fang1, Shengpeng Ji1, Jialong Zuo1, Xize Cheng1, Wenrui Liu1, Xiaoda Yang1, Ruofan Hu1,

Abstract
Text-to-audio retrieval is a fundamental task in acoustic signal
processing. Currently, mainstream approaches primarily em-
ploy a dual-tower architecture, independently encoding text and
audio while performing similarity score matching. However,
these methods struggle to maintain a uniform embedding space
and the latency of score matching increases with corpus size. In
this light, we propose GTA to move towards a generative text-
to-audio retrieval paradigm. Specifically, we utilize a multi-
scale audio tokenizer to embed audio semantics into identifiers,
while incorporating a dual-alignment strategy to ensure consis-
tency between audio and text semantics. Furthermore, we im-
plement curriculum learning to bridge the gap between train-
ing and inference, guiding the model toward precise token-level
generation. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effective-
ness and robustness of GTA, further validating the feasibility of
the generative text-to-audio retrieval paradigm.
Index Terms: text-to-audio retrieval, generative retrieval
1. Introduction
Text-to-audio retrieval is a fundamental task in acoustic signal
processing, enabling users to intuitively retrieve corresponding
audio using natural language descriptions.
Current classical
methods [1, 2, 3, 4] treat this task as a discriminative learn-
ing problem, employing a dual-tower framework to project text
queries and candidate audio into a unified high-dimensional
embedding space, where audio is ranked based on similarity
scores. However, these methods struggle to effectively align
data from different modalities [5] and become inefficient when
dealing with large-scale corpora. Specifically, due to the signifi-
cant disparity in semantic representations across modalities, the
dual-tower framework typically requires large-scale pretraining
to bridge this gap. Additionally, as the number of candidate au-
dios increases, the similarity computation and ranking process
introduces higher latency [6].
As an emerging paradigm in document retrieval, generative
retrieval [7, 8, 9, 10] introduces a new perspective and possibili-
ties for cross-modal retrieval. As shown in Figure 1 (b), the core
idea is to assign a semantic identifier to each candidate target,
and then use a language model to replace the traditional retrieval
process with the task of generating these identifiers. Compared
to traditional retrieval frameworks, generative retrieval searches
for the best candidate targets based solely on the user’s query,
eliminating the need for a unified embedding space and explicit
similarity matching. Given the impressive performance of lan-
guage models [11, 12, 13], it is worthwhile to explore how to
effectively integrate them with text-to-audio retrieval. Firstly,

In this paper, we introduce GTA, which leverages dis-

Generative Text-to-Audio retrieval. To address the aforemen-
tioned challenges, GTA is designed with three key components
to support end-to-end text-to-audio retrieval. Specifically, we
propose to employ a multi-scale residual quantizer as an audio
tokenizer to extract semantic information from audio embed-
dings, taking the closest codeword in each codebook as the iden-
tifier. To facilitate autoregressive modeling and match the hier-
archical refinement nature of the residuals, the size of the deep
codebook is larger than that of the shallow codebook. Mean-
while, to align with the high-level semantics of the text, we
design a dual alignment strategy to bring the paired identifiers
closer to the text. Finally, we employ curriculum learning to
enhance the generative process of the language model. By ran-
domly merging ground truth tokens with predicted tokens, we
reduce the gap between training and inference.

2. Method
2.1. Overview
GTA is an end-to-end text-to-audio retrieval framework, which
takes natural language queries as input and generates corre-
sponding audio identifiers. This pipeline eliminates the need for
auditory input, requiring only the generation training of {Query,
Identifier} pairs.
The whole framework is divided into two
modules: 1) Multi-scale audio tokenizer, which is used to con-
struct semantic identifiers for audio, and 2) Curriculum-learning
based generative training, which utilizes a language model to
convert discriminative retrieval to generative retrieval.
2.2. Multi-Scale Audio Tokenizer
The current popular approach [17, 18, 19, 20, 21] for unstruc-
tured audio data is to discretize it, making it compatible with
autoregressive generation models. Essentially, these methods
are based on vector quantization [22, 23], embedding the se-
mantic information of audio into codebooks and then selecting
the most matching codeword from multiple codebooks. How-
ever, they tend to compress at the ratio of hundreds of tokens
per second and retain too much acoustic information, which fo-
cuses on audio reconstruction and high fidelity. As previously
mentioned, identifiers are not interchangeable for the retrieval
task, restricting the autoregressive model to accurately generate
hundreds of tokens is extremely difficult. Meanwhile, the re-
trieval focuses on semantic relevance to the query rather than
acoustic features. These drive us to design an audio tokenizer
tailored specifically for retrieval tasks.

2.3. Curriculum Learning-Based Generative Retrieval
With the help of the audio tokenizer, the paired {Query, Audio}
is converted into {Query, Identifier}, at which point the retrieval
task can be viewed as end-to-end text generation. Since tokens
come from different codebooks, the same number in different
positions carries different semantics. For example, 31 and 32
in (31, 2, 32, 1) represent different meanings. Considering this,
we add special prefixes to each position to distinguish them, e.g.
(a 3, b 2, c 3, d 1), and add them to the language model vo-
cabulary. Based on the encoder-decoder architecture, the cross-
entropy loss for generating semantic identifiers T given a query

3. Experiments
3.1. Datasets and Baselines
We evaluate GTA on two widely used datasets: Audiocaps [29]
and Clotho [30]. Audiocaps consist of 39,597 audio samples
where each audio sample in the validation and test sets has
five captions, making a total of 45,513 {Query, Audio} pairs.
Clotho consists of 4981 audio samples with five captions per
audio sample, for a total of 24,905 {Query, Audio} pairs.
We select baselines based on the classical dual-tower ar-
chitecture. The CLAP series [3, 31, 4] explores different com-
binations of text encoders and audio encoders, presenting an
efficient way to handle variable-length audio. While VALOR
[32], ONE-PIECE [33] aim to build omni-modality fundamen-
tal models with larger number of parameters and pre-training
scale, which consider text-to-audio retrieval as a subtask. Con-
sistent with previous work [2, 3], we use the standard retrieval
metric R@K where K is set to 1, 5, 10.
3.2. Implementation Details
Following the previous work [8, 10, 34], we generate five addi-
tional captions for each audio as Query for data enhancement.

The audio tokenizer is trained with learning rate of 0.00001 and
batch size of 512. The encoder consists of 4 intermediate layers
with sizes of 768, 512, 256, and 128, respectively, and the de-
coder is completely symmetrical to it. The quantizer includes
four codebooks with sizes of 128, 256, 512 and 1024, each
codebook vector has a dimension of 64. Following [35] sugges-
tion, we are initializing the codebook centroid using K-Means
to ensure training stability. The generative model utilizes T5-
base [11] as the backbone and has an initial learning rate of 1e-

with a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 128. The prob-
ability P of curriculum learning has an initial value of 1 and
decreases by 0.005 per epoch.
3.3. Main Results
In Table 1, we report the the performance of the baseline and
GTA. We observe that all methods perform better on AudioCaps
than on Clotho. Compared to the baseline, GTA performs com-
petitively on AudioCaps and outperforms the baseline across
the board on Clotho. Our analysis indicates that the audio du-
ration in the Clotho ranges from 15 to 30 seconds, whereas the
audio in AudioCaps are generally around 10 seconds. Enhanc-
ing the retrieval performance for long and variable-length audio
sequences is exactly the challenge [3] that the dual-tower model
aims to address. In contrast, the audio semantics are already em-
bedded in the parameters of the language model during training.
GTA only needs to focus on processing text queries and predict-
ing fixed-length audio identifiers, which alleviates the retrieval
burden to some extent.
3.4. Ablation Results
We employ multi-scale codebooks in the audio tokenizer to
match the progressively refined nature of residual transmission.
Additionally, we use dual-alignment to ensure that the identi-
fiers not only encapsulate audio semantics but also align with
the text. During the training stage, we enhance the language
model’s prediction of identifiers through curriculum learning.
To validate the effectiveness of these key components, we re-
port the results of the ablation study in Table 2.
We set the size of all codebooks to 128 to simulate the w/o
multi-scale setting, and the retrieval performance showed sig-
nificant degradation. Intuitively, we believe that as the resid-
uals are passed from level to level, deeper codebook captures
finer semantic granularity. With increasing depth of the code-
book, extending the size of the codebook facilitates improved
quantization accuracy.
Removing dual-alignment during the
audio tokenizer training also heavily impacted retrieval per-
formance, highlighting the importance of aligning identifiers
with query semantics. Finally, removing the curriculum learn-

3.5. Comparison of Inference Latency
We compare the retrieval efficiency of CLAP [1] and GTA on
both CPU and NVIDIA V100 GPU to evaluate the performance
of dual-tower and generative frameworks. We simulate 50 con-
current queries and change the number of candidate audios, the
metric is throughput (queries/per second) and the detailed re-
sults are shown in Figure 3. We pre-encode the audio into em-
beddings, so the primary time cost of CLAP comes from text
encoding, similarity matching, and ranking. With the increase
of audio, the retrieval throughput of the dual-tower framework
gradually decreases. In contrast, GTA generates identifiers di-
rectly based on the query, with efficiency independent of the
number of candidate audios, which is an advantage of directly
encoding all audios as its parameters.

3.6. Deep Analysis on Audio Tokenzier
3.6.1. Analysis through visualisation.
We further investigate the effectiveness of the audio tokenizer.
As shown in Figure 4, we visualize the correlation between the
audio, the corresponding query, and the identifiers.
We use
t-SNE to visualize the distribution of audio embeddings with
the same prefix, showing clear clustering patterns. Embeddings
within clusters are more closely grouped, while those between
clusters are more dispersed. Additionally, we conducted a more
detailed case study. It can be observed that the closer the iden-
tifiers are, the more similar the semantics of the corresponding
audio queries. This demonstrates that the identifiers extracted
by the audio tokenizer not only capture the semantics of the au-
dio but also align with the semantics of the query.
3.6.2. Analysis on token size.
We verify the effectiveness of multi-scale by adjusting the
size of the codebooks, with the experimental results on Clotho
shown in Table 3. We observe that both excessively large and
small codebook sizes degrade retrieval performance, as the size
of the codebook controls the granularity of classification, thus
limiting the expressive power of the semantic identifiers. Ad-
ditionally, an overly large decoding space reduces the accuracy
of autoregressive model predictions. In contrast, the multi-scale
approach effectively alleviates these issues.
3.6.3. Analysis on token length.
We adjust the length of the identifiers by varying the number of
codebooks, with the experimental results on Clotho shown in
Table 4. We observe only slight fluctuations in retrieval perfor-
mance, indicating the effectiveness of the semantic identifiers
and the stability of the retrieval model. Although increasing the
identifier length helps distinguish different candidates at a finer
granularity, excessively long identifiers reduce codebook uti-
lization and increase decoding steps. Therefore, we ultimately
choose to use four tokens.
4. conclusion
In this paper, we introduce GTA, an innovative end-to-end text-
to-audio retrieval framework. By combining a multi-scale audio
tokenizer with a dual alignment strategy, GTA effectively con-
structs audio identifiers that are consistent with text semantics.
With the power of generative models and curriculum learning,
GTA demonstrates outstanding retrieval performance and effi-
ciency across two benchmark datasets. Ablation studies and
in-depth analysis also confirm the robustness of this paradigm.
