First Analyze Then Enhance: A Task-Aware System for
Speech Separation, Denoising, and Dereverberation
Shaoxiang Dang1,2, Li Li2, Shogo Seki2, Hiroaki Kudo1
1Graduate School of Informatics, Nagoya University, Japan
2AI Lab, CyberAgent, Inc., Japan
{dang shaoxiang xa, li li, seki shogo}@cyberagent.co.jp
Abstract
This paper presents theFirst Analyze Then Enhance (FATE)
framework for speech enhancement. In FATE, observed sig-
nals are initially classified based on their specific degrada-
tion types and then enhanced using appropriate modules tai-
lored to each type. This design prevents the overprocessing
of clean signals and reduces process complexity by eliminat-
ing unnecessary procedures. This paper focuses on commonly
encountered degradations, including additive noise, reverbera-
tion, speech mixing, and their combinations. To address these
degradations, FATE includes a separation submodule and a de-
noising/dereverberation submodule the enhancement models.
Degradation types are predicted using features extracted from
pretrained models based on automatic speech recognition and
self-supervised learning. Experiments show that FATE can ac-
curately identify undegraded signals and achieve comparable
enhancement performance for degraded signals in each scenario
while optimizing processing complexity.
Index Terms: Speech enhancement, First Analyze Then En-
hance, Single-channel, Speech separation
1. Introduction
Speech degradation, manifesting as audio signal distortions, is
a common challenge in real-world applications. These degra-
dations encompass but are not limited to, noisy speech (back-
ground noise) [1], reverberant speech (echoes from reflections)
[2], and mixed speech (overlapping voices) [3]. Tradition-
ally, these degradations have been addressed independently us-
ing specialized methodologies tailored to their distinct objec-
tives. For example, speech separation is particularly challeng-
ing because individual speaker information must be conserved,
which introduces a permutation problem and influences both the
model structure and training objectives.
Recently, powerful models [4, 5, 6, 7] have blurred the
boundaries between tasks by simultaneously purifying and sep-
arating signals. This trend encourages tackling more compre-
hensive tasks using unified models, as exemplified by the UR-
GENT Challenge [8]. Employing a single model enables to
use of diverse datasets, leading to broader knowledge acquistion
and more powerful models. However, this approach increases
training difficulty and demands greater computational resources
(e.g., more data and larger networks) to achieve sufficient gener-
alization. Furthermore, the internal structure of unified models
is often opaque, making it difficult to apply task-specific losses
to enhance performance on individual tasks. Therefore, it is cru-
cial to carefully select the tasks to merge, ensuring they can be
trained using shared loss function. This consideration has led
to the adoption of multistage approaches [9, 10, 11], in which
different types of degradation are addressed using specialized

This paper proposes a framework capable of handling mul-
tiple degradations in a multistage manner. Unlike conventional
approaches, which pass observed signals through all submod-
ules, we argue that the type of data degradation should be iden-
tified before processing. Each degradation is then directed to a
corresponding tailored module based on the classification. We
refer to our method asFirst Analyze Then Enhance (FATE). This
design allows undegraded signals to pass through the system
while reducing processing complexity by skipping unnecessary
submodules. We first evaluate the framework on commonly en-
countered tasks, including denoising, dereverberation, and sep-
aration. Notably, the framework is flexible and can be extended
to other enhancement tasks, such as declipping and inpainting.
In a related study, a similar framework was proposed that com-
bined an analyzer and a multistage processor to remove audio
effects [12]. The key differences lie in the target signal and
main motivation, as we focus on speech signals and aim to re-
duce the overprocessing of undegraded inputs. Unlike audio
effects, speech signals present challenges in distinguishing be-
tween several types of degradation due to their similar charac-
teristics, such as reverberation and multi-speaker scenarios.
As shown in Fig. 1, FATE comprises two main modules: an
analyzer and an enhancer. The analyzer must be highly accu-
rate, as misclassification can lead to incorrect routing and di-
rectly affect performance. We utilize models pretrained based
on automatic speech recognition and self-supervised learning
[13] as the backbone of the analyzer due to their powerful
feature extraction capabilities. Observed signals are catego-
rized into four groups, based on whether they require separa-
tion or refinement (i.e., denoising/dereverberation). Therefore,
the enhancer includes a separator and a refiner. We incorpo-
rate an encoder-decoder-attractor (EDA) mechanism to estimate
the number of speakers [14], addressing the challenge of an un-
known speaker count. In addition, the enhancer is designed with
a separation-priority pipeline (SPP) [10] and is trained in multi-
ple phases to ensure that it enhances the observed signals using
only the necessary submodules with sufficient performance.

2. FATE
2.1. Research scope
FATE aims to address a broad range of speech degradations us-
ing an assembled system in which submodules can be flexibly
added, removed, or combined for different applications while
maintaining a low fine-tuning cost. In this study, we explore
three fundamental combinations: speech separation, denoising,
and dereverberation. This results in four data categories, each
with an adaptive processing objective, summarized as follows:
(C1) clean source: passes through; (C2) clean mixture: sep-
aration only; (C3) noisy/reverberant source: refinement only;
(C4) noisy/reverberant mixture: separation and refinement.
2.2. Framework details
In FATE, the observed signals are first classified into predefined
categories by the analyzer module and then processed according
to routing based on the predicted category.
The degradation-type analyzer is built on pretrained mod-
els, leveraging their rich feature representations learned from
large-scale data. We adopt Whisper [15] and WavLM [16] be-
cause of their robustness to noise and their ability to capture
both acoustic and contextual information. Acoustic features are
expected to help distinguish noisy and clean signals, whereas
contextual features are expected to aid in differentiating re-
verberant and mixed signals. Following SUPERB’s principle
[17] that downstream models should remain simple, we adopt a
lightweight architecture comprising two long short-term mem-
ory (LSTM) layers, a time-dimensional average pooling layer,
and a fully connected (FC) layer. As illustrated on the left-hand
side of Fig. 2, the model aggregates features across different
transformer encoder layers and uses them to classify the ob-
served signals into four categories.
The enhancer consists of a separator and a refiner. The
separator addresses mixed signals, whereas the refiner handles
noise and reverberation. The image on the right side of Fig. 2 il-
lustrates the route of a signalX∈ RL that requires both separa-
tion and refinement, with architectures based on a dual-path re-
current neural network (DPRNN) [18], whereL denotes the sig-
nal length in the time domain. The DPRNN forms a latent rep-
resentation of an input signalRT×F through a 1D convolutional
layer followed by an activation function (e.g., ReLU), whereT
and F denote the frame and feature dimensions, respectively.
The input feature is fed into the DPRNN blocks, where intra-
and inter-chunk operations are performed iteratively and alter-
nately. Separation masks for individual speakers are then esti-
mated using a WaveNet-like gating mechanism equipped with
convolutional layers [19]. The separated signals are obtained by
masking the input features and performing overlap-add in the
1D deconvolution. The separator and refiner submodules op-
erate within a cascaded SPP [10] structure. The separated sig-
nals are independently processed by the refiner by folding the
source dimension into the batch dimension. This design effec-
tively manages complex tasks while maintaining compatibility
with simpler subtask processing.
To handle an arbitrary number of speakers in mixed sig-
nals, the separator is integrated with an LSTM-based encoder-
decoder attractor (EDA) [14]. The EDA module estimates the
number of speakers ˆI in the input and produces the correspond-
ing speaker representations for subsequent separation. During
training, given an I-speaker mixture, the EDA module recur-
rently generates ˆI + 1 speaker attractors and their existence
probabilities ˆpi. Generation stops when the probability falls be-
low a threshold, and the first ˆI attractors are used.
2.3. Training strategies of FATE
We train the model in multiple stages using the following strate-
gies to ensure that each module performs well, both individually
and in combination. Noise-Adaptation Training (NAT): The
EDA may struggle to accurately predict the number of speakers
in noisy and reverberant conditions. To fully leverage its poten-
tial, EDA-equipped models are pre-trained on noise-free mix-
tures and then fine-tuned on noisy-reverberant signals contain-
ing single and multiple speakers [5]. This approach is referred
to as noise-adaptation training (NAT). Divide-and-Integrate
Training (DIT): The enhancer is trained using the divide-and-
integrate (DIT) method. Specifically, the separator is trained
on data requiring separation, and the refiner is trained on data
requiring refinement. Subsequently, the enhancer is assembled
and trained on a full degraded dataset. The first stage ensures
the performance of each submodule when operating individ-
ually. The subsequent stage improves performance when the
modules are combined by mitigating the impact of information
loss from preceding submodules.NAT&DIT:We adopt a three-
stage training process to train FATE with a separator incorpo-
rating the EDA mechanism. In the first two stages, the separator
is trained using NAT, while the refiner is trained conventionally.
In the third stage, both submodules are fine-tuned together.

3.2. Model parameters and training details
We used WavLM-base1, WavLM-large2, and Whisper 3, which
have 12, 24, and 12 transformer encoder layers respectively, as
the backbones of the analyzer [15, 16]. The two LSTM layers
had output dimensions of 64, and were mapped to four neurons
via an FC layer. We fine-tuned the analyzer with a batch size
of 24, ensuring that each category contained six samples. The
Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 0.0001,
which remained constant for the first 20 epochs. Afterward, the
learning rate was annealed by a factor of 0.98 every two epochs.
The model was trained for a total of 150 epochs.
Each separator and refiner contained 6 DPRNN blocks. For
comparison, we used DPRNN with 6 and 12 blocks as base-
lines. The DPRNN block took input features generated by a
1D convolution with a window length of 16, hop size of 8, and
64 channels. During internal local and global processing, the
data chunk length and hop size were set to 250 and 125, respec-
tively. The number of output channels for separation models
without EDA was set to 3, corresponding to the largest number
of speakers in the experiment. The batch size was 16. The initial
learning rates for pre-training and fine-tuning were 0.001 and
0.0001, respectively. The learning rate was halved if the model
failed to improve validation performance for five consecutive
epochs. Training continued for a total of 150 epochs, with early
stopping triggered after 30 epochs without improvement. We
investigated the training strategies described in Subsection 2.3
as well as end-to-end (E2E) training, where the entire dataset
was used in a single-stage training. The subdatasets used for

4. Results and discussions
4.1. Overall quantitative results
In addition to SI-SNR [20], we employed short-time objective
intelligibility (STOI) [26] and perceptual evaluation of speech
quality (PESQ) [27] as evaluation metrics. For the EDA-
equipped models, we followed the procedure described in the
Subsection 3.3 of [14] to address the mismatch between the es-
timated number of speakers and the groud truth. For models
without EDA, we used the combination that achieved the max-
imum SI-SNR relative to the ground truth. The overall quan-
titative results are presented in Table 3. Here, we report the
results of FATE using a WavLM-large-based analyzer owing to
its superior performance. Both FATE and FATE EDA demon-
strated superior performance compared to the baseline models.
Specifically, FATE achieved an improvement of 1.25 dB, and
FATE EDA achieved an improvement of 0.83 dB over DPRNN
and DPRNN EDA, respectively, when using the same number
of blocks. Given that the upper bound of FATE was even higher,
these results suggest that task-specific modules have the poten-
tial to outperform unified models. Notably, the model size re-
mains nearly unchanged, further underscoring the advantages
of FATE. Comparing models trained with and without NAT, we
found that NAT contributed to improvements of 0.89 dB in SI-
SNR for DPRNN with 12 blocks and 0.35 dB for FATE, indi-
cating the effectiveness of NAT in enhancing performance.
4.2. Data-type-specific performance analysis
Table 4 presents data-type-specific results for the best system
in each group. All models evaluated consisted of 12 DPRNN
blocks. First, we compared the results for clean single-source
signals. FATE successfully processed all undegraded signals by
passing them through the system, whereas the unified models
resulted in severe distortions. When comparing DPRNN and
FATE, FATE showed a greater advantage in handling complex
tasks, particularly data from ‘C4’. FATE achieved an SI-SNR
improvement of over 1.5 dB in ‘C4’, compared to improve-
ments of 0.8 dB in ‘C2’ and ‘C3’. One important reason for
this is the SPP design, which provides a dedicated route for
processing complex data in FATE, maximizing the benefits of

When integrating EDA into FATE, the advantages of FATE,

4.3. Performance of analyzer
Fig. 3 shows the confusion matrices of the analyzers with differ-
ent pretrained models. The clean single-source and clean mix-
ture are two classes that can be easily classified, with WavLM-
based models achieving 100% accuracy and the Whisper-based
model achieving 99% accuracy. All models struggled to distin-
guish between degraded single-source and degraded mixtures
due to noise and reverberation. On average, WavLM-large
achieved the highest accuracy and best performance and was
therefore used to guide FATE. Future work will include reduc-
ing the analyzer’s model size using techniques such as knowl-
edge distillation to achieve a more efficient system.
5. Summary
Instead of integrating speech separation, denoising, and dere-
verberation into a unified model, this paper has proposed the
FATE framework, which first analyzes the type of data degra-
dation and then enhances it according to the identified needs.
By carefully designing the submodules in sequence follow-
ing the SPP, and employing training strategies such as NAT
and DIT, FATE outperformed unified models in the aforemen-
tioned tasks, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining task-
specific models.
