Speech transcription from South Tyrolean Dialect to Standard German with
Whisper
Luca Ducceschi∗, Greta H. Franzini∗
Institute for Applied Linguistics, Eurac Research, Italy
luca.ducceschi@eurac.edu, greta.franzini@eurac.edu
Abstract
This study presents the first fine-tuned Whisper model for
the automatic translation of South Tyrolean dialectal speech
into Standard German text. To address an unmet need for sub-
titling and translation, we introduce a small corpus of man-
ually annotated and synthetic speech data compiled for this
task. Through fine-tuning and hyperparameter optimisation, our
model achieves a BLEU score of 86.18 significantly outper-
forming baseline error rates. Our findings highlight Whisper’s
effectiveness in handling dialectal speech, contributing to low-
resource language research. The model is already being used
in a heritage collaboration for large-scale translation of audio-
visual archival material and is also being considered for appli-
cation in news broadcasting and tourism promotion. Future di-
rections include expanding the training data and extending hy-
perparameter optimisation to improve the model’s performance
and generalisation across South Tyrolean dialectal variations.
Index Terms: speech-to-text, translation, large language mod-
els, Whisper, low-resource languages, dialects, German
1. Introduction
The South Tyrolean dialect (hereafter South Tyrolean), spoken
in Italy’s northernmost province of South Tyrol (S¨udtirol), is a
protected Austro-Bavarian variety blending Germanic and Ro-
mance linguistic elements (Italian and Ladin) [1]. Unlike South
Tyrolean German, the region’s standard German variety, South
Tyrolean is primarily oral and lacks a standardised orthography.
Widely used informally across generations, South Tyrolean is
favoured for casual communication like text messages and so-
cial media, while formal contexts rely on the standard variety.1
Although unique, South Tyrol’s sociolinguistic situation paral-
lels many European regions where regional dialects dominate
informal oral communication within diaglossic or diglossic set-
tings [3].
Limited subtitling of dialectal speech in formal media con-
texts restricts access for individuals with hearing impairments
or those unfamiliar with South Tyrolean, such as visitors or
non-native residents. This study responds to an explicit demand
from local community beneficiaries for an automated solution
to transcribe and subtitle South Tyrolean speech into Standard
German, introducing the first Whisper fine-tuned model for this
purpose.

2. Related work
In recent years, strides have been made in the field of NLP
for Germanic dialects, enhancing the accessibility and usabil-
ity of these linguistic varieties in digital formats [4, 5, 6, 7, 8].
With regard to spoken data, [9] lists 39 audio datasets covering
low and high German dialects. The AnDy project uses AI and
the REDE corpus[10] to automatically analyse the dynamics of
German dialectal speech, while a 343-hour corpus of read Swiss
German speech annotated with Standard German text has been
used to fine-tune an XLS-R translation model [11]. Swiss Ger-
man and Luxembourgish are the only Germanic dialects to have
been processed for translation into Standard German. Swiss
German studies include hybrid Hidden Markov Models [12],
Time Delay Neural Networks with Kaldi [13] and pronunci-
ation mapping to Standard German [14], as well as transfer
learning with Jasper and DeepSpeech [15, 16] and on other
well-performing hybrid systems [17]. Luxembourgish research
fine-tuned Whisper and wav2vec 2.0 on 67 hours of annotated
speech, achieving better results with Whisper [18].
Building on the speaker-centric NLP perspective advocated
in recent discussions on low-resource language colonising dis-
course [19], a survey of 327 speakers of German varieties found
strong support for systems transcribing dialect to Standard Ger-
man (61%) [6]. While this result may not be fully representative
of the broader population, they highlight interest in advancing
dialect NLP and spoken language processing technologies.
3. Method

As is often the case with under-resourced languages, no ad-
equate corpora exist yet for the development of a Speech-to-
Text/Translation system for South Tyrolean2. For this reason,
we are creating a corpus of labelled South Tyrolean-to-Standard
German data by combining different publicly available sources,
both openly licensed and proprietary, with our own speech
recordings. Publicly available sources span spontaneous and
scripted speech, include both proprietary and openly licensed
language datasets, and capture a wide range of dialectal diver-
sity. The dataset currently comprises 412 minutes (6 hours and
52 minutes) of speech drawn from four distinct spoken sources
(ARB, ALP, EUR, IDM), down-sampled to 16kHz, totalling approx-

3.1.2. SYNTH
The synthetic dataset is derived from the first 119 episodes of
the Budl&Stubn podcast8. The episodes were downloaded as
MP3 files from SoundCloud using the Python soundcloud-lib
library9. The dataset totals 7, 127.35 minutes (118 hours and 47
minutes) of speech. The translated transcription was performed
using the Whisper large-v3 model, with diarisation enabled
through the Python pyannote library. After filtering out sen-
tences shorter than 2 seconds, the final synthetic training dataset
comprised 6, 729 chunks.
3.2. Model
We chose to work with Whisper (large-v3 model) over its
prominent contender, wav2vec 2.0, primarily due to findings
from [18], whose study closely aligns with our objectives. Their
research demonstrated that Whisper’s semi-supervised archi-
tecture surpasses wav2vec 2.0 in handling poor audio quality
and multilingual speech, and in output quality, particularly in
terms of capitalisation and punctuation. This is a critical fac-
tor for Germanic languages where capitalisation rules are strin-
gent. However, as [18] do not address translation tasks, we also
turned to [15] for insights on adapting Whisper for translation.
Each dataset was split 80-20, with 80% allocated for train-
ing and 20% for testing. Within the training set, 20% was used
for validation. We selected the Word Error Rate (WER) as eval-
uation metric for automatic speech recognition and BLEU for
translation. Confidence intervals (CI) were obtained with boot-
strap sampling with 1000 samples. All of the experiments were
conducted on the high-performance computing infrastructure of
the South Tyrol Scientific Network10, using an AMD EPYC
7543P 32-Core Processor (CPU) and an NVIDIA A100 Am-
pere 40GB Graphics Card (GPU).

4.2. Preliminary fine-tuning
In an initial experiment we fine-tuned a model on the GOLD
dataset, explicitly setting the language argument to German11
and using the generic hyperparameter configuration listed in Ta-
ble 2 (column gold). As shown in Table 3, the model demon-
strated a significant reduction in WER and improvement in
BLEU compared to the baseline.
Increasing the number of
training epochs to 50 did not improve performance; neverthe-
less, to assess the impact of this parameter configuration on
boosting Whisper’s performance on our small datasets, we con-
ducted hyperparameter optimisation.
4.3. Optimisation
To gauge the best hyperparameter configuration for model fine-
tuning, we conducted multiple optimisation experiments on
GOLD (hereafter optGold) and on SYNTH (hereafter optSynth)
with the Optuna framework [23]12. Our choice of hyperparam-
eter ranges was informed by existing literature, but we extended
these to explore a broader spectrum of possibilities.
optGold20.
Starting from the range shown in Table 2
and setting Optuna’s optimisation multivariate parameter to
false to examine the impact of Whisper’s hyperparameters in
isolation [24], this experiment ran 20 training trials in 20 hours.
The best configuration achieved a 12% WER and a BLEU score
of 78.90 on GOLD, improving on the preliminary fine-tuning
experiment across all error metrics. These results were influ-
enced by five key parameters, which together accounted for
most of the model’s performance variance. In order of impor-
tance, Adam ϵ had the greatest impact, followed by number of
epochs, warm-up steps, learning rate and Adam β. The domi-
nance of Adam ϵ suggests that its small 1.44 × 10−8 value (the
same as recommended by [25]) was crucial for stable and ef-
fective learning while a higher value could have caused larger
fluctuations in gradients, leading to poor convergence. Training
duration and learning rate scheduling were closer to the higher
end of the value range we set, suggesting that longer training
and more aggressive learning rate scheduling helped achieve

optSynth20. For the optSynth study, we ran an experi-

the GPU and RAM size, as well as the trial numbers to 20, for

best trial from this study, presented in Table 2, used SGD over
AdamW as the optimiser. While slightly better than the base-
line, its overall performance on the test set remained poor. To
further investigate this result, we retrained the best checkpoint
on SYNTH for 50 epochs. However, the performance showed
only negligible improvements compared to training the same
model for 8 epochs and the baseline, suggesting that increas-
ing the number of epochs had no meaningful impact. Indeed,
the most influential hyperparameters for this best trial, in order
of importance, were: Adam β2 (0.97), dropout rate (7%), max
gradient norm (2.72), learning rate (very small at 2.42 × 10−7)
and warm-up steps (129). In the latter experiment, the model’s
optimisation progress after 13 epochs showed no improvement
on the error rates of optSynth20, leading us to conclude that
that Optuna’s multivariate parameter had little to no bearing
on the convergence behavior for this particular benchmark.

4.4. Limitations
The fine-tuning dataset used is limited in size and lacks balance
among the different South Tyrolean variations, which may af-
fect the generalisability of the findings and introduce biases due
to the underrepresentation of certain dialects.
In compliance with submission anonymisation rules, access
to the code and the reusable portion of the training datasets will
be provided upon acceptance of this submission.
4.5. Ethical considerations
Participants of the EUR corpus consented to the use of their
anonymised contributions and their involvement was entirely
voluntary. No metadata or personal identifiable information was
used in the training or evaluation of the model.
5. Conclusion and future work
In this study, we fine-tuned a Speech-to-Text model for trans-
lating South Tyrolean dialect into Standard German using Ope-
nAI’s Whisper, concurrently compiling a first corpus for this
purpose. Compared to the baseline, our optimised model re-
duced the WER from 53% to 12% and achieved a BLEU score
of 86.18, reflecting a higher-quality transcription.
The best
performance was obtained using targeted hyperparameter op-
timisation, particularly for AdamW parameters, learning and
dropout rates. While the model performed well on manually
annotated data, results on the synthetic dataset remained subop-
timal, indicating a need for further refinement. Despite dataset
size and resource limitations, our approach successfully im-
proved dialectal speech processing, showing promise for prac-
tical applications in subtitling and archival documentation. In-
deed, our model is currently deployed in partnership with a lo-
cal heritage beneficiary, the Office for Film and Media of the
Autonomous province of Bolzano, to mass-translate audiovi-
sual archives. The Office will review and correct the transla-
tions, providing feedback to refine the model and ensuring its
practical relevance to community-specific needs. The model is
also being considered for deployment in news broadcasting and
tourism promotion.
Future work will focus on expanding and combining train-
ing data, extending optimisation experiments to include a much
larger number of trials and exploring alternative multitask mod-
els such as Seamless M4Tv213, distill-whisper-large-v314 and
NVIDIA Canary15 for comparative analysis and improved gen-
eralisation.
