An Investigative Study on Recent Sharpness- and Flatness-Based Optimizers for
Enhanced Self-Supervised Speaker Verification
Abderrahim Fathan1, Jahangir Alam1, Xiaolin Zhu*
1Computer Research Institute of Montreal (CRIM), Montreal, Canada
abderrahim.fathan@crim.ca, jahangir.alam@crim.ca, alice.zhuxl@gmail.com
Abstract
Flat minima have recently proven effective for improving
generalization and robustness against label noise. While Adam
and Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) remain the most popular
optimizers in deep learning, other optimizers such as Sharpness-
Aware Minimization (SAM), simultaneously minimizing loss
value and minimizing loss sharpness and/or improving flatness,
have proven to be more effective across a variety of tasks. Moti-
vated by this, we present the first comprehensive study of general-
purpose optimizers (ADOPT, AdEMAMix) and sharpness-aware
variants (SAM, ASAM, GAM, GSAM) for the supervised and
self-supervised speaker verification tasks. We also explore dif-
ferent regularization methods such as weight decay, EMA, and
SWITCH EMA to enhance robustness and learn flat optima with-
out extra optimization costs. Our experimental results highlight
the significant impact of the optimizer choice on generalization,
achieving state-of-the-art performance in self-supervised speaker
verification.
Index Terms: Optimizers, label noise robustness, sharpness-
aware minimization, self-supervised speaker verification
1. Introduction
Automatic speaker verification (ASV) as one of the most con-
venient means of biometric recognition [1], uses the voiceprint
of a speaker to verify his or her identity. Based on known ut-
terances of a speaker, the speaker verification (SV) task aims to
identify whether a speaker is a legitimate user or an imposter.
Although advances in self-supervised learning (SSL) techniques
have helped to improve self-supervised SV leveraging large un-
labeled datasets [2–5], label noise can still dramatically impact
the final accuracy of predictions and generalization due to in-
correct representations. As large-scale datasets are typically
collected from the internet within automatic pipelines [6, 7],
these memorization effects [8] can also impact supervised train-
ing. Therefore, designing robust and effective mechanisms to
select reliable labels [9] for training and/or to curate these noisy
annotations is a crucial task [10].
Many cleansing methods exist in the label noise and data
pruning literature [11–14]. They rely in general on one or mul-
tiple stages of label noise cleansing followed by a supervised
training stage that uses the curated training set. Besides, iterative
approaches to mitigate label noise by refining Pseudo-Labels
(PLs) such as [2, 3] can be intimidating and cumbersome as
they require more memory and computations. Contrary to these
methods and to approaches [4, 15] based on retraining from
scratch on final cleansed datasets, AdaptiveDrop [16] integrates
both label noise correction and filtering mechanisms in a single

framework throughout the whole training to enhance robustness
against noisy labels in one stage of end-to-end training. In-
deed, AdaptiveDrop adopts an adaptive and dynamic strategy
to keep adjusting filtering decisions throughout training: repro-
cess cleansed samples to recover them in later epochs in case
of wrong drop out decisions or to remove inaccurate labels that
were mistakenly included in training. On the other hand, flat
minima have proven recently to be effective for improving gener-
alization and to considerably enhance performance against label
noise [17, 18]. Additionally, optimizing only the training loss
value, as is commonly done, can easily lead to suboptimal solu-
tions. Indeed, learning algorithms motivated by sharpness of the
loss surface, as an effective measure of generalization gap [19],
have provided state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance across vari-
ous tasks and were demonstrated to enhance robustness against
label noise.
Motivated by these recent developments, in this paper we
present the first comprehensive study of various recent opti-
mizers for the tasks of supervised and self-supervised SV for
improved generalization and robustness against label noise. We
study a wide range of recent general-purpose optimizers such
as ADOPT and AdEMAMix, and sharpness-based optimizers
(e.g., SAM [19], Adaptive SAM [20], GAM [17], GSAM [21])
to analyze their impact on robustness of SV systems, especially
when label noise is present during training.
As a comparison, we also explore different regularization
methods and their combinations with optimizers for better label
noise robustness, namely weight decay, exponential moving av-
erage of model parameters (EMA) [22] and SWITCH EMA [23]
to learn flat optima for better generalizations without extra opti-
mization cost. We extend our study to other common success-
ful techniques such as label smoothing regularization [24] and
mixup [25] to dilute label noise, and using small batch sizes [18]
that can lead to flatter local minima to boost the performance of
our networks.
Our experimental results show that the choice of the right
optimizer can make a big difference in terms of training ro-
bustness to label noise and the final generalization performance.
Besides, our approach adopted with our selected list of opti-
mizers achieves superior results compared to a wide range of
self-supervised SV benchmarks.
The contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We perform the first comprehensive study of a wide range of
optimizers for the self-supervised SV task, as alternatives to
the commonly used Adam and SGD optimizers.
• A significant number of experimental results demonstrate the
effectiveness and robustness of our method under real-world
label noise and various loss functions and configurations.
• Despite the effectiveness and robustness of these recent op-
timizers, especially under considerable label noise, we show

2. Related Work and Motivation
Label Noise Robustness One common way to leverage large
unlabeled datasets for SV systems is to use clustering mod-
els [26 –28], or to employ SSL-based objectives (SimCLR,
MoCo) [29] to generate PLs and train the speaker embedding
network using these labels in a discriminative fashion [2, 3].
However, clustering accuracy remains a limiting factor that con-
strains SV performance [2,30]. Alternatively, iterative clustering-
classification methods [2, 31] aim to jointly improve PLs and
SV , but errors in PLs may still propagate, reducing overall per-
formance. Thus, there is a need for more resilient training ap-
proaches to label noise to minimize its impact on generalization.
To tackle label noise, noise-robust algorithms [32,33] can be
used to learn directly from noisy labels, or use label-cleansing
approaches [14, 34] that remove or correct mislabeled samples.
For instance, Subcenter-ArcFace [15] introduced a new loss
function that relaxes ArcFace’s intra-class constraint, improving
robustness to label noise by forming a dominant sub-class of
majority clean samples and grouping hard or noisy samples into
non-dominant sub-classes.
To detect mislabeled instances, one can simply employ ad-
hoc anomaly measures such as cosine similarity to filter out
low-quality instances or correct those that are above a certain
threshold [13, 14] (e.g. low cosine similarity, exceptionally high
training loss value). AdaptiveDrop employs sub-centers to dy-
namically track dominant sub-centers for each class from the
beginning of training via a dictionary table, and performs a dy-
namic drop out of noisy samples at each training step from an
early stage based on the latter table in order to learn progressively
cleaner dominant sub-centers without the need to retrain mod-
els from scratch or to store embeddings and similarities for all
samples. In the context of self-supervised speaker recognition,
to mitigate the negative effect of noise in PLs, [4] proposed an
audio-visual two-step label noise detection and filtering method
by dividing data into easy and peculiar (hard or noisy). [2] also
proposed a two-stage iterative loss-gated learning strategy where
clustering of generated embeddings is applied to generate PLs,
and samples with a large training loss are dropped out.
In another line of work, the BoundaryFace [35] loss adopts a
label correction scheme to correct wrong labels. For that, before
computing the loss of each batch, it performs label correction
by deciding whether to correct a label based on whether the
sample is distributed within the decision boundary of the nearest
negative class: If max{cos(θk +m),∀k̸=yi}− cos(θyi )> 0:

Optimization Algorithms and Regularizers With today’s
non-convex and often complex training loss landscapes, the
training loss provides only few guarantees on model generaliza-
tion ability. To improve that, Sharpness-Aware Minimization
(SAM) [19] navigates the multiplicity of local and global min-
ima by simultaneously minimizing loss value and loss sharpness.
To this aim, SAM seeks parameters that lie in neighborhoods
having uniformly low loss by minimizing the worst-case loss
within a perturbation radius. This results in better generalization
and enhanced label noise robustness. To improve sensitivity to
parameter re-scaling which weakens the connection between
sharpness and generalization gap, Adaptive SAM (ASAM) [20]
has also introduced a concept of adaptive sharpness which is

scale-invariant. Besides, since SAM does not always prefer flat
minima, GSAM [21] defines a surrogate gap to encourage more
flat minima. GAM [17] also presents a novel training procedure
named Gradient norm Aware Minimization to seek minima with
uniformly small curvature across all directions by introducing a
concept of first-order flatness as a stronger measure of flatness.
Finally, ADOPT [36] is a new adaptive gradient method,
suggested as an improvement to Adam by removing the current
gradient from the second moment estimate and changing the
order of the momentum update and the normalization by the
second moment estimate to improve convergence. Besides, to
improve the contribution of older gradients in Momentum based
optimizers, AdEMAMix [37] leverages two EMAs to better take
advantage of past gradients and improve Adam performance.
Additionally, the mixup [25] strategy can be employed as
an efficient strategy to augment data by interpolating different
data samples alongside their labels to mitigate the memorization
effects of label noise. In this paper, we explore two variants of
mixup at both the instance input-level (i-mix) [38] and the la-
tent space (l-mix) [27]. Moreover, Exponential Moving Average
(EMA) [22] is also a widely used weight averaging regularization
to learn flat optima for better generalizations without additional
cost in terms of network optimization. EMA can often contribute
to enhance the stability and output distribution of deep models.
To take both flatness and sharpness into account during optimiza-
tion, Switch EMA (SEMA) method [23] was also proposed as an
enhancement to EMA by simply switching the EMA parameters
to the original model after each epoch.
3. Our self-supervised SV Approach
Clustering-driven PLs can contain a considerable amount of
wrongly annotated samples. To mitigate the negative effect
of these wrong labels on generalization during training and to
improve their accuracy via label correction, we adopt the Adap-
tiveDrop filtering scheme framework [16]. A detailed schematic
diagram of AdaptiveDrop is presented in Figure 1 which depicts
both the label filtering and correction modules.
Typically, we use Cosine Similarity (CS) as a proxy label
confidence indicator to detect the mislabeled instances and set a
constant CS thresholdτ for label filtering to drop high-confident

4. Experimental setup
We conducted a set of experiments based on the V oxCeleb2
dataset [7]. To train the embedding networks, we used the de-
velopment subset of the V oxCeleb2 dataset, which consists of
1,092,009 utterances collected from 5,994 speakers. The eval-
uation was performed according to the Original (V ox1-O) V ox-
Celeb1 trials lists [6].
We employ ECAPA-TDNN [40] as our speaker embedding
network. As acoustic features for our SV experiments, we used
40-dimensional Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)
extracted at every 10 ms, using a 25 ms Hamming window via
Kaldi toolkit [41]. Moreover, we have used waveform-level
data augmentations including additive noise and room impulse
response (RIR) simulation [42] to follow other SV works. Be-
sides, we have applied augmentation over the extracted MFCCs

and regularization techniques, in Tables 1 and 2 we compare
the performance of our system under different configurations,
trained with different optimizers. In Table 1, we report the SV
performance using ground truth labels, while in Table 2 we use

and robustness of SV systems under label noise. We find these
improvements more pronounced when labels are noisy than when
training with true labels. We also notice that these SAM-based
optimizers are considerably more effective than most of our other
studied regularization techniques.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we investigate the performance of various recent
optimizers and regularization methods to improve robustness of
self-supervised (SS) training in the presence of noisy labels. We
proposed a framework encompassing label noise filtering, label
correction, Sharpness-Aware Minimization, and sub-centers of
classes to integrate with any type of loss objective. Besides, our
extensive experiments show that the choice of optimizer can be
crucial for final performance, that label filtering is paramount,
and that our method outperforms various other SS speaker verifi-
cation benchmarks.
