Abstract
We compare automatic speech recognition (ASR) with human
speech recognition (HSR) based on speech material that is tra-
ditionally used for diagnosing hearing deﬁcits. Speciﬁcally, we
quantify the human-machine gap with sentences in noise for
different model sizes and two languages supported by Whisper,
e.g., German and English. For German speech, we also deter-
mine the gap in different rooms and the presence of reverbera-
tion and localized noise. Results are put in context of audiolog-
ical diagnosis using the speech reception threshold (SRT) (i.e.,
the SNR with 50% word recognition rate). We ﬁnd that the
largest ASR system is mildly hearing impaired when exposed
to non-spatial, unpredictable US-English sentences and that us-
ing German speech degrades the SRT by 4.9 dB. In reverberant
rooms, the gap reaches at least 5.9 dB. Based on the language
bias, we estimate that same model can achieve better perfor-
mance than normal-hearing listeners in anechoic conditions for
US-English.
Index Terms : speech recognition, human-computer interac-
tion, computational paralinguistics
1. Introduction
Normally hearing (NH) human listeners excel at recognizing
speech and are able to communicate in complex acoustic condi-
tions such as cocktail parties. In the past, this observation had
motivated comparisons of human speech recognition (HSR) and
automatic speech recognition (ASR) such as the classic review
by [1] who reported that ”Error rates of machines are often more
than a magnitude greater than those of human for quiet, wide-
band, read speech”.
Comparisons between humans and machines were moti-
vated by bridging the gap between two ﬁelds of research [2, 3],
by improving ASR by learning from the biological counterpart
[4, 5] and by simply measuring the gap to quantify how far we
have come in closing the gap between HSR and ASR. Compar-
isons were conducted on sub-lexical level [4, 5, 6], based on
word recognition or on sentence level. The above-mentioned
research was conducted with ASR based on traditional architec-
tures, i.e., a combination of Gaussian mixture models (GMMs)
and hidden Markov models (HMMs).
In recent work, properties of ASR based on transformer-
based E2E ASR has been analyzed, speciﬁcally, the open-
source ASR engine Whisper by openAI which is capable of
multi-lingual ASR [7]. A systematic analysis with different
speech databases has shown that error rates obtained with Whis-
per strongly depend on accent, dialect, and gender (for instance,
with error rates up to three times higher for accented speech
compared to non-accented US English) [8]. Whisper and cloud-
based ASR were benchmarked with QuickSIN, a simple speech-
in-noise test, indicating that Whisper Base could be consid-
ered as mildly hearing impaired, while larger Whisper models
achieve the same or almost the same speech reception thresh-
old (SRT), i.e., the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at which 50% of
words are correctly identiﬁed [9]. This latter comparison was
based on single-channel input without reverberation for accent-
free US English. However, human listeners proﬁt from spatial
release from masking; their speech recognition performance in-
creases when target speech and noise source are not co-located
[10], which is an important aspect in the above-mentioned com-
plex acoustic scenes. In an earlier human-machine comparison
in spatial scenes, the SRT of an ASR system was more than
16 dB higher (worse) compared to human listeners [11], indi-
cating that ASR could not proﬁt from a spatial release of mask-
ing. That study was, however, based on a GMM-HMM system,
and it is unclear how the current state-of-the-art compares to
normal-hearing listeners.
In this paper, we measure the speech-in-noise recognition
of Whisper ASR using an established audiological test, i.e., a
matrix test that is based on noisy sentences. This test is avail-
able in several languages, which enables an analysis of the in-
ﬂuence of input language to the ASR, in our case, US-English
and German. To explore spatial release from masking in ASR,
we also explore error rates in spatial scenes with a speaker at
0° azimuth and a localized masker that is positioned at different
azimuth angles. These analyses are performed with different
ASR models to study the effect of model size and its interaction
with input languages and spatial speech signals.
2. Methods
2.1. Speech-in-noise tests
A widely used method for measuring the SRT relies on matrix
tests with sentence lists speciﬁcally designed to prevent learn-
ing effects. The concept was ﬁrst introduced by Hagerman [12]
for Swedish, where sentences were generated using a random-
walk process through a predeﬁned word matrix. Wagener et
al. [13, 14, 15] later reﬁned this approach by developing a
structured framework that ensured smooth word transitions, ul-
timately leading to the creation of the Oldenburg Sentence Test
(OLSA).
These matrix tests consists of sentences that are syntacti-
cally identical ( 〈name〉〈verb〉〈number〉〈adjective〉〈object〉; e.g.
’Peter kauft drei alte Ringe’; ’Peter buys three old rings’), but
are semantically unpredictable.
In listening experiments, these signals are mixed with a sta-
tionary noise with the same long-term spectrum as the test utter-
ances [15]. During the HSR testing procedure, the SNR is mod-
iﬁed according to the accuracy of word recognition in a noisy
environment, aiming for a 50% correct response rate. This tech-

2.2. Monaural anechoic data
The data for the monaural anechoic task used in this paper was
ﬁrst described in [15]. For this paper the speech was mixed
with noise at 401 different SNRs in the range of -20 dB SNR up
to 20 dB SNR in steps of 0.1 dB. For every SNR, ten sentences
from one test list of the matrix sentence test are used. In total
4010 ﬁles with speech mixed with noise are generated. With
this data, the psychometric function can be sampled. Later the
SRT of the models should be compared to the NH listeners
SRT. Measurements to obtain the NH SRT were conducted in
[15].
The second dataset is similar to the one described above.
It consists of 90 American-English sentences following the
same syntax as the German sentences. A stationary noise
following the long term spectrum of the American-English
speech material is used. Ten sentences from the corpus were
selected randomly and mixed at each analyzed SNR.
2.3. Binaural spatial data
To compare spatial release from masking and performance in
different reverberant scenes, we used an existing dataset intro-
duced by Beutelmann and Brand [20]. It features three spa-
tial scenes (Anechoic, Cafeteria, Ofﬁce) which are generated by
convolving the monaural stationary noise and speech from the
German matrix test with head related transfer functions (HRTF)
recorded for different azimuth angles. HRTFs for Ofﬁce and
Cafeteria were measured by Beutelmann et al. [20], the ane-
choic HRTFs by Algazi et al. [21]. The latter are part of an
open-source database and have been recorded with a KEMAR
manikin. Ofﬁce and cafeteria HRTFs were measured with a
B&K 4128 manikin and using maximum length sequences. The
loudspeakers in the ofﬁce room had a distance of 1.45 m to the
head center of the manikin, while in the cafeteria a single loud-
speaker was positioned at various locations around the manikin
seated at a table. In all scenes, the speaker is virtually placed
at 0° azimuth, while the noise positions differ slightly due to
practical considerations when measuring the HRTFs. (noise az-
imuth for Anechoic and Ofﬁce: -140, -100, -45 0, 45, 80, 125,
and 180◦; noise azimuth for Cafeteria: -135, -90, -45, 0,45, 90,
135, and 180 ◦). To obtain the NH SRTs for the room condi-
tions and angles, Beutelmann et al. [20] conducted measure-
ments with eight NH participants. Speech intelligibility mea-
surements were conducted using the H ¨orTech Oldenburg Mea-
surement Applications (OMA). One random test list containing
20 sentences was chosen to determine the SRT. Participants re-
peated the words they recognized, and responses were scored
by an instructor. The speech level was adaptively adjusted to
determine the SRT. A stationary noise was used as masker (cf.
Section 2.1) and presented at a ﬁxed level of 65 dB. Measure-
ments were randomized and conducted in a laboratory setting.
2.4. ASR models
The models used in this paper are all Whisper [7] models of
different size. The following model variables were examined:
Tiny, Base, Small, large-v3-turbo (from now on referred to as
V3turbo). Due to computational constraints, the largest model
available was not included. A medium-sized model was also not
analyzed since pilot experiments indicated worse performance
but higher computational cost than V3turbo. The implemen-
tation is part of the open-source framework Speechbrain [22]
[23]. No changes were made to the architecture, nor was any
training or adaptation carried out.
2.5. Determination of the speech reception threshold
For all acoustic conditions (including the monaural case and
spatial scenes), audio is mixed with 121 different SNRs in the
range of -40 to 20 dB SNR with steps of 0.5 dB SNR. 10 random
audio ﬁles are selected and then mixed with noise at each SNR.
For spatial scenes only, the speech mixed with noise was
then converted through a binaural speech intelligibility model
(BSIM) from a stereo signal to a single channel signal as de-
scribed below. This resulted in 9,680 signals for each spatial
condition (29,040 in total).

Binaural integration: To obtain the single-channel data re-
quired as input to the ASR models, we integrated the input sig-
nals from the left and right ear in spatial scenes. To this end, we
applied a blind equalization-cancellation (EC) processing stage
borrowed from a model that predicts human speech recognition
performance in spatial speech and noise conditions [24]. This
EC stage can be regarded as a delay-and-subtract beamformer
optimized for yielding human performance. This EC process-
ing works blindly, that is, without any knowledge about the tar-
get speech, the interfering sounds, the room acoustics, or direc-
tions of sound sources. The internal parameters of the EC stage
(i.e., the interaural gain and delay values that are applied before
the cancellation stage) are controlled by a modulation analysis
that maximizes the speech-similarity of the modulations of the
single (mono) output signal. Consequently, this approach does
only work for interfering sounds that are not modulated like hu-
man speech. In this study, only steady state noises were used.
