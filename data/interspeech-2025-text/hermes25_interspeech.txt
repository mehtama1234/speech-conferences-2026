Acoustic similarities, articulatory uniqueness:  
Speech production mechanisms in individuals with congenital lip paralysis 
Anne Hermes1, Ivana Didirková2, Philipp Buech1, Gilles Vannuscorps3 
1Laboratoire de Phonétique et Phonologie, UMR 7018, Université Sorbonne Nouvelle, CNRS,

anne.hermes@sorbonne-nouvelle.fr, ivana.didirkova@univ-montp3.fr,

Abstract 
Moebius syndrome is a rare congenital neuromuscular disorder 
characterized by facial paralysis, which can severely impact 
speech. Despite these constraints, some individuals with 
congenital lip paralysis develop very natural speech 
intelligibility 
by 
employing 
strategies 
that 
remain 
underexplored. This study examines the acoustic-articulatory 
adaptations in three French-speaking individuals with Moebius 
syndrome compared to three controls. Using articulatory and 
acoustic data, we analyzed speech patterns in disyllabic words. 
Results indicate that Moebius speakers achieve acoustically 
similar vowel targets to controls despite significant articulatory 
differences, including increased tongue movement to 
compensate for lip paralysis. These findings highlight the 
adaptability of the speech motor system, supporting the notion 
of motor equivalence in achieving intelligible speech.   
Index Terms: congenital lip paralysis, Moebius syndrome, 
acoustics-articulation, adaptations, motor equivalence, speech 
therapy 
1. Introduction 
1.1. Moebius syndrome 
1.1.1. General 
Moebius syndrome is a rare congenital neuromuscular disorder 
affecting approximately 0.00002 to 0.002% of the world’s 
population. This condition is characterized by the absence or 
underdevelopment of the 6th and 7th cranial nerves, which are 
responsible 
for 
controlling horizontal 
eye 
movements and facial muscles [1]. One of the most prominent 
symptoms of Moebius syndrome is bilateral facial paralysis. 
This paralysis significantly impacts facial motor skills, 
affecting essential functions such as expressions and eating. 
Fig. 1 is showing an individual with Moebius syndrome for 
different tasks involving the lips.  
 
Figure 1: Individual with Moebius syndrome trying to (A) 
close the lips as much as possible, (B) stretch the lips, (C) 
round the lips and (D) open the lips as much as possible. 
1.1.2. Development 
Moebius syndrome can present significant challenges in 
communication, as demonstrated by a study conducted by [2] 
on the communicative disorders of 22 individuals with the 
condition. The findings revealed that most individuals exhibited 
varying degrees of dysarthria. Nonetheless, older children often 
managed to develop intelligible speech through oral language 
stimulation and compensatory articulatory strategies. However, 
children diagnosed with Moebius syndrome frequently 
experience delays in language development. Research by [3] 
indicates that these children encounter cognitive and social 
challenges, including learning difficulties and developmental 
delays. Despite these obstacles, compelling evidence supports 
the positive effects of speech therapy interventions on their 
linguistic skills. This underscores the critical need for targeted 
interventions that enhance speech production and overall 
communication abilities in individuals with Moebius 
syndrome, facilitating better outcomes and improved quality of 
life. 
1.1.3. Articulation and intelligibility 
Individuals 
with 
Moebius 
syndrome 
exhibit 
distinct articulatory strategies due to their congenital lip 
paralysis and resulting in incomplete lip closure. These 
impairments significantly impact the production of bilabial 
sounds (/m, b, p/), labio-dental sounds (/f, v/), and rounded 
vowels. 
Numerous 
studies 
have 
reported 
frequent misarticulations associated 
with 
impaired 
labial 
function 
[4-6]. 
Interestingly, 
approximately 20% 
of 
individuals with Moebius syndrome have been observed to 
develop particularly efficient articulatory strategies. These 
allow them to adapt their speech production despite the 
challenges posed by their condition.  
However, it is noteworthy that to date, there has been 
no quantitative 
analysis specifically 
examining 
acoustic 
and articulatory adaptations in individuals with Moebius 
syndrome who reached intact intelligibility. This research gap 
highlights the need for further studies to explore the adaptations 
in speech production within this population. 
1.2. Acoustics-articulation 
The focus of this study is understanding how different 
articulatory configurations (here in individuals with congenital 
lip paralysis) can produce similar acoustic outputs. This 
phenomenon is crucial for identifying and developing 
adaptations. For example, alternative articulatory strategies can

allow speakers to develop natural intelligibility even when 
primary articulators, such as the lips, are compromised. This 
adaptability, often referred to as motor equivalence [7], 
underscores the ability of the speech motor system to

Investigating how articulatory constraints affect acoustic 
outputs is particularly valuable for developing targeted 
therapies for individuals with speech production challenges. 
This knowledge is especially relevant for conditions such as 
Moebius syndrome, where articulatory movements are 
restricted or impaired. Researchers and clinicians can design 
more effective interventions to improve communication 
abilities, already for young individuals with lip paralysis. 
1.3. Research objectives 
The primary aim of this study is to address the lack of detailed 
research on speech motor control adaptations in individuals 
with Moebius syndrome who exhibit complete lip paralysis but 
achieve intact speech intelligibility. This study represents the 
first detailed acoustic and articulatory analysis of this 
population, focusing on how they adapt their speech production 
to overcome severe articulatory constraints. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Recordings  
2.1.1. Participants 
The study included three female French speakers with Moebius 
syndrome (M1: 41 years, M2: 43 years, M3: 27 years), all of 
whom demonstrated intact speech intelligibility despite the 
presence of complete lip paralysis as determined by a speech 
therapist. To provide a baseline for comparison, the study also 
included three control speakers without Moebius syndrome. 
The control group consisted of C1 (28 years, female), C2 (40 
years, male), and C3 (20 years, male).  
2.1.2. Data collection 
Articulatory and acoustic data were collected simultaneously 
from all six native French speakers. Data collection utilized 
the Electromagnetic 
Articulograph 
AG 
501 (Carstens 
Medizinelektronik GmbH).  
 
Figure 2: Experimental setup for recordings with AG501, 
sagittal view of sensors and extracted trajectories of sensor 
movements. Figure courtesy by XY. 
Sensors were placed on the chin (CHIN), upper and lower 
lips (ULIP, LLIP), tongue tip (TTIP), tongue mid (TMID), 
and tongue body (TBO), with additional reference sensors 
positioned behind the left and right ear and the nasion (see Fig. 
2). The articulatory signal was recorded at a sampling rate of 
1250 Hz. The data were processed using a Butterworth low-
pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 25 Hz and filter order 5. 
Acoustic data were recorded simultaneously at a sampling rate 
of 44.1 kHz with 16-bit resolution. 
2.1.3. Speech material 
The speech material analyzed in this study is part of a larger 
corpus. The current study focuses specifically on word 
productions to investigate articulatory and acoustic adaptations. 
The word production task involved disyllabic target words 
embedded in carrier phrases, e.g., “Elle a dit mamie deux fois.’, 
Engl. ‘She said mamie (=granny) two times.’ These target 
words were designed to vary systematically in their vowel 
qualities across both stressed and unstressed syllables. The 
vowels included in the study were /i, y, e, a, o, u/. These vowels 
were surrounded by different consonantal contexts /b, m, v/, 
allowing for an analysis of how articulatory constraints 
influence speech production and how compensatory strategies 
are employed. For this analysis, a total of 590 tokens were taken 
into account, comprising 97 distinct target words.  
2.2. Annotations and measurements 
Measurements were conducted using a custom script written in 
Python v3.10.15 [8]. For the acoustic analysis, we used 
Parselmouth v0.4.3 [9], which interfaces with Praat v6.1.38 
[10]. For the articulatory analysis, including the x, y-, z-position 
of sensors, the data extraction routine from ADA (Articulatory 
Data Analysis; [11]) was applied. 
2.2.1. Acoustics measures 
For the acoustics analysis, we looked at the spatial and temporal 
domains. The annotation of the acoustics segments was 
performed 
semi-automatically: 
forced-alignment 
was 
conducted using WebMAUS [12] and manually corrected. For 
the temporal domain, we examined syllable durations relative 
to the target word duration (unaccented syllable, i.e., C1V1 vs. 
accented syllable, i.e., C2V2). For the spatial domain, i.e., 
formant analysis, we extracted the formants F1, F2 and F3 for 
the vowels /i, y, e, a, o, u/ at their midpoints (50%) using the 
Burg algorithm, with the maximum formant adapted to the 
speakers’ sex (female = 5500Hz, male = 5000Hz) in the 
unaccented and accented syllables. 
2.2.2. Articulatory measures 
To shed lights on the modifications in the articulatory domain, 
we analyzed the sensor’s position on the tongue body (TBO) 
and tongue tip (TTIP) and the lower lip (LLIP) in 3 dimensions: 
i.e., the horizontal (x), vertical (y) and the lateral (z) 
dimensions, for all vowels at their midpoints for the unaccented 
vs. accented syllables.  
2.3. Statistical analyses 
Bayesian linear regression models were used for the statistical 
analysis. Separate models were constructed for each 
measurement based on vowel position. For formant measures 
and articulatory measures, we had the following model 
structure: parameters were entered as response variable and 
state (control, moebius), vowel (/i, y, e, a, o, u/) and their 
interaction (state*vowel) as common level effects as well as a 
3729

by-speaker intercept as group-level effect. For the parameter of 
relative syllable duration, a similar structure was used, with 
state as common-level effect and by-speaker intercept as group-
level effect. 
The statistical models were executed using Bambi [13], 
which interfaces with PyMC [14]. We utilized default priors 
and ran 4 chains, each with 4000 iterations, resulting in a total 
of 16,000 iterations for the analyses. The articulatory and 
formant data were z-scored for each speaker prior to the 
analysis. Results are reported by the mean and the lower and 
upper bounds of the 95% posterior distribution. Decision-
making was guided by the HDI+ROPE decision rule [15], 
which is based on the relationship between the highest density 
interval (HDI) of the posterior distribution and a Region of 
Practical Equivalence (ROPE). Reported are clear differences 
between control and Moebius speakers when the HDI was 
entirely outside the ROPE, while no or no clear difference was 
determined when the HDI included or was inside the ROPE. 
Reported are also tendencies when the HDI was higher or lower 
0. The ROPE was set uniformly to [-0.1, 0.1] for standardized 
variables following [16]. 
3. Results 
3.1. Acoustics 
3.1.1. Syllable duration  
First, as expected, the analysis revealed that accented syllables 
relative to the word duration were longer than unaccented 
vowels relative to the word duration, in both groups (Moebius: 
accented=0.53 > unaccented=0.47 vs. controls: accented=0.54 
> unaccented=0.46). There were no differences in the relative 
duration of the unaccented and accented syllables when 
comparing individuals with Moebius syndrome and controls 
(unaccented syllable: β=0.07 [-1.35, 1.44], accented syllable: 
β=-0.05 [-1.44, 1.36]).  
3.1.2. Formant patterns 
For the spatial domain, we looked at formant patterns of the 
vowels /i, y, e, a, o, u/ in the unaccented and accented domain. 
Fig. 3 displays the F1-F2 formants comparing individuals with 
Moebius syndrome (dashed) and control (filled). The analysis 
revealed that both speaker groups show a similar pattern with 
rather distinct vowel (both in the unaccented and the accented 
syllables).

3.2. Articulation 
In the articulatory domain, we were interested in looking at the 
positions of the sensors during the vowel production (at 50%) 
to be comparable to the formant patterns of the individuals with 
Moebius syndrome and the control speakers. Fig. 5 displays the 
vertical and horizonal values for each vowel separately for the 
unaccented and accented syllable for individuals with Moebius 
syndrome (dashed) and control speakers (filled). By visual 
inspection, the differences between individuals with Moebius 
and controls are more pronounced in that there is less overlap 
between the groups.  
We found a tendency for differences between the groups in 
horizontal position (TBOx) in the V1 (unaccented, β=-0.31 [-
0.55, -0.09]), in that /u/ is more fronted in individuals with 
Moebius (β=0.63 [0.38, 0.84] and /y/ is more back (β=-0.36 [-
0.63, -0.11]) than in controls. Further, there is a difference in 
the vertical TBO position (TBOy) of individuals with Moebius 
syndrome in V1 (β=0.74 [0.52, 0.96]) with a higher TBO for /i/ 
(β=0.69 [0.46, 0.92]), and a lower TBO for /o/ (β=-0.73 [-0.97, 
-0.5]) and for /u/ (β=-1.12 [-1.35, -0.91]) when compared to 
controls. In the lateral dimension (TBOz), there were no 
differences (β=-0.0 [-0.4, 0.37]). 
In the accented vowel, V2, we did find a difference in the 
horizonal domain TBOx (β=-0.51 [-0.72, -0.3], the vowel /a/ is 
more back, /u/ is more fronted (β=0.66 [0.43, 0.87]), /y/ shows

tendency for being more fronted in individuals with Moebius

To sum up, although we did not find differences in the acoustic 
formant patterns between the two groups, we observed 
differences articulatory patterns during the vowel production, 
particularly in the tongue body vertical and horizontal domain. 
As has been shown by [17] in that tongue movement during 
labial fast syllable repetitions are much more pronounced, 
whereas lip movements much less in individuals with Moebius 
syndrome than in control speakers, we have also analyzed the 
position of the tongue tip sensor as well as the lower lip sensor.   
 
For the unaccented V1, we did not find any difference in 
the LLIPx (β=-0.22 [-0.48, 0.04]), LLIPy dimension (β=-0.2 [-
0.46, 0.05]), or LLIPz (β=0.04 [-0.34, 0.43]. 
 
For V2 (accented syllable), we found a difference in the 
individuals with Moebius syndrome showing lower values in 
LLIPx (β=-0.34 [-0.55, -0.15]) and also in LLIPy (β=-0.43 [-
0.65, -0.19]), with no difference in LLIPz (β=-0.14 [-0.56, 
0.24]).  
For the unaccented V1, we found a difference in the TTIPx 
which is more back for individuals with Moebius syndrome 
(β=-0.57 [-0.82, -0.33]). We also found a difference in TTIPy 
with lower tongue tip for individuals with Moebius syndrome 
(β=-0.48 [-0.68, -0.28]). Interestingly, there also was a 
difference in the lateral dimension, TTIPz, being more left 
oriented during the vowel production (β=1.01 [0.65, 1.4]).  
For the accented V2, we found a difference in the TTIPx 
which is also more back for individuals with Moebius syndrome 
(β=-0.84 [-1.05, -0.62]). Again, we also found a difference in 
TTIPy with lower tongue tip for individuals with Moebius 
syndrome (β=-0.72 [-0.94, -0.49]), and a difference in the 
lateral dimension, TTIPz, being more left oriented during the 
vowel production (β=0.68 [0.27, 1.06]). 
4. Discussion and conclusions 
This study provides new insights into the acoustic and 
articulatory adaptations employed by individuals with 
congenital lip paralysis (Moebius syndrome) to achieve 
intelligible speech despite severe articulatory constraints. The 
key findings are that individuals with Moebius syndrome 
produce vowel formant patterns that are acoustically very 
similar to those of control speakers, despite significant 
differences in their articulatory mechanisms. Specifically, 
Moebius speakers compensate for their inability to use the lips 
by employing more pronounced tongue movements, both in the 
horizontal and vertical dimensions. These adaptations are 
particularly evident in the production of rounded vowels (with 
a lowered F3 for rounded vowels), where tongue movements 
substitute for the lack of lip rounding. Additionally, no 
significant differences were observed in the temporal domain, 
as both groups exhibited comparable relative syllable durations 
for accented and unaccented syllables. 
The observed acoustic similarities, and the articulatory 
uniqueness, align with the concept of motor equivalence, which 
highlights the flexibility of the speech motor system in 
achieving similar acoustic outputs through alternative 
articulatory strategies. This adaptability underscores the 
remarkable capacity of the human speech motor system to 
overcome anatomical or neurological constraints. For 
individuals with Moebius syndrome, the increased reliance on 
tongue movements compensates for the lip paralysis [17], 
demonstrating the critical role of the tongue in maintaining 
intelligibility.  
The results of this study have important implications for 
speech therapy and clinical interventions. The reliance on 
tongue-based compensatory strategies in individuals with 
Moebius syndrome highlights the need for therapeutic 
approaches that focus on enhancing tongue control and 
flexibility. Speech-language pathologists can use these findings 
to design targeted interventions that leverage the tongue's 
adaptability to compensate for lip immobility. For example, 
therapy could include exercises to improve tongue precision 
and range of motion, particularly for producing rounded vowels 
and bilabial sounds. Additionally, understanding the specific 
articulatory adaptations employed by Moebius speakers can 
help clinicians develop personalized strategies to optimize 
speech intelligibility in this population. 
While this study provides valuable insights, several 
limitations should be acknowledged. Future research should 
also explore potential variability in compensatory strategies 
across individuals. Additionally, this study focused primarily 
on vowel production; future research should investigate 
consonantal features and their associated articulatory 
adaptations, particularly for bilabial and labiodental sounds, 
which are most affected by lip paralysis. Detailed analyses of 
tongue modifications, also in the lateral dimension, could 
provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying motor 
equivalence. 
This study highlights the remarkable adaptability of the 
speech motor system in individuals with congenital lip 
paralysis, who achieve intelligible speech despite severe 
articulatory constraints. The findings emphasize the importance 
of tongue-based compensatory strategies in overcoming lip 
immobility and achieving acoustic targets. This research 
contributes to our understanding of speech production in 
populations with congenital lip paralysis. These insights have 
significant implications for clinical practice, offering a 
foundation for developing targeted interventions to improve 
communication abilities in individuals with Moebius syndrome 
and similar conditions.
