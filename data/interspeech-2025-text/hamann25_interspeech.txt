Are loan sequences different from foreign sequences? A perception study with 
Japanese listeners on coronal obstruent – high front vowel sequences 
Silke Hamann, Andrea Alićehajić 
University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands 
silke.hamann@uva.nl, andrea.alicehajic@gmail.com 
 
 
Abstract 
Native phonotactics influences speech perception, as numerous 
studies have shown. The present study tackles the question 
whether there is a difference in perceptual performance if the 
involved sequence occurs only in loanwords, compared to a 
sequence that does not occur at all in the native language. This 
was tested with the native Japanese sequences of palatal 
affricate plus /i/, compared to /ti/ (accepted only in loanwords) 
versus /zi/ (not accepted in Japanese) in an online AX 
discrimination task with 39 Japanese speakers (21-63 years 
old), who also had to answer three questions on their received 
English input. Participants performed significantly better at 
discriminating the accepted loan sequence /ti/, though 
discrimination of the foreign sequence /zi/ was also quite high 
(ranging from 40-100% correct). The results indicate that 
discriminability is only partly guided by native phonotactics. A 
potential role of amount of English input measured by self-
report could not be attested. 
Index Terms: Speech perception, Japanese, discrimination 
task, phonotactic restrictions, loanword adaptation 
1. Introduction 
The perception of an unfamiliar sequence in an unknown or new 
language is strongly influenced by our native phonotactic 
restrictions. This has been anecdotally reported already in 1931 
[1], and experimentally supported by many later studies, for 
example, [2], [3], [4] and [5], showing that participants usually 
have problems distinguishing between structures that are illegal 
in their native language and similar structures with a repair 
strategy applied (such as introduction of an illusory vowel, or 
changes in a consonant or a vowel). The study by Dupoux et al. 
[2], e.g., found that native Japanese listeners performed poorly 
in discriminating words like [ebzo] from [ebɯzo] (due to illegal 
structure */bz/ in Japanese), while native French listeners 
performed poorly in discriminating words differing in a vowel 
duration contrast only (due to the lack of a phonemic vowel 
length contrast in their language; */Vː/). 
At the same time, new phoneme sequences are regularly 
introduced into languages via loanwords due to extended 
contact between a phonotactically more restricted borrowing 
language and a less restrictive source language [6]. This is 
particularly obvious with the rising influence of English via 
social media and technology, bringing new words and 
phonotactics into languages with a stricter syllable structure. 
The question then arises how new sequences can be borrowed 
if they cannot be correctly perceived by the native listeners. A 
potential answer is that these borrowings are performed by 
bilingual speakers who have sufficient competence in both 
languages [e.g., 6, 7]. However, loan sequences are not only 
used by bilingual speakers, hence the perception of all speakers 
of the borrowing language must have shifted over time to allow 
for formerly illegal sequences to enter the language, as argued 
by [8]. The present study looks into the question how this native 
perception changes, and more specifically, whether native 
speakers are more sensitive to phonotactic sequences that have 
been introduced already into their native language via 
loanwords than to sequences that are (not yet) nativized. This is 
done by comparing how well native speakers are able to 
discriminate a foreign language sequence that has been 
integrated into their language via loanwords from its closest 
native sequence, and whether this performance is similar to 
discriminating a sequence from the same foreign language that 
hasn’t entered the native language yet.  
The language of focus in this study is Japanese. Japanese is 
known for its restrictive phonotactics, though several 
previously impossible consonant-vowel sequences entered the 
language over the last centuries through loanwords from 
English, most likely due to increased contact with that language 
[9, 10, 11]. A well-known example for such a phonotactic 
restriction occurring only in loanwords is the sequence /ti/. 
Before 1890, this sequence was adapted into Japanese with 
palatalization and affrication of the plosive, as in the English 
word team borrowed as /tɕiː.mɯ/, following native phonotactic 
restrictions. From 1930 onwards, new loans from English with 
this sequence were adapted as /ti/, e.g. party /paː.tiː/ [12, 13], 
introducing a new syllable to the Japanese language. 
The sequence /zi/, on the other hand, has not yet been attested 
in Japanese pronunciation dictionaries or similar, with the 
exception of the careful articulation of the North American 
letter ‘Z’ [14], and no examples containing /zi/ can be found in 
recent loanword accounts (e.g., Appendix II of [15]). A 
previous study [16] showed that Japanese native listeners are 
better than chance at discriminating [dʑi] – [zi] in non-words, 
with an average performance of 77 percent correct. [16] failed 
to compare this performance to established loan sequences to 
enable a conclusion on whether the found performance is 
average for all “foreign” phonotactic sequences (existing or 
non-existing in the language), or whether it is below the 
performance for sequences that occur in existing loanwords. 
[16] tried to explain potential variation in performance by the 
age of the participants, with the hypothesis that older speakers 
would perform worse than younger speakers as they are 
expected to have had less English input and hence less 
experience with the sequence [dʑi]; however, the influence of 
age on performance was not significant in that study. 
The present study compares how well Japanese listeners 
discriminate [dʑi] – [zi] with how well they perform on [tɕi] –
[ti], with the expectation that they are worse at the former as it 
contains an illicit sequence in Japanese.

It must be noted that for the Japanese sequence /dʑi/, variation 
in the pronunciation has been observed: it can be realized as 
voiced affricate [dʑi] or as voiced fricative [ʑi]. Traditionally, 
this variation was attributed to word-position: word-initial /dʑi/ 
would be realized as the voiced affricate [dʑi], and word-
internal as the voiced fricative [ʑi] [17]. More recent studies, 
however, have found that the variation appears to be primarily 
linked to speech rate, with the voiced fricative [ʑi] occurring in 
quicker speech, and the voiced affricate [dʑi] with a slower 
speech rate [18, 19]. For this reason, we transcribe this sequence 
in the following as [(d)ʑi].  
In addition to testing a potential difference in performance, we 
include self-reported input of English by our participants. [20] 
demonstrated that self-assessment of English input could be 
used as a reliable tool to predict phonetic variation in the 
adaptation of English final consonants by Italian speakers. The 
present study tests whether the amount of self-reported received 
English input of Japanese listeners can similarly predict 
variation in the ability to perceive the contrast between [(d)ʑi] 
– [zi] and [tɕi] – [ti], with the expectation that a self-reported 
greater exposure to English positively corresponds to 
discrimination performance.  
2. Perception experiment 
For the present perception experiment, we employed an online 
AX discrimination task created with the software Experiment 
Designer (ED; [21]). The experiment was approved by the 
ethics committee of the University of Amsterdam. The task 
took approximately 10 minutes to complete and could be 
carried out in the comfort of the participant’s home. 
2.1. Participants 
39 Japanese native speakers (26 female, 12 male, 1 non-binary) 
participated in the online experiment, with ages ranging from 
21 to 63 years at testing. During the selection process, we 
controlled for area of residence to minimize linguistic 
differences: all participants originated from and lived in the 
greater Tokyo region of Japan (including Tokyo, Kanagawa, 
Saitama, and Chiba). Furthermore, we controlled for linguistic 
environment to ensure that the participants’ perceptual input 
was not influenced substantially by another language; all 
participants grew up in monolingual Japanese households. 
Participants not fulfilling these criteria were excluded from the 
experiment (this applied to two, resulting in the given 39). 
2.2. Speaker and recordings 
Our speaker was a female native from the Tokyo region of 
Japan (Saitama prefecture), aged 27 at the time of recording. 
Due to her high level of English, this speaker could comfortably 
produce the sequence [zi], as attested perceptually and via 
inspection of the resulting spectrograms by the authors. The 
speaker produced several tokens of each stimulus, all recorded 
in the sound-attenuated booth of the Speech Lab at the 
University of Amsterdam, with a Neumann TLM103 cardioid 
condenser microphone at a 44.1 kHz sampling rate. 
2.3. Stimuli 
A total of 83 pairs of disyllabic Japanese non-words were 
created as stimuli for the online experiment. 40 pairs made up 
the experimental items: 20 tested the discrimination of [ti] – 
[tɕi], and 20 the discrimination of [zi] – [(d)ʑi]. Out of each of  
these 20 experimental pairs, 10 pairs consisted of ‘same’ 
stimuli, i.e. two tokens of the same item, and 10 pairs consisted 
of ‘different’ stimuli. The latter were presented in one of two 
possible orders, i.e., in a fixed order for all participants. In Table 
1, all experimental items (and for different stimuli also their 
order of presentation) are given.  
For the 10 stimuli representing [(d)ʑi] in the different pairs, five 
were phonetically realized as [ʑi], and five as [dʑi]. In the same 
pairs for [(d)ʑi], only [ʑi] was used, as our speaker produced 
[ʑi] more often than [dʑi].  
An illustration of a [ti] – [tɕi] experimental pair is given at the 
top of Figure 1. We can see that there is a clear difference in 
simple burst with some aspiration noise for [hiti] on the left and 
an affricate with longer friction noise following the burst for 
[hitɕi] on the right side. An example for a [zi] – [ʑi] experi-
mental pair is given at the bottom of Figure 1. Here, the only 
difference between [rɯzi] on the left and [rɯʑi] on the right is 
the starting frequency of the high-intensity friction noise of the 
sibilants, which is much higher for the alveolar [z] in the left 
spectrogram (its beginning is just visible at the upper edge, at 
approx. 8,500 Hz). Pairs with [zi] – [dʑi] have the additional 
acoustic difference of a silence (the closure phase) before the 
alveolopalatal fricative for [dʑ], not illustrated here. 
Further 40 pairs were fillers. Like the experimental pairs, the 
first syllable of filler pairs was always the same, whereas the 
second syllable could be the same or different. The phoneme 
combinations used to compile the filler items are all phono-
tactically licit in Japanese. Half of the filler pairs contained the 
same non-words, half different ones. Example for different 
filler pairs are: [keni] – [kegi], [bopi] – [bopa] and [miɕi] – 
[minɯ]. The remaining three pairs were catch trials that 
consisted of non-word pairs differing both in first and second 
syllable, e.g., [kega] – [teɕi]. These catch trials were deployed 
as a means to filter out participants who were not paying 
attention to the stimuli. If a participant marked a minimum of 
two out of three catch trials as ‘same’, thus incorrectly, their 
responses would be excluded from the results. As only four 
participants responded incorrectly to one out of three catch 
trials, no participant had to be excluded. 
Stimuli pairs were created in Praat [22]. Same stimuli pairs 
always contained two different tokens of the same non-word. 
The inter-stimulus interval was always 800 ms. This ensured 
participants accessed their phonological knowledge instead of 
simply comparing acoustic similarity (cf. [23]).

2.4. Procedure 
Participants were asked to complete the online AX 
discrimination task by listening to two Japanese bisyllabic non-
words at a time, and indicating for each pair whether they 
perceived the two words to be the same or different. This was 
done by pressing the left or right arrow keys on their keyboard, 
where one key corresponded to ‘same’ and the other ‘different’. 
The participants were informed and instructed about the task 
through a short text in Japanese, where it was also explained 
that the amount of ‘same’ and ‘different’ items might not be 
balanced. 
The experiment was divided into two trial blocks: the first 
containing 42 stimulus pairs and the second 41 stimulus pairs. 
The trial blocks were separated by a self-paced break. In the 
first block, participants were asked to select the left arrow key 
for ‘same’ and the right arrow key for ‘different’. After the 
break, the response keys were switched. In order to avoid 
confusion, the responses corresponding to arrow keys were 
listed at the bottom of the screen for the duration of the whole 
experiment. The order of appearance of stimulus pairs was 
pseudo-randomized such that experimental items were spaced 
apart by a minimum of two filler items. In addition, 
experimental items did not occur within the first three stimuli 
pairs in block 1. Catch trial pairs were presented in fixed 
positions as pairs 4, 50 and 79. 
2.5. Self-report on English input 
At the end of the experiment, participants had to answer three 
questions on the amount of English input they received: 
(1) How many hours a week are you exposed to the English 
language? (Through social media, movies and series, 
music, school, interactions with English native speakers) 
(2) How many hours a week do you speak English? 
(3) How many months did you spend in an English-speaking 
country? 
For the first two questions, the response options were ranging 
from “0” to “15 or more hours”, for the last question the range 
was from “0” to “18 or more months”, all with steps of 1 in-
between.  
For each participant, we calculated an English input score by 
adding up the values they provided for each answer. This score 
could range from 0, corresponding to having had no English 
input at all, to 48, corresponding to having had very frequent 
input and having spent at least 18 months in an English-
speaking country. The maximum our participants had was 37, 
see Figure 1 for the distribution of their English input scores.

2.6. Statistical analysis 
The statistical analysis was carried out with R [24] in RStudio 
using lm4 [25] and lmerTest [26] packages. To investigate 
whether participants were better at discriminating [tɕi] – [ti] 
than [(d)ʑi] – [zi], we looked only at different pairs of the 
experimental items. A generalized linear mixed-effects model 
was fitted with Correctness of the responses to the experimental 
items (binary; with 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect) as a 
function of the categorical predictor Contrast (categorical: [ti] 
– [tɕi] or [zi] – [(d)ʑi]; coded with a sum-to-zero orthogonal 
contrast). Random intercepts were fitted for Participant and 
Item. 
For the question on whether self-reported English input had an 
effect on perception of the two contrasts, a generalized linear 
mixed-effects model was fitted with Correctness as a function 
of the interaction between the continuous predictor English 
score and the categorical predictor SameDifferent (coded with 
a sum-to-zero orthogonal contrast). Random slopes were fitted 
for the interaction between SameDifferent and Participant, and 
for the interaction between English score and Item. 
Additionally, descriptive statistics were drawn up by 
calculating the number and percentages of correct responses per 
participant. 
3. Results 
Participants’ performance on ‘different’ experimental items for 
the [(d)ʑi] – [zi] contrast was 90% on average, ranging from 
40% correct (one participant) to 100% correct (18 participants). 
For the [tɕi] – [ti] contrast, participants performed with an 
average accuracy of 98%, ranging from 90% (7 participants) to 
100% (32 participants), see Fig. 3. The statistical analysis 
showed that participants’ odds of responding accurately on 
‘different’ items of [tɕi] – [ti] were 7.28 times higher than the 
odds of responding accurately to ‘different’ items of [(d)ʑi] – 
[zi], and this main effect of Contrast on Accuracy was highly 
significant (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 2.329 .. 22.778). 
 
Figure 3: Accuracy for “different” items (in %) for [(d)ʑi] – 
[zi] (including a foreign sequence) on the left and [tɕi] – [ti] 
(including a loan sequence) on the right. 
The statistical analysis for the influence of English input score 
showed that there was no significant main effect of English 
score (p = 0.411, 95% CI = 0.968 .. 1.081) and no significant 
interaction with SameDifferent (p = 0.636; 95% CI = 0.891 .. 
1.073). 
4. Discussion 
On the basis of the results by [15] we expected that Japanese 
participants would generally perform well on the [(d)ʑi] – [zi] 
contrast, which our current results confirmed.  
We inquired into the discrimination of the [(d)ʑi] – [zi] contrast 
(containing a non-native sequence) in comparison to that of the 
[tɕi] – [ti] contrast (containing two native sequences, one only 
occuring in loanwords). Our results show that there was a 
significant effect of contrast on the participants’ accuracy on 
‘different’ experimental items, meaning that they performed 
better on the [tɕi] – [ti] contrast than on the  [(d)ʑi] – [zi] 
contrast. This is an important finding, because not only does it 
provide evidence for the observation that [ti] has been 
integrated into the Japanese phonotactic system and participants 
are better at distinguishing this than the non-integrated [zi], it 
also shows that our participants generally performed very well 
on the [(d)ʑi] – [zi] contrast, with 29 out of 39 participants 
scoring 90% or higher, though with a high amount of inter-
speaker variability. Considering how well our participants 
performed on the illegal sequence, it leads us to predict that 
loanwords with a faithful adaptation of [zi] are likely to appear 
in Japanese in the near future. 
The second hypothesis, that the degree of self-reported English 
input correlates with the performance in the discrimination, was 
not supported. As the interaction of accuracy and English input 
was not significant, we cannot confirm on the basis of our 
results that participants with higher English input scores are 
more sensitive to both contrasts. This result could be attributed 
to a number of explanations, the first one being that English 
input alone is not sufficient to account for Japanese speakers’ 
ability to perceive these contrasts, and that other variables, such 
as level of education or age, need to be included. Second, the 
system of scoring English input applied in the present study 
may have been oversimplified, in the sense that having spent 
two months in an English speaking country might have a 
different effect on a speakers’ performance than a weekly input 
of two hours.  
Taking an English proficiency test (as traditionally done in L2 
experiments, but not in experiments on loan adaptations) is a 
way to provide a more reliable judgment of participants’ 
knowledge in English, though this is of course also far more 
time consuming.  
5. Conclusions 
Our results provided evidence for the hypothesis that Japanese 
listeners are perceptually better at distinguishing the loan 
sequence [ti] than the foreign sequence [zi] from similar native 
sequences. Their performance for the latter is quite advanced, 
which leads us to the conclusion that perception is not only 
guided by existing native sequences, but also by illegal 
phonotactic sequences the speakers know from an L2. We 
therefore think it likely that the sequence [zi] is likely to enter 
the Japanese language via English loanwords in the near future.
