Enhancing Transcripts of Open-Source Automatic Speech Recognition Models
Through Fine-Tuning with Laughter and Speech-Laugh
Phuoc Hoang Ho1, Dragos, Alexandru B˘alan1, Dirk K. J. Heylen1, Khiet P. Truong1
1Human-Media Interaction, University of Twente, the Netherlands
hohoangphuoc@student.utwente.nl, {d.a.balan, d.k.j.heylen, k.p.truong}@utwente.nl
Abstract
Non-lexical sounds such as laughter are considered important
discourse markers in conversation as these sounds comple-
ment the lexical information in shaping interpersonal relations,
managing the conversation, and in expressing attitudes and af-
fect. However, general purpose open-source automatic speech
recognition (ASR) systems are typically not focusing on these
sounds. Laughter transcription is an understudied task in ASR:
laughter is often not modelled as a token and is discarded in
the evaluation of ASR. In our study, we investigate how current
open-source ASR models are handling laughter and speech-
laugh (speech interspersed with laughter). Using Switchboard
and BuckEye as conversational speech corpora, we fine-tuned
and evaluated Whisper and Wav2Vec2. Our results show that
laughter can be integrated in ASR transcriptions without sub-
stantially degrading word error rate.
Index Terms: automatic speech recognition, paralinguistics,
laughter transcription
1. Introduction
Non-lexical sounds such as backchannels, disfluencies (e.g.,
fillers, hesitations) and laughter are considered important dis-
course markers in conversation as these complement the lexical
information in turn-taking signaling, active listening, shaping
and managing interpersonal relations, and in expressing atti-
tudes and affect among others [1, 2]. Disfluencies and laughter
can also be indicative of cognitive impairment and Parkinson’s
disease [3, 4]. However, current general purpose open-source
automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems have trouble rec-
ognizing these non-lexical sounds correctly while sometimes
some non-lexical sounds are discarded altogether which is gen-
erally the case with laughter [5, 6, 7, 8]. Since laughter is such
an integral and important part of conversation, our aim is to in-
vestigate how the recognition of laughter can be integrated in
current ASR systems.
Many researchers of human conversation such as clinicians,
psychologists, legal persons, and conversational analysts rely
on manual transcriptions that give detailed information about
what has been said and how it has been said. ASR has the po-
tential to support these types of transcriptions but is currently
not doing this. With the exception of GailBot, which integrates
existing speech-to-text services with plugins [9], current ASR
systems do not produce Jefferson-style transcriptions typically
used in conversation analysis (capturing nonverbal features like
pause duration, laughter, and coughing) or verbatim transcrip-
tions (preserving words exactly as spoken, without grammatical
corrections and including laughter) [10]. Moreover, laughter
is considered a non-lexical token (NLT) in ASR evaluations of
conversational speech and is not part of the scoring (see e.g.,
NIST RT-09 Meeting Recognition Evaluation Plan [11], Ope-
nASR21 Challenge Evaluation Plan [12]).
Laughter can be expressed in isolation, separate from
speech, but is also sometimes interspersed with speech [13].
Speaking while laughing is referred to as speech-laugh. It is
largely unknown to what extent words with a laughing qual-
ity can still be correctly transcribed by ASR. Hence, we will
also investigate how well the speech, i.e., the words, in speech-
laughs are correctly transcribed by ASR systems in conversa-
tional speech.
In this paper, the aims are to investigate 1) to what ex-
tent laughter recognition can be integrated in open-source ASR
models without degrading word error rate (WER) in conver-
sational speech, and 2) how laughing interspersed with words
(speech-laughs) affects the transcription of those words. To that
end, we fine-tuned and subsequently evaluated two open-source
ASR models (Whisper and Wav2Vec2) using Switchboard [14]
and the Buckeye Corpus [15]. We describe related work in Sec-
tion 2 and the speech corpora used in Section 3. The method
and results are presented in Sections 4 and 5 respectively.
2. Related work
2.1. Laughter and speech-laugh
Prior work on laughter reveals that laughter is a highly acous-
tically variable signal that shows quite different acoustic char-
acteristics from speech [16, 17, 18]. Consequently, promising
results have been achieved for laughter classification, 91.5%
AUC [19], in the SSPNet Vocalization Corpus [20]. However,
laughter detection remains quite challenging in conversational
speech as [21] reports an F1 of 75.2% on Switchboard and [22]
reports an F1 of 47.91% on the Buckeye Corpus. As it will be
observed in Section 2.2, it remains largely unclear to what ex-
tent laughter recognition in conversational speech can be mod-
elled jointly with ASR without degrading WER.
The extent to which the words in speech-laughs can still be
transcribed by ASR systems also remains largely unknown. In
Nwokah et al. [13], around 20% of all laughs in infant-directed
speech were found to be co-occurring with speech. In their
study, they observe that neither speech nor laughter is domi-
nant when both are combined. F0 of the speech-laugh seems
to be determined by the frequency of speech but amplitude and
rhythm may be determined by the laughter. The speech-laugh
preserves some of the features of speech and laugh, but also
shows the emergence of new characteristics. These observa-
tions could partly explain why speech-laughs degrade the per-
formance of an i-vector based speaker recognition system [23],
and are expected to affect transcription accuracy as well. Hence,
in the current study, one of the aims is to evaluate the transcrip-
tion accuracy of speech-laughs.

2.2. ASR and non-lexical tokens
The integration of recognition of NLTs in an ASR framework
has gained more attention in recent years but has less often in-
volved laughter. Most of the studies that model NLTs in ASR,
address disfluent phenomena such as fillers [24, 25, 26], partial
words [24], hesitations [25], reparandum and interregnum parts
of disfluencies [27], or, non-disfluent short sounds in dialogue
such as backchannels [24]. Horii et al. [25] tagged fillers and
hesitations as # and @ and used these for training E2E mod-
els using a joint connectionist temporal classification (CTC)-
attention Transformer ASR model. Error rates were reduced
when disfluency labelling was applied. In Futami et al. [27],
Transformer-based encoder-decoder models were trained that
jointly address the speech recognition and disfluency detection
task while maintaining or even improving the WER. Similarly,
a study by Mihajlik et al. [24] demonstrated that WER was not
negatively affected when E2E ASR acoustic models were fine-
tuned on datasets with various labelling methods for disfluen-
cies, partial words, and backchannels. Fine-tuning was also ap-
plied in a study by Zusag et al. [26] to achieve timed detection
of filler events where it was also found that WER did not de-
grade with the addition of filler events. Overall, these studies
have shown that jointly modelling NLTs such as disfluencies
with (E2E) ASR models to achieve detection of these disfluen-
cies does not degrade WER. To what extent this also holds for
laughter is largely unknown.
The automatic recognition of laughter in conversation has
largely been approached as an acoustic event detection [21], so-
cial signal detection [20] or non-speech information captioning
task [28], rather than as part of an automated transcription task
(i.e., speech-to-text conversion), although there have been some
developments to unify these tasks [9, 29, 30]. Hori et al. [30] de-
veloped a meeting analyzer that monitors conversations in meet-
ings and that includes the detection of laughter as an acoustic
event that is modelled separately from the ASR module. Gail-
Bot [9] combines ASR services with plugins to generate con-
versation analysis transcripts with paralinguistic features such
as laughter. In contrast to the studies [30, 9] discussed so far,
Inaguma et al. [29] present a unified framework that does in-
tegrate the detection of laughter in an ASR framework. They
describe how social signal detection (which includes laughter,
fillers, backchannels, and disfluencies) and ASR can be inte-
grated in a unified framework based on connectionist temporal
classification, and show that this joint modelling does not de-
grade ASR performance.
As previous work has demonstrated that NLTs, such as dis-
fluencies and backchannels, can be integrated into ASR without
degrading performance, we can expect the same for laughter.
We will investigate how laughter transcription can be integrated
in state-of-the-art open-source ASR models such as Whisper
and Wav2Vec2.
3. Data
As data, we identified two conversational speech corpora that
have laughter (L) and speech-laugh (SL) annotations, and that
have sufficient data for our purpose.
3.1. Conversational speech corpora
For training and testing, we use the Switchboard Telephone
Speech corpus (SWB) which contains conversational speech
with over 500 speakers and over 250 hours of speech and text
(transcripts) [14]. Each original audio contains two channels
referring to 2 speakers of the same conversation, separated into
two audio files. The Switchboard transcripts include detailed
annotations for normal text and special conventions of sponta-
neous conversational speech. These annotations include speak-
ers’ turns, partial words, hesitations, interruptions, vocalized
sounds, such as laughter, etc. Within the laughter segments
of this corpus (i.e. audio segments that contain laughter), two
categories of tokens can be distinguished: laughter, marked as
[laughter], and speech-laugh (i.e., words interspersed with
laughter), marked as [laughter-word], where word de-
notes the actual word spoken.
For evaluation in a cross-corpus setting, we use the Buck-
Eye Speech Corpus [31].
This corpus is another conversa-
tional speech corpus which consists of a collection of spon-
taneous interviews from 40 speakers from the area of Central
Ohio in the US. It contains less audio than Switchboard which
makes it a more challenging and low-resource dataset to test
on. Within the laughter-related segments, laughter is denoted
using <LAUGH>, whereas speech-laugh words are denoted as
<LAUGH-word> where word denotes the actual word spoken.
3.2. Pre-processing
After a close investigation of each corpus, we noticed that sev-
eral segments are noisy or silent which would negatively im-
pact our experiments. Therefore, our pre-processing steps for
the datasets include reformatting by removing silence-only and
noise-only annotated sentences, as these correspond to non-
speech segments, and removing or modifying special word pat-
terns. Original audio recordings are resampled to 16kHz and
clipped into multiple segments, separated by timestamps, in cor-
responding transcripts. The segments are shorter than 30 sec-
onds to ensure compatibility with Whisper’s architecture.
As for text, we normalized the reference transcripts (REF)
and the predicted transcripts (HYP) produced by the different
models. The normalization includes expanding common En-
glish contractions, removing punctuations, trailing spaces, and
lowercasing the letters. We observed that disfluencies are anno-
tated differently across the corpus, so disfluency variations were
normalised to either “uh-huh” or “um-hum”.
To standardize the training and evaluation of laughter, we
normalize laughter tokens to <laugh> across both datasets.
We consider speech-laughs as pronunciation variants of the
same words spoken without laughter.
Therefore, we pre-
serve the word from [laughter-word] in Switchboard and
<LAUGH-word> in BuckEye. Then we apply the same nor-
malization techniques from the previous paragraph.
After pre-processing, Switchboard has been randomly split
into 3 sets: train, dev, and test, with a ratio of 70%, 10%,
and 20% respectively. This has been achieved using Hugging-
face’s Datasets API1. Since the amount of data is much smaller
in BuckEye, we decided to use the whole corpus as a test set
only. Table 1 displays the number of tokens per dataset.

sion of Wav2Vec 2.0 pre-trained on 60,000 hours of speech
from the Libri Vox dataset [32] and Whisper large-v2 [33].
For each pre-trained model, we have fine-tuned two versions
on the train and dev set of Switchboard: one with words

4.2. Experimental setup
In our experiments, we evaluated 3 different ASR models: no
fine-tuning (zero-shot, Z), fine-tuned without laughter (-L), and
fine-tuned with laughter (+L). As test data, we test on a dis-
tinct test set of Switchboard (within-corpus setting), and on the
full BuckEye corpus (cross-corpus setting) to see how well the
results transfer to a different corpus.
4.3. Metrics
To measure the ASR transcription performance, we use two
versions of WER: a) WER that includes laughter in the REF
and HYP transcripts, denoted by WER+L, and b) WER that ex-
cludes laughter in the REF and HYP transcripts, denoted by
WER-L - note that this is the “standard” WER metric that dis-
cards laughter. When calculating WER-L, all segments that con-
sist of laughter only are omitted.
To measure the performance of speech-laugh transcription
(i.e., transcribing the actual words in speech-laugh), we cal-
culate the number of correctly transcribed instances as a per-
centage of the total number of speech-laughs (recall). To take
the insertions into account as well, we used an adjusted WER,
the so-called Individual Word Error Rate (IWER) [7] that can
be calculated for subsets of words (instead of all words, as is
the case when calculating standard WER). As a baseline, we
also calculated recall and IWER for all the exact same words
in speech-laughs (SL) that were not spoken with laughter inter-
spersed (¬SL).

5. Results
5.1. Laughter
In Fig. 1, we can observe that with fine-tuning, WER+L de-
creases overall compared to zero-shot. As expected, ASR per-
formance is better when testing on SWB than on BuckEye,
since the models were fine-tuned on SWB. Comparing Whis-
per to Wav2Vec2, the differences are quite small. Comparing
-L to +L, we observe that adding laughter to the fine-tuning
process decreases WER+L performance slightly (as compared
to fine-tuning without laughter) - this decrease is more apparent
for Whisper than for Wav2Vec2.
More specifically, Table 2 presents both WER-L and WER+L
and corroborates the above observations. There is improvement
in transcribing the linguistic content (see WER-L) when models
are fine-tuned but the improvement is largest when laughter is
not added as a token. The effect of adding laughter transcription
on WER, however, in general is very small (see the difference
between WER-L and WER+L for models fine-tuned with laugh-
ter). It appears that the improvement in performance of the -L
models compared to the +L models can be attributed to the -L
models being more accurate in transcribing the linguistic con-
tent than the +L models. Additionally, the imbalance between
laughter tokens and the total word count prevents the inclusion
of laughter transcription from significantly contributing to WER
reduction.

5.2. Speech-laugh
The transcription of the words in speech-laughs is achieved with
recall rates (R) (in this case, recall refers to whether the words
were correctly transcribed or not) of 40.3%–70.9% and IWERs
of 32.8%–70.2% (see Table 4). Whisper in general performs
better than Wav2Vec2. Fine-tuning helps increase the perfor-
mance of transcribing speech-laughs (for Wav2Vec2, not for
Whisper) but not when laughter is added. Comparing the re-
call rates and IWERs between speech-laugh (SL) and the cor-
responding words without laughter interspersed (¬SL), we can
indeed observe that words interspersed with laughter are subs-
tiantially worse recognized than without laughter.

6. Discussion
In terms of performance, our laughter recognition results are in
line or even better than previous studies who have performed
laughter detection on SWB and BuckEye as well: Gillick et
al. [21] and Weninger et al. [22] achieved F1=75.2 and F1=47.9
on SWB and BuckEye respectively where we achieved F1=79.0
and F1=77.9 respectively. We do find that adding laughter as
a token to the fine-tuning somewhat decreases WER, which
is in contrast with other studies that jointly modelled NLTs in
(E2E) ASR models without degrading WER [27, 24, 26]. Sim-
ilarly, we see that the performance of transcribing speech-laugh
is also degraded with +L models. With precision values be-
tween 74.5% and 84.4% for laughter transcription, the intro-
duction of laughter in the +L models cause confusion lead-
ing to higher WERs. Further inspection reveals for example
that 1.6%–10.1% of all speech-laughs are erroneously confused
with laughter. These observations indicate that in our study,
the introduction of laughter in the fine-tuning process leads to
slightly worse WERs.
For future research, we propose to add more NLTs and to
investigate more models and methods for jointly modelling of
these NLTs in ASR models to improve NLT transcription per-
formance without degrading WER. In addition, fine-tuning and
evaluating on more and other conversational speech corpora is
needed to corroborate the current results.
7. Conclusion
We investigated to what extent laughter transcription can be in-
tegrated in Whisper and Wav2Vec2, and how the transcription
accuracy of the words in speech-laughs is affected by the laugh-
ter of the speech-laugh.
Through fine-tuning with laughter,
we found that laughter transcription can be integrated in Whis-
per and Wav2Vec2 without substantially degrading WER: fine-
tuning with laughter yielded WER+L between 11.1%–19.6%
while without laughter yielded WER+L between 8.5%–14.8%.
With respect to laughter recognition performance, Whisper per-
formed slightly better than Wav2Vec2 with F1 between 77.9%–
79.0%.
For speech-laugh transcription, we found that fine-
tuning increases the transcription accuracy of speech-laughs for
Wav2Vec2 but not for Whisper with recall rates ranging be-
tween 40.3%–68.0%. Additionally, fine-tuning with laughter
degraded speech-laugh transcription accuracy compared to fine-
tuning without laughter. These findings highlight the potential
and limitations of integrating laughter transcription into ASR
models, suggesting that while fine-tuning enables laughter tran-
scription it may come at the cost of the accuracy of word tran-
scription (including speech-laugh), warranting further research.
