Bridging Speech and Singing: Multi-stage Speech-Prompted Singing Voice
Conversion with Speaker Embedding Adaptation
Mingda Liu1, Jiatong Shi2
1School of Physical Science and Engineering, Tongji University, People’s Republic of China
2Language Technologies Institute, Carnegie Mellon University, The United States
liumingda@tongji.edu.cn, jiatongs@andrew.cmu.edu
Abstract
Singing voice conversion (SVC) technology holds immense
potential to transform the entertainment industry and advance
human-computer interaction. Unlike previous SVC studies that
primarily focus on the conversion between different singing
voices, this work specifically investigates the conversion of
speech timbre into singing. In this paper, we introduce a novel
Singing Speech Alignment Network (SSAN) designed to align
speaker and singer embeddings derived from input data. To ef-
fectively train the SVC model, we also propose a cycle train-
ing strategy that ensures the performance of SVC with speech
prompts. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed
method (SSANSVC) achieves superior performance in both the
naturalness of the synthesized singing and its similarity to the
target speech.

1. Introduction
Singing Voice Conversion (SVC) aims to transform the timbre
of a song’s singer into that of another singer while preserving
the content and melody of the singing. It not only holds tremen-
dous potential in the entertainment industry and content cre-
ation but also shows broad application prospects in fields such
as education and personalized voice generation [1].
SVC models are trained using either parallel or non-parallel
datasets.
The parallel dataset requires multiple singers per-
forming the same song, while the non-parallel dataset is more
flexible to involve multiple singers performing different songs.
Early efforts in SVC focus more on using parallel data. These
methods build the mapping of the parallel samples using statis-
tical techniques [2,3].
However, the scale of parallel datasets is limited due to
strict requirements, which has led to increasing attention on
methods that use non-parallel data for SVC. An unsupervised
deep learning SVC method based on WaveNet [4] is proposed,
incorporating a singer-agnostic latent representation embedding
to enhance the flexibility of the conversion [5]. To address the
issue of converted singing voices often going out of tune, a pitch
adversarial mechanism is proposed, which forces the encoder to
learn pitch-invariant phoneme representations from the source
audio.
Then, the extracted pitch information is further fed
into the decoder network, significantly improving the quality of
the converted singing voice [6]. In addition, Variational Auto-
Encoders (VAEs) [7] and Variational Autoencoding Wasserstein
Generative Adversarial Networks (VAW-GANs) [8] have also
been explored for non-parallel SVC.
Recently, diffusion models have emerged as a powerful
generative technique in the fields of image and audio synthe-
sis [9–13]. These models learn the data distribution by gradu-
ally introducing and removing noise to capture subtle features
in audio signals. Due to its remarkable ability to model com-
plex data distributions, it has become a promising alternative
for SVC tasks [14, 15]. A method based on a denoising dif-
fusion probabilistic model, DiffSVC, is proposed for singing
voice conversion. By using PPGs, fundamental frequency fea-
tures, and loudness features as auxiliary inputs, it significantly
improves the naturalness and timbre similarity of the converted
singing voice [14]. The performance of diffusion model-based
SVC methods is outstanding. However, the iterative sampling
process leads to slow inference speed. To mitigate the chal-
lenge, a consistency model-based SVC method, CoMoSVC, is
proposed to achieve high-speed sampling [15].
However, previous SVC research has generally focused on
the conversion between different singers’ voices, with little at-
tention given to the conversion of the speech of a target speaker
to singing.1 Speech signals emphasize clarity and intelligibility,
while singing is usually more diverse, often incorporating richer
emotional expression. Therefore, converting speech prompts to
singing requires careful modeling of the intricate details of tim-
bre. Additionally, datasets containing both speech and singing
from the same speaker are relatively scarce, which presents an
even greater challenge for research on speech-prompted SVC.
According to the above challenges in speech-prompted
SVC, we design a speaker-singer adaptation network (SSAN)
to bridge the gap between speaker embeddings (extracted
from speech) and singer embeddings (extracted from singing),
thereby enhancing the quality of timbre conversion from speech
to singing. Furthermore, we adapted a multi-stage cycle train-
ing strategy to further refine the performance of the proposed
method, namely SSANSVC. Experimental results demonstrate
that our approach outperforms baseline models in both objec-
tive evaluations and subjective metrics. The main contributions
of this work can be summarized as follows:
1. We design an SSAN to align speaker and singer em-
beddings from input audio clips. This network is specifically
tailored to bridge the inherent differences between speech and
singing data by capturing and adapting the unique characteris-
tics of each domain.
2. To address the issue of the scarcity of paired speech data
and singing data from the same speaker/singer, we propose a
multi-stage cycle training strategy to mitigate this challenge.
By adopting the strategy, we successfully achieve cross-domain
singing voice conversion, effectively utilizing the available un-
paired data.

2. Method
In this section, we first describe the SSAN to improve the
speaker embedding network for better transferability in the SVC
modeling. Next, we present the overall architecture of the SVC
model. Finally, we outline the proposed cycle training strategy
to enhance the SVC performance and improve timbre disentan-
glement.
2.1. Speaker-Singer Adaptation Network
As illustrated in Figure 1(a), the framework of the proposed
SSAN is composed of three modules, namely Embedding align
network, Speech reconstruction network, and Singing recon-
struction network. The architecture of the above three layers
is the same, as shown in Figure 1(b).
The Embedding align network network aligns the timbre
embeddings of speech and singing signals.
It captures sim-
ilarities within the same speaker or singer while distinguish-
ing differences between different ones. This helps preserve the
speaker’s unique characteristics while handling stylistic vari-
ations in singing.
The Speech reconstruction network and
Singing reconstruction network are responsible for restoring
timbre embeddings of the speech and singing signals processed
by the Embedding align network to their original feature repre-
sentations. This design helps prevent overfitting of the network,
ensuring that it does not deviate from the original characteris-
tics during training, thereby maintaining the key information of
the original data.
During training, speech trimbre embedding tspeech and
singing timbre embedding tsinging are first passed through the
Embedding align network, where tspeech and tsinging are extracted
by ESPnet-SPK [19].2 One sample embedding is selected from
each of the two datasets to form a speaker-singer timbre em-
bedding pair{tspeech, tsinging}. We assign -1 as target for mis-
matched speaker-singer timbre embedding pairs and 1 as tar-
get for matched timbre embedding pairs. Then, the timbre em-
bedding differences between the two training samples are com-
puted, and the predicted similarity value is obtained through
the similarity adaptation layer. Afterward, the aligned tspeech

2.2. SVC Model Architecture
Our method is based on the CoMoSVC teacher architec-
ture [15], where the main difference is to replace the tradi-
tional singer-id with the speaker timbre feature extracted from
the SSAN model proposed in Sec. 2.1. The proposed method is
named SSANSVC. Different from CoMoSVC, which requires
training on each specific target domain, SSANSVC enables
zero-shot capability, allowing the model to generalize to unseen
target timbres without requiring additional training. Similar to
CoMoSVC, the SSANSVC model consists of two parts, encod-
ing and decoding modules. For the encoding process, the model
encodes the timbre-related information and timbre-independent
features extracted from the signal (shown as S{Speech, Sing} in
Figure 2) into embedded feature representations, which are sub-
sequently concatenated and used as conditional inputs that are
passed to the decoding module to generate the mel-spectrogram.
Next, the generated mel-spectrogram is converted into an audio
signal using a pre-trained vocoder.
specifically, singing signals contain many components
mixed together such as content, pitch, loudness, and singer tim-
bre. During the encoding process, timbre-related and timbre-
independent features are extracted.
Timbre features are ob-
tained by the SSAN described in Sec. 2.1, shown as t{Speech, Sing}

Content features are extracted by using a pre-
trained acoustic model ContentVec [20]. Pitch information is
estimated using the widely used classical F0 estimator DIO
[21]. Loudness information is derived from the squared ampli-
tude of the signal. The content features, pitch information and
loudness information are shown as c{Speech, Sing} in Figure 2. Af-
ter feature extraction, a linear layer is applied to all embeddings
to standardize their dimensions, and they are concatenated to
form the conditional inputs for the decoding module. The de-
coding process remains the same as in [15].
2.3. Cycle Training
As discussed in Sec. 1, obtaining speech and singing data from
the same speaker is both expensive and challenging. A common
approach to this challenge is to combine speech and singing
data from different speakers/singers to create a mixed dataset
with speech and singing data. Content and timbre information
are then extracted from each dataset and used to reconstruct au-
dio data. However, since the content and timbre feature during
training are from the same speaker’s data, existing models strug-
gle to generate data where the content is derived from singing
data and the timbre from speech data. Inspired from [22], we
propose a cycle training strategy to address this limitation and
fully exploit the potential of a dataset that has both speech and
singing voices.

3.1. Experimental Setup
Dataset: Several datasets are adopted to train our model in dif-
ferent stages. We start by using NUS-48E Corpus [23] to train
the SSAN model, since NUS-48E provides the paired speech
and singing voice data.
For SVC model training, we use a
combination of singing voice and speech datasets, including
OpenSinger [24], ACE-KiSing [25] and Aishell-3 [26], to train
SSANSVC model. For evaluation, we utilize M4Singer [1] and
ST-CMDS [27] datasets to evaluate the zero-shot SVC capac-
ity with our proposed model. All audio samples are resampled
to 24 kHz and normalized. Then we calculate the volume fea-
ture, extract the F0 curve along with the voiced flag for each
frame by using DIO, the content feature from the 12th layer
by utilizing ContentVec (i.e., c{Speech, Sing} in Figure 2), and the
192-dimensional timbre feature (i.e., t{Speech, Sing} in Figure 2)
from Section 2.1. These features are respectively projected to
256 dimensions with a linear neural layer and then concate-
nated as the conditional input for the decoding stage. We use
the pre-trained singing voice vocoder released in the M4singer
work, and the mel-spectrograms are computed with 512-point
fast Fourier transform, 512-point window size, and 128-point
hop size with 80 frequency bins. Mel spectrograms are min-
max normalized to the range [-1, 1].
For the SSAN model, we use a Transformer-based en-
coder with six layers, each containing 8 attention heads, a 512-
dimensional feed-forward network, and a dimension of 192 for
attention calculation. Dropout is set to 0.1 to prevent overfit-
ting. The batch size is set to 16 and the learning rate is 3e-
6. We collected a validation set comprising both speech and
singing data from multiple singers as a validation set for the
SSAN model. Specifically, the dataset includes recordings from
4 singers, each contributing about 4 minutes of speech and 2
minutes of singing data. The detailed training process is pro-
vided in Algorithm 1. The training process is stopped when the

3.2. Evaluation
We evaluate the performance of different methods by subjec-
tive and objective metrics. For the subjective test, we invited
10 volunteers to give Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) on natural-
ness (MOS-n) and timbre similarity (MOS-ts) on the converted
audio. We use the 5-point Likert scale for testing with MOS-
n (1-bad, 2-poor, 3-fair, 4-good, 5-excellent). And we use the
3-point Liker scale for testing with MOS-ts (1-not match, 2-
normal, 3-match). We use VERSA to conduct objective evalua-
tions [29]. Specifically, we include Character Error Rate (CER)
obtained by Whisper [30] and speaker SIMilarity (SIM) calcu-
lated by the cosine distance between the speaker embeddings
of the generated audio and the target timbre audio are used as
the objective metrics for SVC evaluation. SingMOS [31] and
SHEET-SSQA MOS score [32] are used additionally for natu-
ralness evaluation.

4. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a novel network (SSAN) designed to
adapt speaker and singer embeddings. In addition, we design
a cycle training strategy for model optimization. This strategy
ensures that the network not only learns to convert the voice
from one domain (e.g., speech) to another (e.g., singing) but
also guarantees that the converted voice maintains consistency
with the original speaker’s identity and singing style. Through
comprehensive subjective and objective evaluations, we demon-
strate the effectiveness of the proposed method.
Though our proposed method achieves better performance,
it still requires further improvements. The primary area of en-
hancement lies in the loss function, where it is important to
not only consider mel-loss but also incorporate timbre loss and
lyrics recognition loss. By doing so, we can more effectively
capture the nuances of both the singer’s timbre and the correct
lyrical content during the conversion. Additionally, we plan to
explore a more efficient training approach by eliminating the
two-stage training method, and opting for a direct one-stage so-
lution. This could simplify the training process and potentially
improve the overall performance of the model.
