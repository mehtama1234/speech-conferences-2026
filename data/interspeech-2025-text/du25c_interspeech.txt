Modeling of Adductor Spasmodic and Primary Myotonic Dysphonia

Abstract
Dysphonia encompasses a broad spectrum of vocal dis-
orders with diverse etiologies, among which adductor spas-
modic dysphonia (ADSD) and primary muscle tension dys-
phonia (pMTD) are particularly challenging to diagnose. Cur-
rently, the primary diagnostic method relies on subjective audi-
tory perception by highly experienced clinicians. To alleviate
the scarcity of diagnostic resources, this study develops a deep
learning–based approach for automatically diagnosing ADSD
and pMTD using patients’ speech data. Our contributions are:
(1) designing a convolutional neural network (CNN)–based di-
agnostic model that leverages handcrafted features derived from
expert knowledge and (2) incorporating self-supervised learn-
ing (SSL) to extract more discriminative representations as in-
put from raw waveforms adaptively. This marks the first appli-
cation of deep learning techniques to ADSD and pMTD diag-
nostic modeling, achieving a classification accuracy of 83.3%
on our newly constructed dataset.
Index Terms: speech disorder classification, self-supervised
learning, diagnostic speech processing
1. Introduction
As is well known, accurate disease diagnosis is crucial for sub-
sequent treatment plans [1]. Misdiagnosis can lead to inappro-
priate treatments, potentially worsening the condition. How-
ever, adductor spasmodic dysphonia (ADSD) and primary mus-
cle tension dysphonia (pMTD) are often confused and misdiag-
nosed [2, 3, 4]. This is because the clinical diagnosis of these
two conditions primarily relies on subjective auditory percep-
tion [5], yet their acoustic features are very similar, making it
difficult for many non-specialist specialists to distinguish be-
tween them [6]. In this context, there is a growing demand for
automated diagnostic methods based on acoustic signals.
In recent years, research on the automatic differential diag-
nosis of ADSD and pMTD has gradually evolved from tradi-
tional acoustic parameter analysis to multidimensional feature
fusion and algorithm-driven approaches. Early studies based
on long-term average spectra (LTAS) spectral moment parame-
ters differentiated ADSD from pMTD, revealing the dispersed
low-frequency energy distribution characteristic of ADSD [7],
which belongs to steady-state speech segment analysis. Roy
[8] further found significant dynamic differences in fundamen-
tal frequency (F0) and glottal closure features between ADSD
patients during reading and conversation tasks, while pMTD
patients exhibited relatively stable features. Li [9] integrated
a creak recognition model that incorporates prosodic position,

(CCC-SLP) in the United States.
To address the challenge of differential diagnosis between
these two disorders, we first designed a method that employs a
CNN framework with multidimensional features from openS-
MILE [13]. Then, we integrated a CNN-based diagnosis model
with a self-supervised pre-trained feature extractor (such as
WavLM [14]). Additionally, considering that pre-trained mod-
els derived from different speech tasks may not be well-suited
for the current specific task, we incorporated adapter modules
into the self-supervised model to better adjust it for downstream
tasks. Extensive experiments and comprehensive comparisons
on a custom-built dataset validate the effectiveness of the CNN
model and SSL features in diagnosing pMTD and ADSD. The
contributions are summarized as follows:

2. Related Work
The differential diagnosis of ADSD and pMTD remains chal-
lenging. Early studies [6] pointed out that acoustic and aero-
dynamic parameters can be helpful in distinguishing ADSD,
MTD, and vocal cord tremor. Rees [12] demonstrated that ex-
perienced speech pathologists can identify voice disruptions, ir-
regular vertical striations, and retained resonance peaks through
spectral analysis, validating its potential for diagnosing dy-
namic phonatory patterns. Yan [15] proposed a spatiotempo-
ral analysis method using high-speed videoendoscopy, quanti-
fying the asymmetry and inhomogeneity of vocal cord vibration
patterns via Nyquist diagrams, along with glottal area wave-
form analysis, successfully distinguishing symmetric vibrations
in MTD from intermittent spasms in ADSD, providing a new
paradigm for the objective assessment of dynamic vibrational
patterns. Carroll [16] further explored aerodynamics and re-
vealed that ADSD patients show higher glottal peak pressure
and final airflow in /p/ syllable tasks, while pMTD patients,
especially those with pMTD, have significantly reduced final
airflow, providing a quantitative basis for the aerodynamic fea-
tures of these two diseases. Chen [17] used high-speed videoen-
doscopy (HSV) combined with digital kymography analysis to
reveal unique oscillatory breaks in the onset phase of vocal fold
vibration in ADSD patients, while MTD patients mainly ex-
hibited excessive vocal fold tension with sustained supraglottic
compression, providing a new method for visualizing and dis-
tinguishing vibratory patterns.
Other research has focused on exploring either ADSD or
pMTD individually. By evaluating the IINFV o perceptual scale
and AMPEX acoustic analysis, finding their complementary
roles helped identify ADSD-specific voice disruptions and fun-
damental frequency irregularities [18]. A work used spatiotem-
poral processing to quantify vocal fold vibration irregularities in
ADSD and distinguish them from neurological disorders [15].
Latest work finding that Maximum Phonation Time (MPT) ef-
fectively distinguishes SD subtypes, with ADSD patients hav-
ing significantly longer MPT than controls [19]. For MTD de-
tection, Past work shows that MTD patients exhibited posterior
glottal gaps, and MPT was reduced compared to other acous-
tic and analysis methods [3]. Another study found significant
correlations between laryngeal features and abnormal subglottic
pressure peaks, showing statistical significance [20]. One of the
latest efforts used random forest models and multivariate sta-
tistical analysis to reveal that the 15 acoustic and aerodynamic
parameters recommended by the American Speech-Language-
Hearing Association, overlap significantly when distinguishing
pMTD from other voice disorders, with a classification accu-
racy of only 65%. This indicates limitations in the traditional
acoustic parameters for characterizing the heterogeneity and
specificity of pMTD, suggesting the need for combining physi-
ological dynamics to improve diagnostic accuracy [21].
The automatic diagnosis of pathological voice disorders
is receiving increasing attention, particularly with the applica-
tion of deep learning algorithms in speech disorder classifica-
tion. A review pointed out that the trend of combining self-
supervised learning with acoustic features offers new perspec-
tives for speech pathology applications [22]. A study demon-
strated the advantages of the pre-trained Wav2Vec 2.0 model
for cross-database speech disorder detection[23]. Another study
proposed a hybrid model combining Mel frequency cepstral
coefficient and other acoustic features to enhance the robust-
ness of detection [24]. And improved classification accuracy
by combining CNN with transfer learning [25]. One of the lat-
est efforts presented a SSL approach that significantly improved
speech disorder detection efficiency and scalability with a small
amount of labeled data [26].
3. Dataset
The audio data in this study were collected from 80 participants
at the V oice Evaluation Room of the Eye and ENT Hospital of
Fudan University. The dataset consists of 40 ADSD patients
and 40 pMTD patients, with detailed demographic information
provided in Table 2. It is divided into three subsets: training,
validation, and evaluation, with statistical analysis conducted
on the number of sentences, speech duration, and gender dis-
tribution of participants. Additionally, the dataset includes the
total number of speech samples and gender distribution of the
participants. The study was approved by the Ethical Review
Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University
(IRB2021060).

4. Method
4.1. Framework overview
As shown in Figure 1, the proposed framework integrates ex-
pert knowledge-driven feature extraction with SSL to improve
the differential diagnosis of ADSD and pMTD. The architec-
ture consists of two parallel pathways: CNN-EK, which cap-
tures handcrafted features derived from domain expertise, and
CNN-SSL, which leverages self-supervised representations to
adaptively extract salient speech patterns.
In the CNN-EK branch, openSMILE extracts a compre-
hensive set of handcrafted acoustic features, which are subse-
quently processed through a series of one-dimensional convolu-
tional layers to capture temporal dependencies. Meanwhile, the
CNN-SSL branch directly operates on raw speech waveforms,
utilizing a pre-trained WavLM model with adapter modules to
refine feature representations for the specific classification task.
The extracted representations are further processed through ad-
ditional one-dimensional convolutional layers to enhance their
discriminative capability. By combining expert knowledge with
data-driven representation learning, this dual-branch framework
effectively balances interpretability and adaptability. The final
classification is achieved through a fully connected layer with a
Sigmoid activation function, ensuring robust prediction results.

4.2.2. CNN-SSL Module
In the CNN-SSL system, a self-supervised model based on
WavLM is adopted [28]. The model architecture consists of
a series of convolutional layers, feature projection layers, and
encoder layers designed for processing and classifying speech
data. It is initialized with a pre-trained WavLM feature encoder,
which extracts high-dimensional features directly from raw
speech waveforms. The feature extraction module comprises
a sequence of convolutional layers, each employing a GELU
activation function, along with normalization and dropout tech-
niques to refine layer outputs. The model includes seven con-
volutional layers, each designed to reduce dimensionality while
preserving key speech information.
Following feature extraction, the model employs an en-
coder with self-attention mechanisms and feedforward layers,
combined with position-based convolutional embeddings, to
maintain the sequential characteristics of speech data. Addition-
ally, adapter modules are incorporated to fine-tune each layer.
These adapters modify the learned features from the WavLM
model to better suit the specific classification task. The adapter
operates hierarchically, where higher layers are fine-tuned using
progressively narrower task-specific adapters, ensuring optimal
feature customization for downstream tasks.
After adaptation, the feature maps are further refined us-
ing a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN),
enabling the model to capture temporal dependencies and ex-
tract discriminative patterns required for classification. This ap-
proach effectively harnesses the power of SSL while minimiz-
ing the dependence on large labeled datasets, thereby enhancing
model performance in low-resource scenarios.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
5.1.1. Baselines
To compare with previous work, we select the following models
as baselines:
• LTAS [7] Using the spectral matrix as feature input and com-
3545
pleting classification with a logistic regression classifier.
• CSID [8] Using the spectral matrix as a feature input and
completing the classification with a logistic regression clas-
sifier.
• Creak% [9] Using vocal fry as the sole feature, combined
with a threshold classifier to complete the classification task.
5.1.2. Evaluation Metrics and Strategy
We evaluate the performance of the model using three widely
recognized metrics: accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity.These
metrics offer a thorough evaluation of the model’s performance,
with sensitivity and specificity highlighting how effectively the
model identifies positive and negative cases in binary classifica-
tion tasks. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and
test sets with a 0.7:0.2:0.1 ratio. To ensure reliable results, the
mean and standard deviation of test performance are reported in
multiple runs.
5.2. Experimental Results
In our proposed CNN-SSL model, we conducted an ablation
study on four mainstream speech pre-trained models: Whis-
per [29], SpeechTokenizer [30], Wav2Vec2 [31], and WavLM
[14], as shown in Table 3. WavLM achieved the best per-
formance across all three metrics, with an accuracy of 0.833
and a sensitivity of 0.875, representing a relative improvement
of 20.9% and 20.7%, respectively, compared to the baseline
model Whisper. Notably, while WavLM has a larger parame-
ter size, the smaller Wav2Vec2 model still achieved an accu-
racy of 0.779, outperforming SpeechTokenizer and Whisper by
7.7% and 13.1%, respectively. Therefore, WavLM is more suit-
able for the classification of ADSD and pMTD. Consequently,
we selected WavLM as the pre-trained model for the CNN-SSL
framework in subsequent experiments.

6. Conclusion
This study developed a deep learning-based method for diag-
nosing ADSD and pMTD from speech data, addressing the lim-
itations of traditional methods that use a limited set of features
and shallow classifiers. Specifically, both CNN-EK and CNN-
SSL showed excellent performance on a real-world dataset. Ab-
lation experiments showed that the CNN-SSL model outper-
formed CNN-EK, with adapter modules further improving the
performance. Finally, the five-fold cross-validation confirmed
that the model outperforms traditional methods in diagnostic
accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity, demonstrating its potential
to enhance dysphonia diagnosis.
