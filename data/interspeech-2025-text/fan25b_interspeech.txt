Creaky voice facilitates more efficient phonological processing of Mandarin 
Tone 3

Abstract 
Though tone recognition is primarily influenced by pitch cues, 
phonation type may also play a role. For example, creaky voice, 
a non-modal phonation, can enhance Mandarin Tone 3 (T3) 
recognition. While most studies explore creaky voice in tone 
recognition behaviorally, its neural processing remains unclear. 
This study aims to investigate tone processing using 
electroencephalography to examine how the brain integrates 
pitch and creaky voice to process T3. We measured Mismatch 
Negativity (MMN) responses of 10 native Mandarin speakers 
exposed to an oddball paradigm with real-word single syllable 
stimuli in two conditions: creak and non-creak. Results showed 
stronger MMN responses in the creak condition, with larger 
mean MMN amplitude, shorter peak MMN latency, and larger 
negative peak amplitude. These findings suggest that creaky 
voice enhances T3 distinctiveness and facilitates more efficient 
phonological 
processing, 
contributing 
to 
a 
deeper 
understanding of tone processing mechanisms.

1. Introduction 
While pitch is the dominant cue in lexical tone recognition [1, 
2], other factors, such as phonation type [3, 4], also play a role. 
Phonation refers to the sound generated as the vocal folds 
vibrate during speech production. Variation in the way the 
vocal folds vibrate leads to differences in phonation that can be 
generally categorized into two types: modal phonation and non-
modal phonation. Modal phonation, the most common and 
default voice quality, is characterized by regular, periodic 
vibrations of the vocal folds. In contrast, non-modal phonation 
includes various complex patterns of vocal fold vibration that 
differ from modal phonation, including irregular or aperiodic 
vibration [5]. Creaky voice, a type of non-modal phonation, is 
often marked by this irregular vibration [6]. It typically co-
occurs with specific tones and serves as an additional cue in 
tone recognition, even in tonal languages without phonological 
phonation contrasts, such as Mandarin [3, 7, 8, 9]. Despite its 
relevance, most research on tone recognition has focused on 
pitch-based cues, with limited attention to the role of phonation 
[1, 10, 11]. This study aims to provide deeper insights into the 
mechanisms underlying tone recognition by examining how 
tone is processed when non-modal phonation cues such as 
creaky voice are introduced.  
As a tone language, Mandarin has four distinct tones: Tone 
1 (T1) is a high-level tone, Tone 2 (T2) is a rising tone, Tone 3 
(T3) is a low-dipping tone, and Tone 4 (T4) is a falling tone 
[12]. Among the four tones, creaky voice can occur at the 
lowest portion of T2, T3, and T4, specifically, at the onset of 
T2, the turning point of T3, and the offset of T4 [8]. Notably, 
creaky voice is most frequently associated with T3. Since 
Mandarin tones are primarily distinguished by pitch, phonation 
contrasts are not a defining feature. Consequently, phonological 
studies suggest that creaky voice functions merely as a 
redundant cue in Mandarin tone [7]. However, perception 
studies indicate that creaky voice enhances both the speed and 
accuracy of T3 recognition, while it does not provide similar 
benefits for other tones, regardless of whether natural or 
resynthesized stimuli are used. For instance, Belotel-Grenié and 
Grenié [13] found that listeners recognized T3 more quickly 
when it was realized with creaky voice in a gating task. 
Similarly, Huang [3] superimposed tone contours for each of 
the Mandarin tones T1-T4 onto T1 syllables and added creaky 
voice to the resynthesized stimuli, reporting that creaky voice 
improved T3 recognition but hindered recognition of T1 and T4.  
Moreover, distinguishing T3 from T2 based solely on pitch 
can be challenging due to the acoustic similarity of their pitch 
contours [14, 15]. However, research on the role of phonation 
cues on Mandarin tone perception suggests that listeners rely 
more heavily on creaky voice for recognition of T3. Notably, 
Yang [16] found that T3 was more easily recognized in a tone 
continuum generated from a T3 syllable than from other-tone 
syllables, provided that syllables of all four tones maintained 
their original phonation cues, specifically, the T3 syllable 
retained creaky voice, and other-tone syllables maintained 
modal phonation cues. Likewise, Way [17] found that T2 was 
more likely to be perceived as T3 when creakiness was more 
pronounced in the middle portion of T2, leading to higher 
incorrect recognition of T2. Given these findings, creaky voice 
would seem not to be a redundant cue but rather a useful cue for 
T3 recognition.  
Little is known about the neural processing of creaky voice 
in tone recognition. Most studies on Mandarin tone processing 
have focused on pitch cues. For instance, Chandrasekaran [18] 
used an oddball paradigm in an electroencephalographic (EEG) 
study to investigate Mandarin tone processing of pitch 
dimensions (e.g., pitch height and pitch contour), finding that 
T3 was more difficult to distinguish from T2 than from T1 when 
relying solely on pitch cues. Similarly, Li and Chen [19] 
suggested that pitch alone may be insufficient for reliably 
differentiating T3 from T2. However, despite behavioral 
evidence highlighting the importance of creaky voice in T3

discrimination, it remains unclear how the brain integrates pitch

Mismatch Negativity (MMN) has been widely used in 
speech research to reflect the neural response to changes in 
acoustic cues [20]. Three key measures are commonly used to 
evaluate the robustness and efficiency of MMN response: (1) 
mean MMN amplitude, which reflects the overall strength of a 
neural response; (2) peak MMN latency, which indicates the 
timing of deviance detection; and (3) peak MMN amplitude, 
which provides insights into the efficiency of neural processing 
of stimulus deviations. In general, as the magnitude of stimulus 
deviation increases, neural activation intensifies, leading to a 
larger mean MMN amplitude, a shorter peak MMN latency, and 
a larger negative peak MMN amplitude [20]. In addition, the 
MMN scalp distribution can offer insights into which types of 
information are processed: the right hemisphere is typically 
associated with acoustic information encoding, while the left 
hemisphere is more specialized for encoding linguistic 
information [21, 22].  
In this study, we measured MMN responses to T3 with and 
without creaky voice, aiming to answer the following questions: 
(a) How does the brain integrate pitch and creaky voice to 
distinguish T3 from T2?  (b) At which linguistic level, acoustic 
or phonological, does creaky voice facilitate T3 processing? We 
predicted that (1) the presence of creaky would enhance T3 
distinctiveness, evoking an earlier and stronger MMN; (2) 
Creaky voice would enhance phonological encoding at the early 
stage of T3 processing, showing an early bilateral scalp 
distribution of MMN responses. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Ten native Mandarin-speaking participants (20-28 years old; 8 
females, 2 males) currently living in Australia took part in the 
experiment. All participants were born and raised in China and 
were either university students or had obtained a bachelor’s 
degree. They were all right-handed and had normal hearing 
thresholds between 250 Hz and 8000 Hz. None reported any 
language difficulties, neurological impairments, or a history of 
serious head injury. The study was approved by Macquarie 
University’s Human Ethics Committee. All participants 
provided written informed consent before the experiment began 
and received compensation upon completion. The experiment 
was conducted in the EEG lab at Macquarie University. 
2.2. Design 
In this study, we used the T2-T3 contrast, in which T3 had two 
variants: creak and non-creak. We employed the passive 
oddball paradigm which is widely used to elicit MMN. In this 
paradigm, a sequence of repetitive standard stimuli is 
occasionally interrupted by a rare deviant stimulus. MMN is 
triggered by the brain’s detection of the changes introduced by 
the deviant relative to the standard. We adopted this approach 
to measure MMNs in two conditions: a creak condition and a 
non-creak condition. In the creak condition, creak T3 served as 
the deviant stimulus, while non-creak T2 acted as the standard 
stimulus. In the non-creak condition, non-creak T3 was the 
deviant, with non-creak T2 again as the standard. Each 
condition included 700 trials, comprising 600 standard stimuli 
(86%) and 100 deviant stimuli (14%). EEG was used to collect 
data due to its high temporal resolution, enabling the capture of 
neural activity on a millisecond scale essential for studying 
rapid auditory processing. 
2.3. Stimuli 
The target stimuli consisted of two monosyllabic CV Mandarin 
words distinguished only by lexical tones: /ja2/ (‘tooth’) and 
/ja3/ (‘dumb’). For /ja3/ (‘dumb’), there were two variants: 
creak /ja3/ and non-creak /ja3/. All stimuli were resynthesized.   
2.3.1. Tone resynthesis 
Three monosyllabic CV words /ma/, /ja/, and /wa/ produced 
with all four tones were used to fit the tone contours. The target 
tone contours were T2 and T3, while T1 and T4 contours were 
used to ensure proper distribution of T2 and T3 within the 
speaker’s natural pitch range. A native Mandarin male speaker 
(23 years old) uttered each word in isolation. Each word was 
repeated 15 times. Recordings were made using TotalMix FX 
audio processing software with a high-quality Neumann 
TLM103 microphone at a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz in a 
sound-attenuated room at Macquarie University Phonetics Lab.  
Each tone category consisted of 45 tokens (3 words * 15 
repetitions). For each token, thirteen F0 points were extracted 
from its pitch track at equal time intervals using Praat [23]. 
Based on the F0 values, four tone contours were fitted using 
second-order polynomial models in R [24], as shown in 
Equation 1 to 4. The fitted T2 and T3 contours were then 
applied to the word /ja1/ to create /ja2/ and /ja3/ using the pitch 
synchronous overlap-add (PSOLA) method in Praat [23].

2.3.2. Creak resynthesis 
The creak /ja3/ pitch contour was derived from the 
resynthesized non-creak /ja3/ by introducing irregular F0 
serving as the primary cue for a jittered “creak” portion using 
Praat [23].  F0 values in the creak portion ranged from 60 Hz to 
105 Hz. Specifically, an irregular F0 was inserted at 33.3% of 
the total syllable duration, covering approximately one-third of 
the entire syllable [3].  
All stimuli were rated by native Mandarin-speaking 
listeners to confirm their naturalness. Of these, the recognition 
accuracy of /ja2/ was 97.5%, /ja3/ was 79.2%, and creak /ja3/ 
was 100%. The duration of all the stimuli was 400 ms, and the 
amplitude was scaled to 70 dB. 
2.4. Procedure 
Participants watched a self-selected movie with the sound 
muted while passively listening to the auditory stimuli 
presented at 70 dB via a loudspeaker. They were seated on a 
sofa approximately 1 meter from the screen and 0.8 meters from 
the loudspeaker. To minimize distractions, they were instructed 
to avoid extraneous movements. Chinese subtitles were 
provided to help maintain their focus on the movie. At the end

of the experiment, they were required to provide a brief 
summary of the movie to confirm their engagement. 
The order of conditions was counterbalanced across 
participants. Within each condition, stimuli were presented in a 
pseudorandomized sequence, ensuring that each deviant 
stimulus was preceded by at least two standard stimuli. The 
inter-trial interval ranged from 600 to 800 ms, and each 
condition began with 10 extra standard stimuli. 
2.5. EEG data acquisition and analysis 
The EEG signals were recorded via a SynAmps 2 amplifier 
(NeuroScan Inc.) with a 64-channel EASYCAP (EASYCAP 
GmbH, Germany) carrying Ag/AgCl electrodes placed 
according to the international 10–20 system. Vertical eye blinks 
and horizontal eye movements were monitored using electrodes 
placed above and below the right eye (VEOG) and at the outer 
canthi of both eyes (HEOG). The EEG was recorded with an 
online reference to the left mastoid. All electrode impedance 
levels were kept below 5 kΩ. The signals were continuously 
recorded and digitized at a 24-bit resolution with a sampling 
rate of 1000 Hz, using a bandpass filter of 0.05 Hz to 400 Hz. 
Pre-processing of EEG signals was implemented using 
fieldtrip [25]. The raw EEG data were first re-referenced to the 
mastoids and then filtered using a 0.5 – 30 Hz band-pass filter. 
Eye movement and blink artifacts were corrected using 
independent component analysis (ICA). The corrected data 
were segmented into epochs from -200 ms to 600 ms relative to 
the word onset. For each epoch, there was a time window of 
200 ms preceding the onset of the words serving as baseline 
correction. Epochs with amplitudes exceeding ±75 μV or ocular 
artifacts were excluded from further analyses. The mean 
acceptance rate of epochs was above 80% in each condition. 
 MMN was calculated by subtracting the standard event-
related potential (ERP) waveform from the deviant ERP 
waveform. MMN was identified through visual inspection of 
the grand-average MMN and its corresponding topographic 
distribution maps. Additionally, 15 fronto-central electrodes, 
where MMN amplitudes were expected to peak, were chosen 
for analysis: F1, F3, FC1, FC3, C1 and C3 on the left 
hemisphere, Fz, FCz and Cz located along the midline, and F2, 
F4, FC2, FC4, C2 and C4 on the right hemisphere. The grand-
average MMN refers to the averaged MMN waveform across 
all electrodes of interest and all participants. Their 
corresponding topographic maps were plotted in 20ms 
intervals, with the last subfigure plotted according to the actual 
residual duration. Mean MMN amplitude was calculated by 
averaging the ERP signal across trials at an electrode within a 
specific time window. Peak MMN latency was defined as the 
time point of the largest negative peak of an electrode within 
the time window, while peak MMN amplitude was the 
corresponding voltage at this peak. 
Linear mixed-effect models were performed to evaluate 
mean MMN amplitude, peak MMN latency, and peak MMN 
amplitude across conditions using the lmerTest package [26] in 
R [24]. All models were fitted with maximal random structures 
permissible by the data without singularity or convergence 
issues and were subsequently simplified and selected based on 
a likelihood ratio test to identify the best-fitting model for each 
analysis [27] (see below for details of final model structure). If 
a significant main effect of a multilevel factor or a significant 
interaction was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were 
conducted using the emmeans package [28].

4. Discussion  
The current study investigated how the brain integrates pitch 
and creaky voice to distinguish T3 from T2. Our findings 
supported the predictions that creaky voice would enhance the 
distinctiveness of T3, leading to stronger neural responses and 
more efficient phonological processing. Specifically, we 
observed a larger mean MMN amplitude, a shorter peak MMN 
latency, and a larger negative peak MMN amplitude in the creak 
condition, along with a bilateral scalp distribution during the 
earlier stage of processing. These results suggest that creaky 
voice facilitates T3 processing by enhancing its distinctiveness, 
leading to more efficient phonological encoding. 
Our results offer several important theoretical implications. 
First, they provide further neural evidence that creaky voice is 
a useful cue for T3 processing supporting behavioural studies 
[3, 13, 16]. Creak T3 elicited both a larger and faster MMN 
compared to non-creak T3, highlighting that creaky voice 
enhances T3 distinctiveness and speeds up neural processing. A 
larger negative peak MMN amplitude evoked by creak T3 
further indicates the enhancement of efficiency in T3 
processing, supporting previous studies that the larger the 
stimulus deviation, the more robust and efficient the processing 
[20].   
Second, creak T3 may engage both acoustic and 
phonological encoding early on, whereas non-creak T3 
primarily involves acoustic processing. MMN topography 
showed a bilateral distribution in the creak condition during 
early processing, while the non-creak condition showed right-
hemisphere dominance. These findings suggest that creaky 
voice may facilitate access to phonological representations 
during tone processing. These interpretations are consistent 
with neuroimaging findings that the right hemisphere processes 
general acoustic information, such as pitch, while the left 
hemisphere specializes in linguistic processing, including 
phonological processing [21]. However, since the current 
statistical analysis did not include processing time as a factor, 
these interpretations remain tentative. Future research should 
adopt time-sensitive methods to confirm that these neural 
processing differences are distinct in brain areas.  
Third, our findings challenge a purely pitch-centric view of 
tone processing by emphasizing the significant role of creaky 
voice in tone recognition. The current study highlights the 
importance of incorporating non-pitch cues into our 
understanding of tone recognition, particularly in languages 
where pitch is traditionally considered to be the primary cue.  
Finally, while the current study provides valuable insights 
into the contribution of creaky voice to tone processing, further 
research is needed to determine whether creaky voice truly 
enhances phonological encoding, particularly when accounting 
for language experience. Additionally, further studies should 
investigate how creaky voice functions in non-word contexts, 
where linguistic experience has less influence. This would help 
clarify the role of creaky voice in tone processing in the absence 
of familiar linguistic context. 
5. Conclusions 
This study investigated how creaky voice contributes to 
Mandarin T3 processing.  The results show that creaky voice 
enhances the distinctiveness of T3, eliciting a stronger MMN 
with a larger mean MMN amplitude, an earlier MMN peak, a 
more negative peak MMN amplitude, and a bilateral 
distribution in the early stages. These preliminary findings 
suggest

contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the 
cognitive mechanisms underlying tone recognition.
