Pathology-Aware Speech Encoding and Data Augmentation for
Dysarthric Speech Recognition
Ilja Baumann1, Dominik Wagner1, Korbinian Riedhammer1, Tobias Bocklet1
1Technische Hochschule N¨urnberg Georg Simon Ohm, Germany

Abstract
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) for pathologic speech
remains a major challenge due to high variability in articu-
lation, phonation, and prosody distortions. In this work, we
propose a pathology-aware speech encoder based on BEST-RQ
pre-training, which incorporates 46k hours of speech, including
pathologic and atypical speech. We continue pre-training for
domain adaptation and experiment with etiology-specific code-
books. We achieve a 13.2% relative word error rate (WER)
improvement using the pathology-aware speech encoder with
etiology-specific continued pre-training. Additionally, we ex-
amine the impact of incorporating synthetic and out-of-domain
(OOD) data to further enhance ASR performance. Synthetic
data reduces WER by up to 8.7%, while OOD data improves
WER by 12.2%. Finally, we introduce a semantic similarity-
based data augmentation technique to optimize data selection,
achieving a WER improvement of up to 9.7% while minimizing
the need for additional training data.
Index Terms: pathologic speech recognition, speech augmen-
tation, self-supervised learning
1. Introduction
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) has made significant
progress, yet recognizing pathological speech remains a ma-
jor challenge due to high variability in articulation, phonation,
and prosody distortions. Limited annotated data further exacer-
bates this issue, making it difficult for ASR models to general-
ize across different speech impairments. To address these chal-
lenges, we introduce a pathology-aware speech encoder trained
using BEST-RQ [1] and investigate data augmentation strate-
gies to improve ASR performance. We pre-train a conformer
[2] model using BEST-RQ with speech data incorporating both
pathologic and healthy speech. We continue pre-training us-
ing only pathologic speech and explore etiology-specific code-
books. Additionally, we investigate whether adding more syn-
thetic and OOD data consistently improves ASR performance.
Our study also introduces a semantic similarity-based data aug-
mentation approach, which enhances model training by select-
ing optimal training samples from OOD datasets. By combin-
ing these approaches, we aim to improve the robustness and
accuracy of ASR systems for pathologic speech. Our findings
contribute to the broader field of inclusive speech technology.

1.1. Related work
Continued pre-training on domain-specific data has been shown
to enhance performance in specialized settings such as medi-
cal transcription [3] and accented speech recognition [4]. Hsu
et al. [5] found that continued pre-training on in-domain data
helps the HuBERT model capture better phonetic representa-
tions for low-resource languages. Early approaches to dataset
augmentation in ASR primarily involved modifying raw audio
signals. Techniques such as time stretching, pitch shifting, and
noise addition have been widely adopted to enhance model ro-
bustness. Ko et al. [6] introduced SpecAugment, a method that
applies time warping, frequency masking, and time masking to
spectrogram representations, significantly improving ASR per-
formance without requiring additional labeled data. Another
area of research has focused on generating synthetic speech to
increase data diversity. Jia et al. [7] demonstrated the effec-
tiveness of text-to-speech (TTS) models in augmenting training
datasets, particularly for low-resource languages. Furthermore,
Kim et al. [8] explored the use of voice conversion techniques to
synthesize speech with varying speaker characteristics, improv-
ing ASR generalization. In context of pathologic speech, sev-
eral works [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] evaluated data augmentation
techniques, including input signal modification and generative
adversarial networks (GANs) [15].
2. Data
We use the Speech Accessibility Project 2 2024-11-30 dataset,
which comprises a train and dev set, from 374 speakers and
56 speakers, respectively. In further use, we denote the dataset
as SAPC. Speech recordings of five etiologies amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis (ALS), cerebral palsy (CP), Down syndrome
(DS), Parkinson’s Disease (PD) and stroke (SK) are part of the
dataset. The most dominant etiology is PD with ∼74%, fol-
lowed by ALS with∼14%, CP with∼6%, DS with∼4% and
SK with∼2%. The challenge evaluation is performed on two
non-publicly available test sets, test1 and test2, consisting of
7601 and 8043 utterances. We create a separate dev set using
10% of the train speakers for model selection and report the
results on the original dev set throughout this work.
Word error rate (WER) and semantic score (SemScore) are
the two evaluation measures used in our experiments. The Sem-
Score is a weighted combination of the BERT score [16], pho-
netic distance3, and natural language inference probability [17].
The metrics used in this work are computed using the provided
template by the challenge hosts4.

3. Method
3.1. Pathology-aware speech encoder
BERT-based Speech pre-Training with Random-projection
Quantizer (BEST-RQ) [1] is a self-supervised learning method
for automatic speech recognition (ASR). The core concept in-
volves utilizing a random-projection quantizer (RQ) to convert
speech signals into discrete labels, which serve as training tar-
gets. The RQ applies a fixed projection matrix to map input
features onto a vector aligned with the codebook dimension k.
Given an input speech signal X, latent representations are ex-
tracted using the convolutional frontend: H = fθ(X), where
each feature vector ht lies in a d-dimensional space. To intro-
duce target projection, a fixed, randomly initialized projection
matrix W ∈ Rd×k, where k < d, is used to obtain the pro-
jected features Z = HW . The most similar codebook entry
is then identified using cosine similarity, and its index is used
as the target label. The RQ operates as a non-trainable trans-
formation that maps high-dimensional speech representations
into a lower-dimensional space. Following [18, 19] we employ
multiple codebooks during pre-training to stabilize the training.
The speech encoder is composed of 12 stacked conformer lay-
ers, each incorporating multi-head self-attention with 8 atten-
tion heads. Cosine similarity between projected features and
codebook entries is used to select the target index. We use
cross-entropy and auxiliary Kullback-Leibler divergence loss
proposed in [19]. Following [18], we use a multi-codebook
setup with 16 equally weighted codebooks in pre-training.
3.1.1. Continued pre-training
To further adapt the pre-trained speech encoder to pathologic
speech, we continue pre-training in two ways. Initially, using
only SAPC data and equally weighted codebooks following the
preceding setup. Subsequently, we experiment with etiology-
specific codebooks to determine if speech variations associ-
ated with different pathologies can be effectively captured. For
this, we employ only the first five codebooks for each etiology
and discard the remaining in continued training. During pre-
training, the loss for each sample is weighted based on its cor-
responding etiology. A primary weight wp is assigned to the
codebook specific to the sample’s etiology, while a secondary
weightws is applied to the remaining codebook losses. To en-
sure a stronger focus on etiology-specific representations, we
enforce the constraint wp≥ 6ws. This weighting strategy pri-
oritizes learning from pathology-relevant codebooks while still
allowing generalization across different etiologies. For both
pre-training setups, we use AdamW [20] (ϵ = 10 −9,β1 = 0.9,
β2 = 0.98) optimizer and maintain the same learning rate of
2× 10−4 without warmup, matching the final learning rate used
in the multi-corpus pretraining.

include 1k hours of children’s speech, as both children and
individuals with speech impairments share high acoustic vari-
ability, weaker articulation, and altered phonation, resembling
characteristics of neurodegenerative conditions (e.g., ALS, PD).

3.2. Synthetic data augmentation
We enhance the dataset using XTTS [35] in a zero-shot setting,
generating speech from reference audio. Only samples ≥15s
are selected to ensure sufficient reference speech. A time scal-
ing factorα∼U (0.7, 1.0) sampled from a uniform distribution
was used to simulate the slower speech rates occurring in patho-
logic speech. Transcripts of the SAPC train set are employed as
prompt for generation.
3.3. Out-of-domain data augmentation
Unlike synthetic data, which demands computational resources
even for zero-shot generation, we expand the fine-tuning dataset
beyond SAPC data by incorporating OOD datasets. Specifi-
cally, we utilize widely employed datasets such as LibriSpeech
[36], Common V oice [37], and People’s Speech [38]. For our
experiments, we select an equal number of samples from each
dataset, a maximum of ∼66k samples of each dataset. Addi-
tionally, we investigate the impact of OOD data on fine-tuning
by using varying portions of LibriSpeech, Common V oice, and
People’s Speech.
3.3.1. Semantic similarity data augmentation
Instead of randomly selecting OOD speech samples, we employ
a semantic similarity-based approach using Sentence Trans-
former [39, 40] embeddings. For each unique transcript in the
SAPC dataset, we identify the most semantically similar and
the most dissimilar utterances from the OOD datasets. The se-
lection process relies on cosine similarity scores computed be-
tween the embedding representations of the transcripts. During
fine-tuning, we construct training triplets by selecting the clos-
est and farthest OOD utterances: (1) the SAPC utterance as the
anchor a, (2) the most similar OOD utterance as the positive p,
and (3) the most dissimilar OOD utterance as the negative n.
The additional loss is calculated on the mean embeddings from
the speech encoder and can be formulated as:
Lsem = (1− cos(a, p)) + (1 + cos( a, n)) (1)
Since not every transcript in the SAPC dataset has a closely
matching OOD utterance, we introduce an additional weight
based on the cosine similarity of the transcript embeddings.
This way, not appropriately matching OOD utterances are less
weighted. The overall proposed setup is illustrated in Figure 1.

4. Experiments
All experiments share the same fine-tuning setup. Fine-tuning is
performed by adding a two layer bidirectional LSTM [41, 42]
on top of the encoder. The learning rate is set to 3× 10−4,
AdamW (ϵ = 10 −9, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.98) is used as opti-
mizer. We employ connectionist temporal classification (CTC)
[43] objective and fine-tune all models for 30 epochs. Best
model is selected based on the WER of our dev set.
4.1. Baselines
We begin by utilizing widely adopted pre-trained speech en-
coders, including wav2vec 2.0 [44], HuBERT [5], and BEST-
RQ [19], which we fine-tune on the SAPC dataset to estab-
lish our baselines. All models are used in their self-supervised
pre-trained versions, ensuring direct comparability as they fall
within a similar parameter range. Table 1 presents the baseline
results. Among them, wav2vec 2.0 performs the worst over-
all, while BEST-RQ achieves the best fine-tuning results, with
a WER of 22.73 and a SemScore of 83.01. However, a more
detailed analysis across different etiologies reveals that while
BEST-RQ is superior for PD, HuBERT outperforms the other
models across all remaining etiologies.
4.2. Pathology-aware speech encoder
Building on the baseline experiments, we pre-train a pathology-
aware speech encoder using BEST-RQ. We choose BEST-RQ
for its simpler training process, avoiding the multi-stage pre-
training and clustering required by HuBERT, while ensuring
greater stability than wav2vec 2.0. We first pre-train the speech
encoder on SAPC data, assigning a separate codebook per eti-
ology, following the feature-level clustering approach in [19].
As shown in Table 2, this pre-training approach leads to lower
WER and SemScore compared to the same model pre-trained
on LibriSpeech in Table 1. To enhance performance, we pre-
train the speech encoder on a diverse 46k-hour dataset, de-
scribed in Section 3.1.2. The results in Table 2 show that the
multi-corpus pre-trained speech encoder (base-MC) achieves an
11.8% relative WER improvement over the baseline model.
4.2.1. Effect of continued pre-training
To examine continued pre-training for domain adaptation, we
refine the 46k-hour pre-trained model by further training on
SAPC data for 20k additional steps. We compare two setups:
(1) shared codebooks, following the initial pre-training, and
(2) etiology-specific codebooks for targeted adaptation. Ta-
ble 2 presents the results for both, continued pre-training and
etiology-specific codebook pre-training. Continued pre-training
with shared codebooks does not show a significant positive or
negative effect on the general performance metrics. However,
incorporating etiology-specific codebooks during pre-training
yields the best WER and SemScore results compared to the
shared codebooks setup. Using etiology-specific codebooks
in continued pre-training (base-ESC) not only improves over-
all performance — achieving a 13.2% relative WER reduction
— but also leads to notable improvements for the ALS and CP
etiologies. Given its superior performance, we select the base-
ESC model for subsequent experiments.
4.3. Synthetic data augmentation
We expand the dataset by generating XTTS synthetic speech
using SAPC reference audio, at 50% to 100% of the training
set. While XTTS improves WER, it is computationally inten-
sive. As shown in Table 3, 50% synthetic augmentation reduces
WER by 8.7%, benefiting underrepresented etiologies (ALS,
CP). However, exceeding 100% synthetic data leads to dimin-
ishing returns, likely due to artifacts affecting generalization.
4.4. Out-of-domain augmentation
We evaluate OOD data from LibriSpeech, Common V oice, and
People’s Speech for fine-tuning, optimizing augmentation ratios
for ASR performance. As shown in Table 3, OOD data consis-
tently improves generalization, with the best results at 150%
augmentation, reducing WER to 17.32% and increasing Sem-
Score to 86.12. However, excessive OOD data slightly degrades
performance, especially for DS, emphasizing the need for care-
ful data selection to match pathological speech characteristics.
4.5. Semantic similarity augmentation
To identify the most similar and dissimilar utterances from
OOD datasets, we leverage Sentence Transformer (ST) embed-
dings, extracted from transcripts. The selected utterances ac-
count for an additional∼29% of the SAPC dataset. For embed-
ding extraction, we use the pre-trained all-MiniLM-L6-v2
model across all transcripts. We begin with a na ¨ıve approach,
where each SAPC utterance is paired with its most similar OOD
counterpart in training batches (OOD-b) without further modifi-
cations. Next, we introduce an additional cosine similarity loss
between the embeddings of a SAPC utterance and its most sim-
ilar OOD utterance (OOD-bl). This loss is further weighted by
the similarity score computed from the ST embeddings (OOD-
blw). Inspired by triplet loss, we extend the approach by incor-
porating the most dissimilar utterance alongside the most simi-
lar one (OOD-t). Finally, we apply per triplet of samples a loss
weighted by the transcript similarity score (OOD-tw).
Table 3 presents the results of fine-tuning with semantically
selected utterances from OOD datasets. The na ¨ıve approach
without an additional loss yields diminishing returns. Introduc-
ing an embedding loss (OOD-bl) between only positive pairs re-
sults in a 9.1% relative WER improvement. Weighting the loss
by transcript cosine similarities (OOD-blw) leads to a slight but
consistent WER improvement, from 17.93% to 17.81%, across
almost all etiologies. In contrast, using unweighted triplets with
an additional embedding loss (OOD-t) performs worse than
positive pairs alone, and applying weighting (OOD-tw) does
not yield significant gains. The training process suggests that
the harder training objective may require longer fine-tuning to
surpass the performance of positive pairs alone.
5. Discussion
This study demonstrates that pre-training a speech encoder on
diverse datasets significantly improves ASR performance for
pathological speech. By leveraging a pathology-aware speech
encoder with BEST-RQ and incorporating multiple data aug-
mentation strategies, we effectively mitigate the challenges
posed by high speech variability and data scarcity.
Our findings highlight the effectiveness of etiology-specific
codebooks in capturing distinct speech patterns associated with
different pathologies. This approach led to a 13.2% relative
WER improvement compared to baseline models, confirming
that targeted representation learning enhances ASR accuracy for
dysarthric speech. While continued pre-training using SAPC
alone did not significantly impact performance, the integration
of etiology-specific codebooks proved beneficial, particularly

Data augmentation emerged as a crucial factor in improving
ASR generalization, with both synthetic and OOD data playing
a key role. Using XTTS-generated speech provided an 8.7% rel-
ative WER reduction. However, our results indicate diminish-
ing returns when the synthetic-to-real data ratio exceeds 100%,
likely due to the introduction of artifacts that affect model gen-
eralization. Incorporating speech from external datasets such
as LibriSpeech, Common V oice, and People’s Speech improved
WER by 12.2% at an optimal augmentation ratio of 150%. Se-
lecting OOD utterances based on Sentence Transformer embed-
dings further refined augmentation by prioritizing semantically
similar data. This method resulted in a 9.7% relative WER im-
provement, demonstrating that quality selection of augmenta-
tion data is more impactful than simply increasing dataset size.
Despite these advancements, our study identified areas that
require further investigation. Unlike other etiologies, DS speech
did not benefit from augmentation techniques at all. Only the
etiology-specific pre-training improved the results. Future re-
search could analyze phonetic and acoustic properties of DS
speech to develop specialized augmentation strategies. The im-
pact of extended fine-tuning on harder training objectives, such
as weighted triplet loss, remains unexplored and requires further
research.
6. Conclusion
We introduced a pathology-aware speech encoder utilizing
BEST-RQ pre-training and evaluated multiple data augmenta-
tion techniques to improve ASR performance for pathologic
speech. Our results demonstrate that etiology-specific code-
books effectively enhance recognition accuracy, yielding an
overall 13.2% improvement in WER compared to baseline. This
underscores the importance of explicitly modeling pathology-
specific variations in speech processing. Additionally, we found
that synthetic data augmentation significantly reduces WER,
particularly when used in moderate amounts, while excessive
reliance on synthetic data can lead to diminishing returns. Out-
of-domain data augmentation further improved ASR general-
ization, with the best performance observed with most out-
of-domain data. The semantic similarity-based augmentation
strategy also contributed positively, demonstrating that targeted
transcript selection enhances fine-tuning efficiency.
