A Neural Codec Approach for Noise-Robust Bandwidth Expansion
Xi Liu1, Mu Yang1, Szu-Jui Chen1, John H.L. Hansen1
1Center for Robust Speech Systems (CRSS), The University of Texas at Dallas, USA
Xi.Liu@utdallas.edu, Mu.Yang@utdallas.edu, Szu-Jui.Chen@utdallas.edu,
john.hansen@utdallas.edu
Abstract
Neural audio codec technology has recently attracted sig-
nificant attention in various speech processing tasks due to its
efficient quantized latent features. In this work, we introduce
a novel approach that leverages a pre-trained neural codec net-
work to perform both speech denoising and bandwidth expan-
sion simultaneously. Specifically, we design a conformer-based
deep neural network to predict clean codebook indices, which
are then used by the pre-trained audio codec model to generate
enhanced and bandwidth-expanded audio. We investigated sev-
eral strategies for generating the clean indices and compared
our approach with state-of-the-art methods on the Valentini-
Botinhao noisy test set. Experimental results demonstrate that
our method achieves performance comparable to leading ap-
proaches in noise-robust bandwidth expansion tasks while of-
fering promising improvements in the quality and intelligibility
of narrow-band signals. Audio samples are available.1
Index Terms: speech enhancement, bandwidth expansion, neu-
ral codec
1. Introduction
Audio bandwidth expansion (BWE), also known as audio super-
resolution, is a technique designed to restore or generate the
high-frequency components of an audio signal that have been
lost or were never recorded, typically in narrow-band record-
ings (such as traditional telephone speech). The primary goal
is to enhance the perceived quality and naturalness of the voice
by extending its effective frequency range. Narrow-band au-
dio generally covers frequencies up to about 3.4 kHz, and the
absence of higher-frequency information can make the voice
sound muffled and less intelligible. In addition, many histor-
ical recordings suffer from background noise that further de-
grades speech quality. In this work, we propose a noise-robust
bandwidth expansion approach that not only recovers the miss-
ing high-frequency content but also effectively suppresses back-
ground noise.
Early BWE methods employed signal processing algo-
rithms such as linear predictive coding (LPC)[1] or rule-based
spectral mapping[2] to restore the high frequency details. How-
ever, these approaches rely on linear or heuristic models that
are not well-equipped to capture the complex, nonlinear rela-
tionships present in natural speech, often resulting in subopti-
mal reconstruction of the high-frequency components. Recent
approaches leverage deep neural networks (DNNs) to achieve
remarkable performance due to their powerful nonlinear mod-
eling capabilities.

DNN-based bandwidth expansion (BWE) approaches can
be broadly classified into three categories: methods operating
in the Mel-spectrum domain, those working in the linear spec-
tral domain, and those applied directly in the waveform domain.
Mel-spectrum domain methods [3, 4, 5] aim to reconstruct the
Mel-scale spectrum of wide-band or full-band speech and then
convert the enhanced Mel-spectrum into an audio waveform us-
ing a neural vocoder [6]. These methods are computationally
efficient because of using fewer frequency features compared
to processing all frequency bins. However, the Mel-scale repre-
sentation has lower frequency resolution in the high-frequency
range, which inevitably leads to a loss of fine details. Further-
more, since the Mel-spectrum only captures amplitude infor-
mation without phase components, these approaches are less
effective in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions, often re-
sulting in speech distortion and artifacts in the high-frequency
bands. To address these issues, SWiBE [7] and AERO [8] uti-
lize the complex spectrum as the input feature to better recon-
struct high-frequency components. Specifically, AERO incor-
porates Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to generate
higher-quality speech, while SWiBE employs a stochastic De-
noising Diffusion Process (DDP) to achieve notable improve-
ments. On the other hand, [9] proposed a waveform-domain
approach for BWE using a DDP model, and Lin et al. [10] de-
signed a two-stage network that jointly processes narrow-band
signals in both the frequency and time domains.
In contrast to methods that use spectral or waveform fea-
tures, Genhancer [11] leverages quantized latent representa-
tions produced by a Neural Audio Codec (NAC) for generative
speech enhancement. Specifically, it predicts clean codebook
indices from noisy ones using MaskGIT and a language model
(LM). NAC [12, 13, 14] compresses audio into discrete tokens
through residual vector quantization (RVQ) and an encoder-
decoder architecture, achieving a high compression rate without
losing critical audio information. This technique has also been
successfully applied to language model-based speech synthesis
tasks such as Text-To-Speech (TTS) [15, 16].
Inspired by previous work, we propose a novel approach for
noise-robust bandwidth expansion. Our method employs a sep-
arate model that uses continuous embeddings as input features
to predict clean discrete tokens (i.e. clean codebook indices).
These estimated tokens are then used by a pre-trained Neural
Audio Codec (NAC) to generate the clean audio waveform. The
main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1) We present
a NAC based method on noise-robust bandwidth expansion. 2)
We conduct a comprehensive study on the performance of clean
token prediction using different types of embeddings and train-
ing strategies.

2. Method
2.1. Method Overview
The system overview is presented in Figure 1. In this work, we
focus on recovering a clean, high-resolution (HR) speech signal
(sampled at 16 kHz) from a low-resolution (LR) speech signal
(sampled at 8 kHz) that is degraded by background noise. To
achieve this, we employ a pre-trained Descript Audio Codec
(DAC)[14] as our audio waveform generator, which operates on
16 kHz audio. We propose a simple, Conformer-based clean to-
ken predictor [17] to generate predicted clean tokens (i.e., clean
codebook indices). These predicted tokens are then used to pro-
duce quantized embeddings that are fed into the DAC Decoder
to synthesize the reconstructed HR audio.
2.1.1. Descript Audio Codec
DAC is a high-fidelity audio compression model that converts
audio waveforms to low-bitrate discrete tokens while preserving
critical speech quality. Audio codecs are typically used for ef-
ficient signal transmission, but recently researchers discovered
that the discrete token is more efficient than continuous latent
representations on various downstream tasks such as language
modeling and speech synthesis. Like other neural audio codec
approaches, DAC comprises three major components: an En-
coder module, a Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) module,
and a Decoder module.
The Encoder consists of a series of 1D convolutional layers
that downscale the waveform to a lower rate, while the Decoder
is trained to reconstruct the original audio from quantized em-
beddings. The core of DAC is the RVQ module, which is made
up of N vector quantization processing block corresponding to
N different codebooks. Similar to VQ-VAE [18], RVQ is a
multi-stage process that iteratively quantizes the original signal
and its residual error, with each stage refining the approxima-
tion. By breaking the quantization task into smaller, successive
approximations, RVQ strikes a balance between compression
rate and signal fidelity.
Because the convolutional encoder-decoder model directly
processes waveforms, it may struggle to capture fine frequency
details, particularly in the high-frequency range. To address
this, DAC employs multi-scale STFT discriminators and a Mel-
scale loss to alleviate aliasing artifacts and frequency-domain
distortions, potentially making it well-suited for bandwidth ex-
pansion tasks. In this work, we use the discrete tokens from N

2.1.2. Parallel clean token prediction
Considering the hierarchical structure of RVQ, the model quan-
tizes and generates audio in a coarse-to-fine manner, as il-
lustrated in Figure 2. As the number of residual codebooks
increases, the reconstructed speech—encompassing content,
speaker identity, and harmonic structure—becomes progres-
sively more recognizable and intelligible. Consequently, some
speech synthesis methods based on speech language models
(SLMs) [15, 16] adopt an auto-regressive (AR) approach to pre-
dict target tokens, since each subsequent residual token strongly
depends on the current one. In contrast, our approach predicts
the clean token in a parallel manner without relying on any SLM
or textual information. We believe that, given sufficient prior
knowledge, the model can learn the hierarchical structure effec-
tively. Our clean token predictor consists of two components: A
multi-layer Conformer block comprising M Conformer layers

To identify the optimal input features for our predictor,

quantized embeddings generated by DAC at various stages (see

the coarse-to-fine structure of RVQ, leveraging the insight that
the first codebook plays a crucial role in shaping overall per-
formance. Detailed training setup and comparative results are
discussed in Sections 3 and 4.
3. Experiments
3.1. Dataset
3.1.1. DNS-challenge wide-band dataset
The DNS-challenge[19] dataset has been widely used for var-
ious DNN-based speech enhancement tasks and noise-robust
speech technology. The clean speech training set is about 760
hours and contains over 10k speakers and 11 different lan-
guages. The noise dataset is collected from several resources
and has total 181 hours and 150 classes. In this study, we only
use DNS-challenge wide-band dataset for training, it contains
about 160 hours high quality read speech in English and 50
hours noise clips.
3.1.2. Valentini-Botinhao dataset
The Valentini-Botinhao dataset[20] is another popular dataset
for speech processing tasks under noise conditions. This En-
glish corpus comprising 28 speakers for training and 2 speakers
for testing. The training set features 40 distinct noisy condi-
tions, while the test set includes recordings under 20 additional
noisy conditions. Since the original audio is sampled at 48 kHz,
we down-sampled it to 16 kHz for this task. In our experiments,
we use only the noisy test set to evaluate speech quality im-
provements.
3.2. Experimental setup
We use a pre-trained DAC network2 trained on clean speech and
music data at a 16 kHz sampling rate, which compresses a one-
second audio clip into 50 frames. The total number of code-
books N used in RVQ is 12. Our clean token prediction model
incorporates a Conformer block implemented in PyTorch, con-
sisting of 8 layers with 8 attention heads. The depthwise convo-
lution within the Conformer block uses a kernel size of 15, and
2https://github.com/descriptinc/descript-audio-codec
the feedforward network is configured with 1024 hidden dimen-
sions. The model features a multi-head linear block composed
of two linear layers separated by a ReLU nonlinear activation
function and followed by a LayerNorm layer. The final linear
layer projects the 1024-dimensional hidden state into a 12*1024
output, yielding 12 separate predictions with 1024 classes each.
During training, a dropout rate of 0.1 is applied, and the model
is optimized using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learn-
ing rate of 0.0003. All layer weights are initialized using the
Xavier initializer.
To achieve noise-robust bandwidth expansion, we adopt a
two-stage training strategy. In the first stage, the predictor is
pre-trained solely on the noise suppression task. In the second
stage, it is fine-tuned on a joint task that combines noise sup-
pression with bandwidth expansion. Although the training tar-
get remains the same across both stages, the input data differs:
during stage one, the input is only corrupted by background
noise; during stage two, the noisy input is downsampled to 8
kHz and then upsampled back to 16 kHz using sinc interpola-
tion. Additionally, paired training data is synthesized on the fly
during each training iteration. In every training loop, we gener-
ate 24,000 clips with 2 seconds duration, and the signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) of the synthesized noisy data is randomly sampled
from -5 dB to 20 dB. The progressive training method described
in Section 2.1.2 is structured as follows: Epochs 1–10: Train
the model to predict codebook 1; Epochs 11–20: Extand train-
ing to predict codebooks 1–4; Epochs 21–30: Extend prediction
to codebooks 1–6; Epochs 31–40: Further train on codebooks
1–8; Remaining epochs: Train the model to predict all code-
books (1–12).

4.2. Training strategy comparison
We investigated several training strategies using the same model
architecture to examine the impact of different input features
and training methods on performance. Table 2 compares three
approaches:
• Quantized: In this approach, we use quantized embeddings
as the input feature to predict clean tokens.
• End2End Here, we forgo the two-stage training method
(where the model is first pre-trained on a denoising task)
and instead directly use downsampled noisy speech to pre-
dict high-resolution clean speech tokens.
• Progressive: In this method, we still use pre-quantized em-
beddings and a two-stage training strategy; however, the
model is trained progressively, as described in Section 3.2.
According to Table 2, model performance significantly
drops when the two-stage training method is omitted. This in-
dicates that jointly training noise suppression and bandwidth
expansion using a simple prediction model is more challenging
and may struggle to converge to an optimal solution. In other
words, It also implys pre-training on noise suppression provides
a solid foundation for extending the model’s ability to perform
bandwidth expansion. Because theoretically, bandwidth expan-
sion rely on a relative clean speech component in low frequency
range. Another key observation is that pre-quantized (or con-
tinuous) embeddings outperform quantized embeddings for the
speech enhancement task in our work. This suggests that, al-

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a neural codec approach for noise-
robust bandwidth expansion that recovers high-resolution audio
from low-resolution input under noisy conditions. Our experi-
mental results demonstrate that our method outperforms other
state-of-the-art techniques. Furthermore, our study on training
strategies shows that a noise suppression pre-training process
significantly benefits the bandwidth expansion task in noisy en-
vironments. Moreover, token prediction accuracy further con-
firms that the first codebook is the most critical component in
neural codec-based speech generation. Future research will fo-
cus on incorporating semantic tokens to further enhance speech
quality and mitigate speech suppression in low SNR scenarios.
