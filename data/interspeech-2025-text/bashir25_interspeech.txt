Intelligibility Prediction for Time-Modified Speech Signals Using
Spectro-Temporal Modulation Features

Abstract
Reference-based speech intelligibility prediction algo-
rithms (RB-SIPAs) are limited to speech degradations that
maintain the time alignment between the clean and degraded
speech signals. We address this limitation by augmenting the
existing RB-SIPAs framework with time alignment. To achieve
robust time alignment at low signal-to-noise ratios, we pro-
pose using spectro-temporal modulations (STM) of the speech
signals for dynamic time warping (DTW). Our experiments
demonstrate that in DTW, the use of specific STM compo-
nents as features for clean and time-modifieddegraded speech
achieves the baseline time alignment obtained using clean
speech and noise-free time-modified speech. Moreover, we pro-
pose two methods to incorporate time alignment into existing
RB-SIPAs. Using these methods, we compare the output scores
of the RB-SIPA with listening test scores and show better cor-
relation results using the STM features as compared to MFCCs.
Index Terms: speech intelligibility prediction, dynamic time
warping, spectro-temporal modulation
1. Introduction
Speech intelligibility prediction (SIP) involves predicting the
degree to which normal hearing listeners identify a spoken
message. SIP algorithms (SIPAs) are designed to replace time-
consuming and costly listening tests for assessing speech intel-
ligibility (SI) under various degradation conditions. Reference-
based SIP algorithms (RB-SIPAs) typically entail measuring
the similarity between a clean reference signal and the corre-
sponding degraded signal, by comparing features crucial for un-
derstanding speech. Although effective for assessing SI, exist-
ing RB-SIPAs [1–4] assume time alignment of the input sig-
nals. This limits their utility, as the time dimension of the de-
graded speech signal may have been modified to suit application
needs, such as pre-degradation speech enhancement (SE) [5,6],
speaking style change [7], etc. On the other hand, reference-
free SIPAs (RF-SIPAs) require only the degraded speech sig-
nal to generate SIP [8]. However, RB-SIPAs and RF-SIPAs are
complementary. For instance, RB-SIPAs have been used to la-
bel degraded signals for training RF-SIP deep neural networks
(DNNs) [9]. RB-SIPAs have also been used as loss measures
for training SE algorithms [10]. Since SE DNNs are capable of
altering aspects of spoken speech such as making phoneme or
speaker identity substitution [11], the lack of a reference signal
makes it harder for RF-SIPAs to identify signals with fake con-
stituents. Therefore, RB-SIPAs that could allow variable time
scales would provide additional flexibility for innovating such
algorithms. In this paper, we devise a method to extend the ap-
plication of RB-SIPAs to degraded speech signals with time
scale modified relative to the clean reference signal. This is
achieved by: (I) estimating the unknown time-scale change in-
curred by the degraded signal and (II) incorporating the estimate
to modify the RB-SIPA processing steps.
For (I), we estimate the time-scale change between the
clean reference signal and the degraded signal, using dynamic
time warping (DTW) [12,13]. DTW finds a correspondence (see
“alignment path” defined in Section 2.2.1) between the time
frames of the clean and degraded signal by matching the most
similar time frames of the two signals. However, in the case of
highly degraded speech, finding an accurate alignment path be-
comes challenging. Traditional audio features, such as the mel
frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) [14], perceptual lin-
ear prediction (PLP) [15,16], and linear predictive coding coef-
ficients (LPCCs) [17] degrade with decreasing signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR), making it challenging for DTW to find an accu-
rate alignment path [18–22]. In King et. al [23], a probabilistic
latent component analysis (PLCA) framework is used to model
spectrograms as probability distributions and learn speech com-
ponents for DTW using expectation-maximization. PLCA fea-
tures for DTW have shown better alignment accuracy compared
to MFCCs, PLP and LPCCs. However, the method relies on
expectation-maximization, making it sensitive to initialization.
Moreover, the learned components do not have a clear corre-
spondence with perceptually meaningful speech attributes. In
this paper, we introduce spectro-temporal modulation (STM)
analysis (see Section 2.1) to identify and isolate perceptually
interpretable speech components that demonstrate better align-
ment accuracy compared to MFCCs for various types of noise
and SNR levels.
For (II), we propose two methods to incorporate time align-
ment into existing RB-SIPAs: (a) use the estimated alignment
path to align the speech signal spectral sequences calculated by
the RB-SIPA, without changing the rest of the RB-SIPA, and
(b) in addition to (a), average over the intermediate spectral
similarity scores across time indices informed by the alignment
path to form the aggregate score output by the RB-SIPA. We
demonstrate the generality and efficacy of the proposed method
by applying it to four existing RB-SIPAs: Short-Term Objective
Intelligibility (STOI) [1], Extended STOI (ESTOI) [2], Spectro-
Temporal Glimpsing Index (STGI) [3], and Weighted Spectro-
Temporal Modulation Index (WSTMI) [4].
2. Proposed Method
2.1. Spectro-temporal Modulation Analysis
The STM analysis framework provides a computational model
for central auditory processing [24,25]. The model includes two
processing stages: (I) calculating log mel spectrogram, and (II)
STM decomposition. In the second stage, a Gabor STM filter
bank, representing the collection of spectro-temporal receptive

fields, is applied to the log mel spectrogram. Each filter in the
filter bank is tuned to a specific spectral and temporal modu-
lation frequency. Together, these filters span a range of STM
frequencies that are germane to auditory perception [4, 26].
In this study, we use S = 11 spectral modulation frequen-
cies and R = 4 temporal modulation frequencies (see Table 2
in [27]) to generateU =S×R = 44 STM frequency channels,
i.e., STM-filtered spectrograms that we analyze as features for
DTW alignment. The choice of STM frequencies is motivated
by prior studies [4, 28] on salience of certain STM channels to
SI. Our aim is to determine a subset of the U STM frequency
channels that gives the best DTW alignment accuracy for vari-
ous types of noise. For this purpose, each of the U STM chan-
nels is used as a distinct feature representation for finding DTW
alignment path. Based on this initial analysis, we combine the
best performing STM channels and evaluate their joint impact
on DTW alignment accuracy.

2.3.1. Simple-SIP
After performing the time alignment of the TF-features of
the RB-SIPA using Algorithm 1, RB-SIPA calculates similar-
ity scores between the time-aligned TF-features Xa [fs,t ] and
Ya [fs,t ]. Both TF-features and similarity scores are specific to
an RB-SIPA. The time dimension of the aligned TF features
is preserved. Simple-SIP retains the RB-SIPA’s original way of
calculating and combining intermediate similarity scores to pro-
duce the SI prediction output.
2.3.2. Mean-SIP
As with Simple-SIP, Mean-SIP calculates the similarity scores
between the time-aligned TF-features. Next, Mean-SIP aver-
ages the similarity scores across the indices of the alignment
path that are repeated due to time scale modification. As a re-
sult, repeated frames are represented by a single average score.
After this step, Mean-SIP continues as in the original RB-SIPA.
3. Experiments
We divide our experiments into two sections: (I) Experiment-I
analyzes STM channels as features for DTW under noise con-
ditions from the UrbanSound8k dataset [31], (II) Experiment-II
analyzes the impact of time alignment produced by DTW using
STM features identified in Experiment-I, on SIP performance
using Simple-SIP and Mean-SIP for Hurricane Challenge-I
(HC-I) dataset [5].
3.1. Datasets
3.1.1. Hurricane Challenge-I (HC-I)
The Hurricane Challenge-I [5] was designed to evaluate the per-
formance of various near-end listening enhancement (NELE)
algorithms aimed at increasing SI while listening in noisy en-
vironments. These pre-degradation SE algorithms were evalu-
ated using listening test scores for SI. The dataset consists of
clean speech and corresponding degraded speech produced by
two types of additive noise at three different SNRs: (I) compet-
ing speech (CS) at -7 dB, -14 dB and -21 dB, and (II) speech-
shaped noise (SSN) at 1 dB, -4 dB and -9 dB. For this study, we
only test the ten NELE algorithms that modified the time scale
of the clean speech signal before adding noise. This provides
2× 3× 10 = 60 mean SI scores for the RB-SIPA evaluation.
3.1.2. UrbanSound8k
UrbanSound8k dataset [31] contains urban sounds from ten
classes: air conditioner, car horn, dog bark, children playing,
drilling, engine idling, gun shot, jackhammer, siren, and street
music. Each of these ten types of noise is used to evaluate DTW
performance by adding the noise to the clean speech signals
from HC-I at 3 different SNRs: -5 dB, -10 dB and -15 dB.
3.2. Experiment-I
Experiment-I analyzes STM channels as features for DTW by
comparing clean paths with degraded paths for ten types
of noise from UrbanSound8k [31]. We compare two alignment
paths by finding their PA within the thresholdτ = 2 which cor-
responds to 25 ms using the window size of 25 ms with 50%
hop-length (see Section 2.2.2).

3.3. Experiment-II
Experiment-II involves using STM channels identified in Sec-
tion 3.2 to find degraded pathsand incorporating them into
RB-SIPAs to study the impact of alignment on SIP performance
using Simple-SIP and Mean-SIP.
3.3.1. Evaluation Metrics
Simple-SIP and Mean-SIP are used to find SIP scores for four
existing RB-SIPAs (see Section 1) which are compared with lis-
tening test SI scores in HC-I, using five metrics: (I) Pearson cor-
relation coefficient (Pear), (II) Spearman rank correlation coef-
ficient (Spear) (III) Concordance correlation coefficient (CCC),
(IV) Root mean square error (RMSE) and (V) Kendall’s corre-
lation coefficient (Ken).

4. Discussion
The experimental results show that the range of STM chan-
nels important for the time alignment of speech signals overlaps
with those essential for SI, i.e., spectral modulation frequen-
cies below∼ 4 cyc/kMel and temporal modulation frequencies
below∼ 8 Hz [4, 28]. This range of spectral modulaton fre-
quencies is occupied by energy associated with the resonants
frequencies of the vocal tract, i.e., formants, which are crucial
for differentiating vowels and some consonant sounds. Also,
the temporal modulation frequencies below 8 Hz lies within
the temporal modulations range corresponding to the conversa-
tional rate of syllables, at around 2 to 6 Hz [32,33]. The regions
within these modulation frequency ranges over which they dis-
agree comprise spectral frequencies below ∼ 0.2 cyc/kMel
and temporal frequencies below∼ 2 Hz. This is evident given
that the regions of low spectro-temporal modulation frequen-
cies lack the spectro-temporal details to help DTW find an
accurate alignment path. Therefore, the STM channels salient
for SI but lack sufficient spectro-temporal details are ineffec-
tive for precise speech alignment. Thus, for time alignment, our
scheme deploys SI-salient STM channels that also preserve crit-
ical spectro-temporal details in the presence of noise.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a novel approach leveraging STM
features for DTW to achieve accurate time alignment between
clean and degraded speech signals. We identify noise-robust
STM channels across various types of noise and present a
method to select multiple STM channels to improve time align-
ment. Our results demonstrate that DTW using STM features
shows better alignment accuracy compared to DTW using
MFCCs features. Furthermore, we demonstrate the integration
of the time alignment into RB-SIPAs and use the augmented
RB-SIPAs to generate SIP scores. We further compare the out-
put of RB-SIPA with listening test scores and show superior cor-
relation performance using STM features compared to MFCCs.
Our findings reveal that the STM channels critical for the time
alignment of speech overlap with those essential for SI.
