Abstract 
We present PERCEPT-US, a multimodal corpus of audio and 
ultrasound data from 126 American English-speaking children 
ages 8 to 17. Collected during clinical trials investigating 
biofeedback in speech therapy for residual speech sound 
disorder (RSSD), it includes 80 children with no history of 
speech-language-hearing challenges and 46 children with no 
challenges other than RSSD. The corpus is balanced by sex (58 
females, 68 males) and stratified by age and speech therapy 
history. Participants completed syllabic, word, and sentence 
level speech production tasks, totaling 24,699 utterances, with 
a focus on American English rhotics. The corpus demonstration 
uses mixed-effects linear regression on segmented acoustic and 
labeled ultrasound data from 69 children to show that rhotic 
tongue shape categories significantly predict the acoustics of 
higher formant frequency values - the first study to demonstrate 
this acoustic-articulatory relationship in a large pediatric 
sample. 
Index Terms: child speech corpus, ultrasound, acoustic-
articulatory relationships, English rhotics 
1. Introduction 
Speech sound disorder (SSD), which affects a significant 
portion of school-aged children, has been shown to adversely 
impact social, emotional, and literacy outcomes [1-3]. 
Evidence-based treatment methods can alleviate these impacts, 
but research suggests that roughly 5,000 perceptually accurate 
productions are necessary for a sound to generalize to 
spontaneous speech [4], a goal that is challenging to achieve 
given speech language pathologists’ (SLPs) overburdened 
caseloads [5]. This creates a critical need to develop accessible 
and reliable resources that allow children to continue therapy 
outside of direct clinical interactions. Tools that incorporate 
artificial intelligence (AI) have tremendous potential to extend 
direct clinical services through guided home practice. In 
response to this need, our team has created and tested the 
PERCEPT-US corpus. This multimodal corpus includes 
simultaneously collected ultrasound images of the tongue and 
audio recordings from children with no history of speech, 
language, or hearing challenges and children with residual 
speech sound disorder (RSSD) affecting the American English 
rhotic /ɹ/ who have participated in biofeedback speech therapy. 
1.1. Motivation 
Current AI tools for speech typically classify productions as 
correct or incorrect and have demonstrated general success[6]. 
However, they have limited insight into the nature of the 
adjustment needed to achieve perceptually accurate output. 
SLPs are trained to provide clients with individualized feedback 
and phonetic cueing to adjust their speech structures for target 
sound production. However, there are limits to the guidance 
SLPs can offer based on auditory perception alone. For 
instance, perceptually accurate English /ɹ/, which is clinically 
one of the most challenging sounds to address, can be produced 
using multiple tongue shapes [7]. Several studies have 
investigated treatment for RSSD affecting /ɹ/ using ultrasound 
visual biofeedback, where an ultrasound probe placed beneath 
the chin allows the SLP and child to view the shape and 
movements of the tongue during speech therapy. The 
ultrasound display provides a unique ability to pair the SLP’s 
corrective verbal cues with a visual representation of the target 
tongue shape to assist the child in adjusting their own tongue 
placement. Recent studies have shown positive outcomes for 
speech therapy using ultrasound biofeedback, including an 
advantage for ultrasound biofeedback over traditional treatment 
[8-9]. Unfortunately, ultrasound remains out of reach for most 
SLPs due to the cost of the equipment. AI tools capable of 
inferring articulatory postures from the acoustic signal thus 
have the potential to offer two benefits: they could mimic the 
feedback of an SLP during home practice sessions and could 
simulate the unique contribution ultrasound provides to SLP-
guided therapy. 
1.2. Contribution 
While the number of child speech corpora has increased in 
recent years, most feature only audio data paired with 
transcriptions. Ultrasuite, a multimodal audio and ultrasound 
corpus collected during speech therapy based in the United 
Kingdom [10], is the closest comparison to the present 
American English corpus. We have compiled PERCEPT-US 
from data collected during four large ultrasound studies over 
the past decade. The current version of the PERCEPT-US 
corpus includes approximately 25,000 utterances, making it 
nearly twice as large the Ultrasuite UK English corpus.

Data were collected in laboratory settings at Haskins

audio and ultrasound data were simultaneously recorded.  
2.1. Participant Description 
The current version of the corpus includes 126 participants 
(mean age = 11;10 [years;months], range = 8;8 - 17;8). There 
are 58 females and 68 males. Two datasets collected earlier 
focused specifically on the American English /ɹ/ sound, while 
two more recent datasets include a wider variety of speech 
sounds with an emphasis on /ɹ/ and /s/ sounds. All speakers 
were from the Tri-State area (CT-NJ-NY) and spoke a rhotic 
variety of American English in the home. Participants passed a 
hearing screening and had no history of speech-language-
hearing diagnosis, apart from RSSD for 46 children.

2.1.1. Children with RSSD 
Simultaneous audio and ultrasound data were collected from 46 
children with RSSD during two different treatment studies for 
American English /ɹ/: 34 children completed 22 sessions of 
ultrasound biofeedback treatment, and 12 children received 20 
sessions of speech therapy, randomly assigned to conditions 
with or without biofeedback. Data were collected pre- and post-
treatment for the first set of children, while for the second set, 
data were only collected post-treatment. The greater number of 
males in this group (Table 1) is in line with previous research 
showing greater prevalence of RSSD among boys [14].  
2.1.2. Children without RSSD 
Among the 80 children without RSSD, 36 participated in a 2-
day study of speech and sensory function and 44 children 
completed a 1-day study.  
2.2. Study Protocols 
The speech tasks were similar across the four previously 
mentioned studies. There are six different tasks: one syllable-
level, four word-level, and one sentence-level. The syllable and 
sentence tasks included English /ɹ/ in each utterance. The word 
level tasks have greater variety: one featured English /ɹ/ in each 
utterance; one featured English /s/ or /z/; one was specialized to 
investigate tongue shape complexity; and one was specialized 
with minimal pairs. Tasks included direct imitation (syllable), 
picture naming (word-level /ɹ/ and tongue shape complexity), 
and reading (sentence and word-level /s/-/z/ and minimal pairs).

Because this corpus is compiled from four different studies, 
there are inevitably some differences in data collection, 
including for the methods used to ensure consistent alignment 
of the ultrasound with the participant’s speech structures. In the 
oldest of the four studies (ultrasound biofeedback treatment), 
small blue stickers were placed on the face and on the 
ultrasound transducer positioned in a microphone stand under 
the chin during the video recording. These were used to identify 
transducer displacement during data processing; misaligned 
ultrasound frames have been discarded [15]. For the remaining 
three studies, a plastic 3D printed helmet [16] was used to 
secure the ultrasound transducer under the child’s chin. For the 
two most recent studies, there is imaging of the children’s palate 
and occlusal plane (n=52; 13 with RSSD, 39 without RSSD). 
Across all studies, the ultrasound transducer was always 
positioned to collect midsagittal images of the tongue.  
3. Data Preparation 
All audio and ultrasound data were collected simultaneously at 
the task level using various live screen recording software. MP4 
files from Avermedia software and MKV files from Open 
Broadcaster Software were converted in VLC media player to 
a lossless WAV file and MP4 (H.264 + MP3). Bandicam 
recordings were collected as WAV and MP4 simultaneously. A 
team of trained research assistants used the WAV files and Praat 
[17] TextGrid files to manually transcribe each utterance or 
verify automated transcription of the orthographic labels. The 
2806

Montreal Forced Aligner [18] and the PERCEPT pre-trained 
acoustic model [19] were used to segment phone boundaries. 
Processing of the ultrasound MP4 files occurred in Matlab 
using the GetContours and SLURP programs [20, 21], which 
link the timestamps of phone boundaries in Praat TextGrid files 
to ultrasound image frames. All images within the segments of 
interest were visually inspected by a team of trained research 
assistants. In some cases, the most representative image of the 
target phoneme was selected and manually traced by inserting 
16 spline anchor points directly beneath the white line in the 
image, which represents the boundary with air directly above 
the tongue surface. In other cases, the SLURP algorithm was 
used to automatically trace all images with 16 anchor points and 
the research assistants hand-corrected the traced contours if 
necessary. The 16 anchor points were used to generate 100 X-
Y coordinates evenly spaced along the traced contours.  
4. Data Annotation 
The data processing previously described creates multiple types 
of annotations per utterance: audio transcription, phone 
labeling, and frame-based coordinates from tongue shape 
tracing. A subset of the corpus also includes perceptually rated 
accuracy and/or rhotic tongue shape categorization.  
4.1. Perceptual Rating Labels 
All data was originally collected with perceptual accuracy 
ratings by the treating clinicians or trained experimenters. A 
subset of the corpus has been re-rated to establish ground truth 
perceptual ratings. Some of these ratings were completed by 
SLPs, while others were obtained from untrained listeners on 
the Amazon Mechanical Turk crowdsourcing platform. For the 
crowdsourced ratings, the ground truth label comes from the 
average across nine listeners, as recommended in [22]. We 
consider both types of ratings as ground truth labels, although 
one is clinical and the other is from the general public. Rater 
type is included in the corpus metadata.  
4.2. Tongue Shape Category labels for English /ɹ/ 
Ground truth labels provided in this corpus have been collected 
with the goal of articulatory feedback for English /ɹ/ in mind. 
The 100 X-Y coordinates from the frame-based tongue shape 
tracing process are quantitative labels that correspond to 
specific frames of interest within the MP4 files. After tracing 
was complete, the target frames went through an additional 
annotation process to provide a qualitative ground truth label of 
tongue shape category. Currently, 2,698 of the perceptually 
accurate productions from the syllable-level task have been 
annotated with these qualitative tongue shape category labels. 
The raw image was extracted from the MP4 with the two 
preceding and following frames (five frames per utterance). To 
obscure participant information, all frame sequences were 
randomized and presented for coding in a custom PsychoPy 
[23] program. The raw images were qualitatively coded using 
the ultrasound-based decision tree proposed by [24], which 
classifies /ɹ/ tongue shapes into one of five categories using a 
holistic visual inspection approach [11, 24]. The five tongue 
shape categories (Mid-Bunched, Front-Bunched, Front-Up, 
Tip-Up, and Curled-Up) were also collapsed into binary 
bunched (tongue tip down) and retroflex (tongue tip up) 
categories to align with previous research with adult tongue 
shapes [11-13]. For this subset of the corpus, due to the lack of 
occlusal plane imaging and inability to rotate all images to the 
same plane, it is unclear whether the tongue tip is up or down 
in the Front-Up shape, so the category was excluded. To assess 
reliability, the dataset was re-coded by the same individual 
approximately one year after the first round of coding (a 
practice followed by [24]). Cohen’s Kappa [25] was computed 
to assess intra-rater agreement across the two time points, 
yielding weighted k = 0.88 for classification into five categories 
and unweighted k = 0.9 for binary classification. The raw 
percentage of intra-rater agreement was 88.66% for the five 
categories and 95.76% for the binary categories. An additional 
rater labeled 500/2,698 images (18.53%), yielding weighted k 
= 0.9 for classification into five categories and unweighted k = 
0.88 for binary classification. The raw percentage of inter-rater 
agreement was 87.11% for the five categories and 96.91% for 
the binary categories. The inter and intra rater reliability 
represents “almost perfect” levels of agreement [26]. There are 
2,385 images with multiple ratings that agree. 
4.3. Future Corpus Development and Distribution 
Development and annotation of the PERCEPT-US corpus is 
ongoing. Future planned work includes ultrasound image 
labeling for a subset of the perceptually inaccurate portion of 
the corpus. Expert clinicians familiar with ultrasound 
biofeedback will provide ground truth labels related to three 
tongue-related features: anterior constriction, tongue root 
retraction, and lowering of the tongue body. Additionally, new 
audio and ultrasound data will be collected over the next two 
years through a new clinical trial. The PERCEPT-US corpus 
will be made publicly available through the PhonBank platform 
[27] as part of the NIH commitment to data sharing that is 
Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR).  
5. Corpus Demonstration 
To demonstrate a potential application of the corpus, we 
examined the relationship between acoustics and articulation in 
the subset of the English /ɹ/ syllable task that has both 
perceptual ratings and tongue shape category labels (2,385 
utterances). Previous research using electromagnetometry [12] 
and vocal tract Magnetic Resonance Imaging [13] found that 
the fourth and fifth formant frequencies (F4, F5) differed 
between bunched and retroflex tongue shapes for English /ɹ/. 
That work was conducted with 1-2 adult participants. The 
ground truth tongue shape labeling in our corpus provides the 
unique opportunity to examine this acoustic-articulatory 
relationship with a larger sample size (n=69) and in child 
speakers. This demonstration specifically asks whether tongue 
shape predicts the acoustics of American English /ɹ/ when 
controlling for phonetic contexts such as onset and vowel 
context, as well as participant sex and participant group. 
5.1. Methods 
With the goal of measuring the first through fifth formants (F1-
F5), the Voweltine Praat script [28] was adapted to use the burg 
function to select the optimal LPC settings between 5000 – 
7500 Hertz, increasing in 500 Hz steps, when estimating 5.5 
formants for each participant. It then selected the steadiest 25 
milliseconds (ms) in the segmented /ɹ/ interval by comparing 
the standard deviation of formant measures across 5-ms 
windows within the interval. The script measured F1-F5 in the 
steadiest 25-ms window using the settings that yielded the 
lowest standard deviation and then calculated the mean formant 
values in Hz for that window. The Voweltine script was 
selected for use in this demonstration specifically to address the 
variability inherent to child speech data while avoiding the 
2807

time-intensive process of manually selecting optimal LPC 
settings for each participant/utterance. It was also adapted to 
measure higher formants, since F3, F4, and F5 are important for 
English /ɹ/ and its range of tongue shapes. 
Once the formant measures were extracted, the acoustic 
data was linked to participant demographic data and ground 
truth tongue shape labels using the tidyverse package [29] in R 
[30]. This resulted in 1,966 observations of formant measures 
paired with tongue shape labels. Outliers for formants F1 
through F5 were identified and excluded using the rstatix 
package, which uses the boxplot method [31]. A total of 143 
observations (7.3%) were excluded. Three mixed-effects linear 
regression models using the lme4 [32], LmerTest [33], and 
emmeans [34] packages were used to evaluate whether binary 
bunched and retroflex tongue shapes predicted the mean values 
of F3, F4, or F5 in Hz. The models also included fixed effects 
of syllable position (onset, non-onset), vowel context (front, 
non-front), and their interaction with tongue shape, as well as 
fixed effects of participant sex (female, male), age, and their 
interaction, as well as group (with/without RSSD). To account 
for variation across individuals and lexical items, the models 
also included random intercepts for word and random slopes for 
participant with intercepts for tongue shape, syllable position, 
and vowel position.  
5.2. Results 
The 
mixed-effects 
linear 
regression 
models 
revealed 
statistically significant associations between binary tongue 
shape labels (bunched and retroflex) and mean F3 and F4 
frequencies in Hz when controlling for all other variables. 
There was no significant association between binary tongue 
shapes and mean F5 frequencies. These results somewhat 
substantiate previous research in adults. 
For F3 frequencies, there was no significant main effect of 
bunched and retroflex shapes. There was a significant main 
effect of vowel context (β =-210.1, SE = 50.8, p< 0.001) 
whereby on average, non-front productions resulted in lower 
mean F3 Hz values regardless of tongue shape. However, there 
was a significant interaction between tongue shape and vowel 
context, such that non-front vowels produced using a retroflex 
tongue shape resulted in higher mean F3 Hz values relative to 
those produced with a bunched shape (β =170.17, SE = 63.91, 
p< 0.01; non-onset was reference level). This significant 
interaction was unexpected, but it suggests that vowel context 
is important to consider when analyzing the acoustic signature 
of different English /ɹ/ tongue shapes. (Figure 1).

6. Conclusions and Future Work 
This is the first study to examine acoustic-articulatory 
relationships for American English /ɹ/ in the context of a large 
sample size and with child speakers. The binary bunched and 
retroflex tongue shapes predicted F4 frequencies when 
controlling for all other variables. This was expected based on 
previous adult research. The significant findings related to F3 
frequencies, as well as the lack of significant associations 
between tongue shape and F5, were unexpected and will be 
further explored.  
Future work aims to train an acoustic classifier to recognize 
aspects of the tongue shape so that clinicians will be able to "see 
inside the mouth" (as if they had access to ultrasound imaging) 
to provide more targeted cueing. Ultrasound remains out of 
reach for many SLPs and advances in AI have created the 
potential to develop broadly accessible software that simulates 
the unique contribution ultrasound provides to speech therapy. 
In the longer term, we aim to integrate this articulatory feedback 
technology

acoustic biofeedback. Software that combines all of these tools 
is expected to support effective home practice, which could 
enhance children's learning while reducing the heavy caseload 
burden faced by practicing clinicians.
