Abstract
Low-resource languages inherently lack the extensive data
typically required to train effective intelligent systems, lead-
ing to a substantial gap in technological support. In this pa-
per, we address this challenge by focusing on the language vari-
eties spoken in Sardinia, which are marked by limited and frag-
mented linguistic resources. First, we present the design and
implementation of a reproducible pipeline that led to the prepa-
ration of SardinianVoxes, an audio-text dataset comprising
approximately 170 hours of transcribed speech, carefully an-
notated to reflect the internal linguistic diversity of Sardinian.
Second, we introduce a tailored evaluation protocol and bench-
marking suite aimed at assessing the performance of state-of-
the-art speech-to-text models, and their fine-tuned counterparts,
across Sardinian varieties.
We expect that our contributions
will support the development of intelligent speech technolo-
gies for Sardinian. Source code: github.com/ai4limba/
sardinian-voxes.
Index Terms: Low-Resource Languages; Minority Language;
Speech-to-Text; Language Resource Development.
1. Introduction
Languages, particularly those spoken by minority or local com-
munities, offer a unique lens through which to understand the
world, as they embody the history, traditions, and cultural iden-
tity of their speakers. However, the ongoing loss of these lan-
guages often results in the irreversible disappearance of distinct
worldviews and the knowledge they encode [1]. The preser-
vation and valorization of such languages are essential for safe-
guarding linguistic diversity and protecting cultural heritage [2].
Additionally, promoting their use can contribute to social co-
hesion and inclusivity, while fostering recognition of cultural
identities and enriching the collective human experience.
Intelligent systems can offer new opportunities for support-
ing the preservation and valorization of minority or local lan-
guages, through applications such as speech recognition, text-
to-speech synthesis, and machine translation that can enable
documentation, accessibility, and revitalization at scale. How-
ever, current advances behind these advanced applications have
largely focused on high-resource languages, such as English,
Chinese, or French, which benefit from abundant textual and
speech data for model training and evaluation [3]. In contrast,
low-resource languages1, including many spoken in minority or
local communities, face challenges due to limited, fragmented,
or non-digitized linguistic resources and shrinking speaker pop-
1In this work, we use the term low-resource languages to refer to
minority or local languages that lack sufficient digital or annotated lin-
guistic resources for developing modern computational tools.
ulations. This data imbalance limits the reach of intelligent sys-
tems and reinforces the technological marginalization of these
languages. Timely reversing this trend calls for immediate ef-
forts to develop high-quality, representative datasets, adapt ex-
isting models to low-resource scenarios, and design evaluation
protocols that respect linguistic and cultural specificity [4].
One notable technology with the potential to advance the
(digital) presence of low-resource languages is speech-to-text
(STT). By converting spoken input into written text, STT sys-
tems can facilitate the creation of transcribed corpora, support
language education tools, and enable voice-based interfaces tai-
lored to local linguistic contexts, as examples. These capabili-
ties are particularly valuable for languages with strong oral tra-
ditions, helping to ensure their continuity and visibility in the
digital age [5, 6]. Sardinian2 offers a compelling case: despite
its active use across multiple varieties, its linguistic resources
are sparse, fragmented, inconsistent, and limited in volume to
support modern, intelligent STT models training [7] and recent
studies underscore the need for Sardinian-specific tools [8].
To this end, in this paper, we address the limitations out-
lined above through two main novel contributions: (i) the design
and implementation of a reproducible pipeline for the prepara-
tion of SardinianVoxes, an audio-text dataset comprising
approximately 170 hours of transcribed speech, with coverage
of the diverse, annotated varieties of the Sardinian language;
and (ii) the design and development of a tailored evaluation
protocol and benchmarking suite for assessing the performance
of state-of-the-art STT models across Sardinian varieties. Our
study lays the groundwork for a standardized framework to de-
velop speech technologies for Sardinian, also beyond STT.
2. Related Work
This work builds upon two foundational areas: (i) the advance-
ment of deep learning approaches for speech recognition, and
(ii) the design and curation of speech datasets for low-resource
languages. We briefly review key developments in both areas.
2.1. Deep Learning for Speech Recognition
Early approaches to STT primarily relied on statistical models,
notably Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) [9]. With the advent
of deep learning [10], substantial progress has been made, par-
ticularly through the use of architectures such as Recurrent Neu-
ral Networks (RNNs), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs),
and Transformers. Additionally, traditional STT systems were
typically built as multi-stage pipelines.In contrast, recent ad-
vances have shifted toward end-to-end models capable of di-

For instance, Deep-
Speech [11] employs a tailored loss to align input and output
sequences without requiring frame-level annotation. Moreover,
sequence-to-sequence models, such as LAS [12], incorporate
attention mechanisms that allow the model to focus on the most
relevant segments to improve accuracy. An important advance-
ment of this model class is the Transformer architecture, which
employs self-attention mechanisms to model long-range depen-
dencies in speech signals. Conformer [13] enhances this capa-
bility by combining convolutional and self-attention layers to
capture both local and global context.
In low-resource language settings, where annotated speech
data is scarce, models such as Wav2Vec [14] and HuBERT [15]
have proven particularly valuable.
These approaches learn
meaningful representations from raw audio by predicting
masked portions of the input signal. Once pretrained, they can
be fine-tuned on smaller labeled datasets, achieving competitive
performance with limited supervision. More recently, multilin-
gual models like Whisper [16] and XLS-R [17] have been devel-
oped to support a wide array of languages and varieties. These
models exploit cross-lingual transfer via shared representations,
enabling zero- or few-shot learning in low-resource scenarios.
2.2. Speech Datasets for Low-Resource Languages
To better understand the landscape of available resources and
the challenges specific to our study, Table 1 presents a com-
parative overview of existing speech datasets which also in-
clude low-languages. Despite the valuable contributions these
datasets offer to the field, the datasets for many languages re-
main limited in scale or suffer from issues like low audio qual-
ity, sparse annotations, or a lack of linguistic diversity, hinder-
ing the development and evaluation of robust STT models. No-
tably, the only existing dataset for Sardinian is a small subset
available through the Mozilla’s Common Voice project, con-
taining approx. 3 hours of audio. This minimal coverage is far
from sufficient to support the training of modern speech mod-
els. As such, Sardinian remains underrepresented in the speech
technology landscape, highlighting the need for the dataset cre-
ation pipeline and evaluation framework proposed in this work.
3. Methodology
We emphasize that our proposal pursues two main goals. First,
we present a reproducible pipeline for constructing a dataset
of Sardinian speech, consisting of audio segments aligned with
textual transcriptions and covering all major varieties spoken on
the island. Second, we fine-tune state-of-the-art STT models on
this curated dataset and evaluate their performance.

3.1. High-Level Pipeline Description
Figure 1 shows the steps of our pipeline, designed to meet the
outlined objectives. In the first stage (Data Generation),
we assumed to source and process videos with associated subti-
tles from a publicly accessible digital library3, to obtain aligned
audio-text segments. Each segment was then expected to be
manually labeled with its corresponding variety by a commit-
tee of expert linguists with recognized expertise in Sardinian4.
The next stage (Filtered Data Generation) involved
organizing the annotated segments into curated datasets for each
distinct Sardinian variety. These datasets were then used to fine-
tune multiple instances of a pre-trained STT model (Model
Fine-Tuning). Each stage of our proposed pipeline is de-
scribed in detail in the following subsections.
3.2. Part 1: Data Generation
The first part focused on the acquisition and preparation of
audio-text data. To this end, we assumed to begin by collecting
public videos from a digital archive dedicated to the Sardinian

language. Each video is expected to feature a person coming
from a distinct geographical area of Sardinia, resulting in a col-
lection that spans a broad spectrum of Sardinian varieties. Sub-
titles associated with these videos were also retrieved. The sub-
title content, combined with the video titles and metadata, pro-
vided insights into each speaker’s village of origin, reinforcing
the integrity of the dataset. After acquisition, the videos were
processed to extract audio in .wav format, minimizing infor-
mation loss. Subtitles in .vtt format were cleaned and nor-
malized to remove inconsistencies, and their embedded times-
tamps were used to align them with the corresponding audio.
Given that many pre-trained models used for fine-tuning ex-
pect 30-second audio inputs, we chunked each audio-transcript
pair accordingly. Specifically, using the subtitle timestamps,
the speech and text were segmented into chunks of up to 30
seconds, preserving sentence integrity and ensuring that no spo-
ken words were cut mid-utterance. Due to the high variability
among Sardinian varieties, we involved again the same expert
linguists to label each segment based on the variety spoken. The
annotation considered the three most prominent Sardinian vari-
eties (Campidanese, Logudorese, and Nuorese), while
also including two additional labels: Other (for less common
Sardinian varieties), and Non-Sardinian (e.g., other lan-
guages spoken in Sardinia, having different linguistic roots).
3.3. Part 2: Filtered Data Generation
Based on the labels provided by domain experts, we curated two
distinct datasets: one encompassing all language varieties spo-
ken in Sardinia, and another focused exclusively on the three
main Sardinian varieties: Campidanese, Logudorese, and
Nuorese. This was achieved by filtering the labeled data ac-
cording to the assigned variety tags in the metadata file, ensur-
ing each dataset met the intended linguistic scope.
3.4. Part 3: Model Fine-Tuning
To evaluate the effectiveness and reliability of the two generated
datasets, we adopted Whisper [16], a state-of-the-art multilin-
gual model for STT tasks. Each dataset was used to fine-tune a
separate instance of the model, enabling a comparative evalua-
tion across distinct experimental settings. This setup allowed us
to assess the model’s adaptability to the level of specificity.
4. Experiments
In this section, we present an analysis of the generated datasets,
describe the experimental setup adopted for the STT task, and
discuss the results obtained from our benchmarking.
4.1. Example Pipeline Instantiation
We instantiated the proposed pipeline to construct a Sardinian
dataset. Specifically, we leveraged publicly available material
from the Sardinian Digital Library, gathering 341
video interviews of varying durations, typically between 30 and
35 minutes. Each video features a different speaker from a dis-
tinct area of Sardinia. For each resulting audio chunk, we gener-
ated corresponding metadata, including a unique identifier, the
speaker’s place of origin, and spoken language variety. The fi-
nal dataset comprises approximately 170 hours of transcribed
speech data. Note that, to respect the terms of use of the orig-
inal source, we do not redistribute the media files. Instead, we
release the complete codebase required to reproduce the dataset,
thereby ensuring transparency, compliance, and reproducibility.
4.2. Exploratory Data Analysis
In a first analysis, we explored the characteristics of the gener-
ated dataset to inform the fine-tuning strategy for STT models.
In particular, we focused on two key aspects: (i) the length of
the transcribed sentences, and (ii) the number of audio segments
available for each variety. The former helps assess dataset con-
sistency by identifying audio segments that may contain exces-
sively long silences or insufficiently informative transcriptions.
The latter provides insight into the distribution of linguistic va-
rieties, allowing us to adapt the fine-tuning process accordingly.

4.3. Data Partitioning for Model Benchmarking
To establish a solid reference for future research on Sardinian, it
is essential to define an appropriate train/validation/test split of
the data. Given the nature of our dataset, we adopted two differ-
ent, complementary splitting strategies. First, the global dataset
(SardFull) was randomly divided into training, validation,
and test sets, using a standard 80%-10%-10% ratio. Second,
we created a filtered dataset (SardTriV) by retaining only
the three most prominent Sardinian varieties (Campidanese,
Logudorese, and Nuorese and applied a stratified splitting
strategy to it to ensure that the distribution of linguistic varieties
is preserved across training, validation, and test sets.

4.4. Evaluation Protocol for Model Benchmarking
One of the key objectives of our work is to evaluate the per-
formance of pre-trained STT models, specifically those that
have had little or no exposure to low-resource languages, un-
der different fine-tuning setups. To this end, we conducted an
experimentation phase involving several versions of the Whis-
per model. We assessed performance using standard metrics in
STT tasks, namely WER (Word Error Rate) [18] and the CER
(Character Error Rate) [19]. The evaluation followed a three-
fold strategy5: (i) using each model in its original, pre-trained
form; (ii) fine-tuning each model on the training set of the full
dataset (SardFull); and (iii) tuning on a subset (SardTriV)
that includes only the three most prominent Sardinian varieties.
In the third scenario, we evaluated model performance not only
across the entire test set but also separately for each linguistic
variety, to explore variety-specific behavior and generalization.

To contextualize our findings, we discuss them with respect
to those reported in [7], which evaluated multiple models on a
Campidanese-only dataset (not publicly available). In their
study, the best-performing Whisper medium model, after fine-
tuning, achieved a WER of 0.79. By contrast, our fine-tuned
counterpart obtained a lower WER (0.61) on the same variety
(a +22.78% gain). While the comparison is not fully equivalent
due to differences in datasets and setups, it nonetheless rein-
forces the effectiveness of our design and fine-tuning strategy
in supporting speech recognition for low-resource languages.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we proposed SardinianVoxes, a speech
recognition dataset aimed to address the under-representation of
low-resource languages in speech recognition. The dataset cap-
tures multiple linguistic varieties spoken across Sardinia, ful-
filling our first contribution: the design, implementation, and
adoption of a reproducible pipeline for preparing data to train
models on Sardinian speech recognition. As a second contri-
bution, we designed a framework aimed at standardizing the
model benchmarking for this language. Finally, we validated it
with several versions of the Whisper model, showing that fine-
tuning with Sardinian-specific data can improve performance.
Nevertheless, the findings highlight several areas requiring
further investigation. First, the limited coverage across vari-
eties suggests the need for more balanced data collection. Sec-
ond, the persistence of errors in linguistically complex scenarios
points to the importance of exploring advanced audio prepro-
cessing techniques to enhance input quality. Third, the reliance
on fixed chunking strategies may constrain model effectiveness,
indicating that alternative segmentation methods merit evalua-
tion. Lastly, assessing a broader range of models and training
new ones from scratch could yield deeper insights into the most
effective strategies for low-resource speech recognition.
