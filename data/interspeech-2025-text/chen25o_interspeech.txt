Predicting Adolescent Suicidal Risk from Multi-task-based Speech:  
An Ensemble Learning Approach 
Xi Chen1, Renzhe Yu1, Yansen Tan1, Yiyi Li1, Quan Qian2️, Ying Lin1 
1Department of Psychology, Sun Yat-sen University, China

Abstract 
Adolescent suicide is a pressing global public health issue. 
Timely identification of suicide risk is crucial. Traditional 
methods of assessing suicide risk are often limited by their 
reliance on subjective input and resource requirements. This 
paper aims to address these limitations by detecting suicide risk 
from multi-task-based speech, utilizing a dataset of 600 Chinese 
adolescents (age: 10-18 yr) provided by the 1st SpeechWellness 
Challenge. Our approach involved both acoustic and semantic 
features extracted by OpenSmile, Emotion2Vec, and a fine-
tuned BERT-Chinese model. The base models were trained 
using XGBoost and SVM, etc., with hyperparameters tuned by 
Bayesian optimization. Then we implemented a multi-model 
and multi-task nested voting ensemble framework to integrate 
the base models, achieving a final test set accuracy of 0.63 
(recall=0.74, F1≈0.67). This work highlights the potential of 
voice-based biomarkers in mental health assessment. 
Index Terms: adolescent suicide, speech-based predictive 
modeling, ensemble learning 
1. Introduction 
Research on adolescent suicide prediction is of importance due 
to its potential to inform prevention strategies and mitigate the 
risk of premature mortality among this vulnerable population. 
Identifying reliable predictors of suicide can facilitate early 
intervention, reducing the likelihood of suicide completion. 
The existing methods for assessing suicidal risk, including 
self-report, clinical assessment, are effective in some cases. 
However, they also have a number of limitations. First, self-
report methods are susceptible to factors such as adolescents' 
emotional fluctuations and social expectations, resulting in 
more subjective data. Second, traditional clinical assessments 
rely on diagnosis by professionals and are limited by time, 
resources, and regional availability. In addition, the factors 
influencing suicidal ideation are complex and multidimensional, 
involving individual and enviromental aspects. It is often 
difficult for traditional methods to comprehensively capture 
such diverse features, leading to inaccurate or one-sided 
assessment results. It is thus difficult for traditional single 
assessment methods to fully capture risk factors [1]. 
To address the above limitations, speech-based prediction 
becomes a promising solution. The 1st SpeechWellness 
Challenge (SW1) in InterSpeech 2025 offers a speech dataset 
of 600 participants and reveal each participant’s responses to 
three different speech tasks designed to capture a range of 
linguistic, cognitive, and emotional markers relevant to suicidal 
risk detection [1]. In particular, Task 0 (ER) assesses 
spontaneous speech, providing insights into emotional 
expression, coping strategies, and linguistic patterns associated 
with distress. Task 1 (PR) offers a controlled linguistic task, 
enabling the analysis of prosody, articulation, and speech 
rhythm. Task 2 (ED) evaluates emotional perception and 
cognitive processing by requiring participants to describe a 
facial expression. Deficits in emotion recognition and 
verbalization are commonly linked to suicidal risk, making this 
task valuable for assessing affective and social cognition. As 
such, the SW1 dataset facilitates a comprehensive analysis of 
semantic, prosodic, and cognitive-linguistic features, enhancing 
the reliability of speech-based suicidal risk assessment. 
By combining different models derived by different 
methods, features and data, ensemble learning can compensate 
for the shortcomings of a single model and integrate the 
advantages of different models to reduce overfitting and 
improve generalizability. Considering the multi-task nature of 
the SW1 dataset and the high complexity of speech data, 
ensemble learning offers a straightforward and promising way 
to build a competitive suicide risk prediction model. In 
particular, ensemble learning allows us to involve richer input 
information by combining models based on acoustic features 
(e.g., emotional tone, speech rate, etc.) and semantic features 
(e.g., textual utterance analysis).  It also enhances the model's 
ability to capture complex affective and psychological states 
through joint learning of multiple tasks. 
2. Methods 
In this section, we elaborated on the proposed method for 
predicting the adolescent suicide risk based on the multi-task 
speech data offered by SW1. For details of the dataset and the 
third-party tools used in the method, refer to Section 1.1, 1.2 
and 1.3 in Supplementary Materials. 
2.1. Feature engineering 
2.1.1. Independent speech features 
In this study, we conducted a statistical analysis of audio 
features across three different tasks (TASK0, TASK1 and 
TASK2) to identify significant features in suicidal risk 
detection. We utilized the openSMILE [2, 3] toolkit to extract a 
total of 88 eGeMAPS[1] features from the training set of each 
task. To assess the differences in features between the two 
categories (Label as 1 and 0), we performed independent two-
sample t-tests on each feature. In each task, features with a p-
value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. 
The results indicated that there were 10, 12, and 11 significant 
features for TASK 0, 1, and 2, respectively.

To analyze features across different frequency ranges, we 
divided the extracted frequency band energy into low frequency 
(0-40 Hz), mid frequency (40-80 Hz), and high frequency (80-
128 Hz) bands, and calculated statistical features for each band, 
including mean, standard deviation, maximum, and minimum. 
Additionally, we calculated the overall energy's mean, standard 
deviation, range, skewness, and kurtosis to comprehensively 
describe the distribution of frequency band energy features. 
Taken together, we extracted 145 features, including 128 
individual frequency band energies, 12 statistical features from 
the three frequency bands, and 5 overall statistical measures.  
Based on the training set, we selected the features with 
significant inter-group difference (p < 0.05, effect size > 0.15). 
Then we performed cross-validation using XGBoost to further 
select key features. Ultimately, the three tasks identified 5, 4, 
and 8 key features for TASK 0, 1, and 2, respectively, which 
are of great significance for building suicide recognition models. 
Furthermore, we enriched the acoustic feature set by 
incorporating a broader range of features extracted using 
openSMILE, including spectral, cepstral, and voice quality 
parameters, along with emotion embeddings derived from 
Emotion2Vec and a novel silence ratio feature. This process 
resulted in a high-dimensional feature space with 725,931 
features, which were then standardized and subjected to an 
independent-sample t-test to identify significant features (p < 
0.05). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to 
reduce the dimensionality while preserving 99% of the variance. 
This approach ensured that the most salient acoustic features 
were retained for subsequent analyses. 
2.1.2. Integrated speech features 
We proposed a framework for feature fusion and optimization. 
We merged the aforementioned independent speech features 
according to the tasks. The initial feature dimensions were 15, 
16, and 19, respectively. To extract more effective feature 
representations, we applied Incremental Principal Component 
Analysis (Incremental PCA) for dimension reduction of the 
features for each task. Considering the inter-task correlations, 
we utilized a feature interaction method based on Partial Least 
Squares Regression (PLSR) to handle task pairs (TASK0-
TASK1, TASK0-TASK2, and TASK1-TASK2) separately. 
Specifically, for each pair of tasks, we first used PLSR to 
learn the mapping relationship between the feature spaces of the 
two tasks, extracting 4-dimensional interaction components. 
We then concatenated these interaction components with the 
first 4 original features of the target task to form 8-dimensional 
mixed interaction features. This design retains the direct 
information of the original features while capturing the latent 
associative patterns between tasks. 
Finally, we combined all the base features and interaction 
features to obtain a 48-dimensional vector and performed 
recursive feature elimination with cross-validation(RFECV)  
based on Random Forest . Ultimately, we selected 40 of the 
most discriminative features, significantly reducing the 
dimensionality of the feature space while preserving the ability 
of the features to discriminate. In addition, by including gender 
and age, we had a total of 42 features. 
2.1.3. Semantic features 
We conducted feature engineering for the text classification 
task to optimize model performance and enhance generalization 
ability. Since the original data was provided in audio format, 
we first used the Paraformer speech recognition model to 
convert the audio into text, forming the input features for 
subsequent natural language processing (NLP) tasks. This 
conversion process not only preserved the semantic information 
but also enabled the BERT pre-trained model [6] to effectively 
extract features from the text data. 
In the text processing stage, we utilized the BERT-Base-
Chinese pre-trained model to encode the text. BERT constructs 
contextual relationships through a bidirectional Transformer 
mechanism and generates high-dimensional semantic features. 
Specifically, we used the hidden state vector at the [CLS] 
position as the sentence-level representation, which has a 
dimensionality of 768 and represents the semantic information 
of the entire sentence. 
To further enhance the model robustness, we introduced the 
nlpcda data augmentation strategy. This strategy includes 
methods such as synonym replacement, character swapping, 
and random deletion to expand the training data and improve 
the model's generalization ability. Through this augmentation 
strategy, we can generate more variants of the training samples, 
increasing the model's adaptability to different inputs. 
2.2. Model training and ensemble 
2.2.1. XGBoost Training with Independent Features 
We trained an XGBoost classifier in a 5-fold cross-validation 
(CV) on each set of speech features (please refer to section 
2.1.1). The Bayesian optimization method was employed for 
tuning the model's hyperparameters (500 iterations with 50 
random initialization), with the optimization objective of 
maximizing the CV accuracy. 
2.2.2. Ensemble training with integrated features 
As for integrated speech features(in section 2.1.2), we proposed 
an 
ensemble 
learning-based 
classification 
framework 
combining random forests and support vector machines (with 
both linear and RBF kernels). In the preprocessing stage, we 
standardized features and enhanced model robustness through 
410

data augmentation, specifically by adding Gaussian noise (σ = 
0.01) and applying scale perturbation (0.95-1.05). 
The model training followed a two-phase strategy: first 
optimized the base classifiers' hyperparameters by Bayesian 
optimization (100 iterations with 10 random initializations), 
then determined the ensemble weights (50 iterations with 10 
random initializations) to maximize 5-fold CV accuracy. 
2.2.3. MLP training with semantic features 
The semantic features(in section 2.1.3) were fed into a multi-
layer perceptron (MLP) classifier, which comprises two fully 
connected layers with ReLU activation functions. To mitigate 
the overfitting risk, dropout regularization was employed. The 
output layer utilized a sigmoid activation function to perform 
binary classification. 
For the loss function, we employed Focal Loss to address 
class imbalance issues. Additionally, AdamW was used as the 
optimizer, with a learning rate set to 2e-5. A cosine annealing 
learning rate scheduler was incorporated to enable dynamic 
adjustment of the learning rate throughout the training process. 
During training, we utilized batch-wise optimization 
strategy with the batch size of 16. To enhance the training 
stability, mixed precision training was applied, facilitating a 
faster training process while reducing memory consumption. 
We splited the dataset into 80% for training and 20% for 
validation, performing evaluations on the validation set at the 
end of each epoch to assess the model's generalization ability. 
2.2.4. Model ensemble 
The above three types of models were ensembled through a 
hierarchical approach. First, we conducted hard voting on the 
XGBoost models for each set of independent speech features, 
and then combined their results with those from the semantic 
feature model and the integrated feature model using additional 
hard voting. All models were trained and validated using 5-fold 
cross-validation on the training set. The final performance was 
evaluated on the given test set of 100 samples using accuracy, 
recall, precision, and F1-score as evaluation metrics. 
2.3. Model Explanation 
To understand the contributions of different acoustic features 
and their interactions, we conducted a comprehensive feature 
importance analysis based on our ensemble model. The 
importance scores were calculated from three perspectives and 
then combined using the optimized weights obtained from 
ensemble training. Specifically, for the random forest classifier, 
we computed the mean decrease in Gini impurity across all trees. 
For the linear SVM, we used the absolute values of the feature 
coefficients. For the RBF SVM, we employed permutation 
importance, 
which 
measures 
the 
decrease 
in 
model 
performance when a feature is randomly shuffled. The final 
importance score for each feature was obtained by summing up 
these individual importance scores using their corresponding 
model weights determined in the ensemble optimization phase.

3.2. Feature importance 
In this study, the feature importance analysis was performed 
exclusively for TASK 2 (comprising features from openSMILE, 
emotion2vec, and silence ratio) for the following reasons. First, 
Task 2 offers a comprehensive set of features from multiple 
dimensions of speech, including time-domain, frequency-
domain, and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), 
which provide a rich array of data for evaluating the relationship 
between speech characteristics and suicidal risk. Unlike the 
other tasks that are more focused on specific aspects of speech 
(e.g., emotional regulation or facial expression recognition), 
Task 2 encompasses a broader spectrum of features that may be 
more indicative of an individual's emotional and cognitive 
states, thus offering greater potential for detecting subtle 
patterns of distress. Second, Task 2 has the potential to provide 
more nuanced insights into speech as a risk indicator. By 
411

incorporating features from both emotion2vec (a speech 
emotion representation model) and traditional acoustic features, 
Task 2 captures not only raw speech characteristics but also 
higher-level emotional content. Given the complexity of 
suicidal tendencies, it is crucial to explore speech patterns from 
a multifaceted perspective. As Task 2 was the most 
comprehensive and potentially the most relevant to suicidal risk, 
it made sense to prioritize the analysis of features from this task. 
In Task 2, the extracted features included time-domain 
features, frequency-domain features, and MFCCs, each 
contributing uniquely to the model’s performance. For example, 
time-domain features like RMS energy capture variations in 
speech intensity, reflecting the emotional energy of the speaker. 
These features are important because fluctuations in speech 
intensity often correspond to changes in emotional states, such 
as distress. Similarly, frequency-domain features, such as 
Spectral Kurtosis and Spectral Slope, provide insight into the 
speech signal's frequency distribution. Spectral Kurtosis 
measures the sharpness of spectral peaks, which can indicate 
emotional intensity or distress, while Spectral Slope reveals the 
distribution of frequency components across the speech signal. 
Together, these features help the model understand both the 
energy and clarity of the speaker's emotional expression. Table

4. Discussion and conclusion 
4.1.  Model performance 
Our ensemble learning approach for predicting adolescent 
suicidal risk from multi-task speech data demonstrates 
promising performance in detecting psychological distress 
through speech patterns. BERT's semantic feature extraction 
effectively identifies language patterns associated with suicidal 
tendencies, including negative self-references, cognitive 
rigidity, and social isolation - traits previously documented in 
at-risk individuals [7, 8]. 
The multi-task approach, capturing different emotional, 
cognitive, and prosodic features, provides a comprehensive 
assessment of adolescents' mental states. Our ensemble method 
integrating diverse feature models outperforms individual 
classifiers, particularly in recall - crucial for identifying high-
risk individuals. The model achieved 67% accuracy and 71% 
F1-score, indicating potential for practical applications, though 
further optimization is needed to improve detection of subtle 
risk indicators. 
4.2. Feature importance 
Feature importance analysis reveals that combinations of time-
domain, frequency-domain, and MFCC features are critical for 
identifying suicidal risk. Time-domain features like RMS 
energy reflect speech intensity disruptions common in 
psychological distress. Spectral features, particularly spectral 
kurtosis and slope, indicate that speech clarity and emotional 
intensity serve as key risk indicators, with spectral kurtosis 
specifically linked to emotional distress markers. 
MFCC features effectively capture speech timbre and 
prosody, which reflect emotional states like sadness and anxiety 
- common in at-risk adolescents. The interaction features 
between different speech tasks, especially between emotion 
regulation (Task 0) and facial expression description (Task 2), 
demonstrate strong discriminative ability. This suggests that 
relationships between emotional expression and social 
cognition provide crucial supplementary information for risk 
prediction. 
4.3. Conclusion and future directions 
This study demonstrates speech-based analysis's potential for 
predicting adolescent suicidal risk. By capturing subtle 
linguistic, prosodic, and emotional signals through multi-task 
learning and ensemble methods, our model offers a promising 
alternative to traditional assessment approaches. Early 
detection of distress markers through speech could become a 
valuable tool for timely intervention. 
Future research may incorporate longitudinal data to track 
how speech characteristics evolve over time relative to stressors 
and mental health changes. This could provide deeper insights 
into 
individual 
vulnerability-environment 
interactions, 
potentially enabling more personalized intervention strategies. 
Using deep learning techniques may further optimize risk 
prediction, enhancing accuracy and real-world applicability.
