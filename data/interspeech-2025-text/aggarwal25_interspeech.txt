Abstract
This study evaluates large language models’ (LLMs) rea-
soning capabilities in spoken language understanding (SLU)
during interpersonal interactions. We incorporated several fac-
tors into LLM prompts: instructing via examples (IE), inte-
grating domain knowledge (DK), and including context (IC).
Experiments with Gemini-1.5-pro, GPT-3.5-turbo, and GPT-4o
were conducted on an SLU task that classifies the degree of ex-
planation (i.e., under-explained, succinct, comprehensive, over-
explained) in job interview responses—an important step to-
ward developing automatic interview training systems. Results
demonstrate the feasibility of few-shot (1- to 4-shot) learning,
with ablation studies confirming that modifications to prompts,
especially when combining IE, DK, and IC, lead to further per-
formance improvements.
Index Terms: large language models, reasoning, spoken lan-
guage understanding, dialog, job interview training, degree of
explanation
1. Introduction
Job interviews are the entryway to the job market, serving as a
crucial step in assessing a candidates competencies and evaluat-
ing their suitability for any specific role [1]. During interviews,
interviewees must effectively broadcast their expertise through
their speech content, prosody, and other verbal and non-verbal
behaviors. In this regard, tracking conversations and extract-
ing underlying meaning from candidate responses is essential
for delivering targeted feedback that could enhance interview
performance [2, 3].
Recent advances in LLMs have demonstrated promising
reasoning abilities, such as chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
[4], that enable these models to follow the flow of dialogue,
identify salient patterns, and understand semantic nuances. This
involves systematically “thinking” about a problem and draw-
ing logical conclusions based on available information [5]. In
addition, the integration of LLMs in educational and profes-
sional settings is gaining traction. Recent studies indicate that
generative AI tools can significantly improve workplace learn-
ing by providing personalized, real-time feedback [6]. Research
has further highlighted the effectiveness of LLM-based systems
in enhancing collaborative performance and streamlining com-
munication in professional settings [7]. Moreover, conversa-
tional AI is emerging as a key component in promoting a posi-
tive workplace climate and facilitating interpersonal exchanges
[8].
This paper investigates the ability of LLMs to detect inef-
fective communication in job interviews, an important type of
spoken language communication used to assess a candidate’s
qualifications and communication skills. Particularly, we fo-
cus on the degree of explanation of interview responses and ex-
amine the ability of LLMs to classify a response among four
classes (i.e., under-explained, succinct, comprehensive, over-
explained) via incorporating several factors into the prompt,
namely, instructing via examples (IE), integrating domain
knowledge (DK), and integrating context (IC). Results indicate
that LLMs are capable of few-shot learning for the focal task,
and that the aforementioned techniques contribute to varying
degrees in improving model performance. These findings un-
derscore the potential of LLMs to process conversational data
and have implications for these models ultimately providing
feedback for interview training and offering insights that help
users refine their responses through informed reasoning.
2. Prior Work
CoT prompting was introduced as a method to help LLMs en-
gage in reasoning instead of simply providing answers [4]. CoT
prompting was implemented with zero-shot learning by simply
adding a prompt (i.e., “ Let’s think step by step ”) before each
answer without including in-domain examples. Results indicate
that zero-shot CoT outperformed LLMs without CoT in a vari-
ety of benchmark tasks. Further work has explored few-shot
CoT by providing manually crafted reasoning of demonstra-
tions alongside examples with step-by-step reasoning [4]. This
method was effective mainly for large models with around 100
billion parameters and complex multi-step reasoning problems.
However, the manual nature of few-shot CoT entails the limi-
tation of handcrafting examples. To address this, other studies
have proposed automated methods to construct demonstrations
broadening the applicability of CoT prompting [9, 10].
LLMs have been recently evaluated for their ability to per-
form SLU tasks, which differ from written text in terms of lan-
guage use, potential disfluencies, and reliance on dialog con-
text. An initial quantitative and qualitative evaluation of Chat-
GPT was conducted on publicly available benchmarks for SLU
tasks [11]. Similarly, ChatGPT and GPT-3.5 were found ef-
fective in zero-shot dialog understanding tasks, though they
did not always match the performance of state-of-the-art fine-
tuned models [12]. Results further indicated that LLMs show
improved dialog understanding when multiple dialog turns are
available, rather than a single one. ChatGPT and GPT3.5 with
a large number of parameters performed well on intent classifi-
cation with zero- and few-shot learning, sometimes surpassing
fine-tuned models on the same tasks [13]. In contrast, smaller
models, such as GPT-2, OPT, and GPT-3.5 Curie, depicted
lower performance in zero-shot cases. Expanding upon this,
a two-stage prompting, which transforms the SLU task into a
question-answering problem, achieved better results compared
to LLMs without prompting [14].

The contributions of this paper are: (1) It introduces a novel
method of integrating DK into LLM prompts, demonstrating
the benefits of incorporating intuitive task-specific knowledge
to enhance LLM performance; (2) It improves our understand-
ing on IE and IC in LLM prompts for SLU tasks, two tech-
niques that have been underexplored in spoken dialog settings;
and (3) It expands upon existing SLU tasks by examining the
LLMs’ ability to detect the degree of explanation in job inter-
view responses, a task heavily reliant on dialog context and the
structural quality of interview responses. Although our task is
domain-specific, our contribution lies in showing that minimal
in-context prompt augmentation enables LLMs to adapt to spe-
cialized, context-dependent tasks with few-shot learning.
3. Data Description
Our data comes from the from the VetTrain dataset [15] and
include 38 military veterans who participated in a mock job in-
terview conducted by experienced interviewers via Zoom [16].
Audio recordings and transcripts of the interviews were ob-
tained and cleaned. Collectively, the participants responded to
286 interview questions. In order to label the degree of ex-
planation of these responses, three annotators were employed.
Based on their annotation, each response was labeled as one of
four possible categories–Under-explained, Succinct, Compre-
hensive, and Over-explained (Table 1) [17]. The disagreement
among the annotators was resolved through multiple rounds of
adjudication and discussion. For each response, the final label
was obtained through majority voting. The annotation process
resulted in a moderate inter-annotator agreement, measured in
terms of Krippendorff’sα = 0.677.
4. Methodology
The responses belonging to the Under-explained and Suc-
cinct classes are significantly shorter in length compared to re-
sponses from the Comprehensive and Over-explained classes.
In terms of total word count, Under-explained and Succinct
responses contain significantly fewer words than Comprehen-
Under-explained responses have more:
* Words with political content (e.g., words and phrases
related to politics, governance, and regulations).
Succinct responses have more:
* Polite expressions (e.g., words and phrases that show
courtesy or deference such as ”please,” ”thank you,” and
”excuse me”).
* Tentative words (e.g., words indicating uncertainty or pos-
sibility such as ”if”, ”maybe,” ”perhaps,” and ”probably”).

4.1. Eliciting reasoning in LLMs
4.1.1. Instructing via examples (IE)
LLM instruction offers specific guidance to the model, allowing
users to influence how the model interprets a request [4]. Here,
we implemented instruction via providing reasoning steps cor-
responding to the degree of explanation for five randomly se-
lected examples per class. For n-shot learning, a random sub-
set ofn examples per class was selected once and consistently
used in the LLM prompts across all experiments. To avoid data
leakage, we ensured that examples used in the prompt were ex-
cluded from the evaluation set; that is, performance was com-
puted only on instances not shown to the model during prompt-
ing.
4.1.2. Integrating domain knowledge (DK)
We integrated domain knowledge to help mitigate issues
of weak factuality and hallucinations often occurring in
LLMs [18]. We leveraged the psycholinguistic characteriza-
tion of the degree of explanation in the responses by extracting
several psycholingustic properties using the Linguistic Inquiry
and Word Count (LIWC) toolbox [19]. These properties in-
cluded among others general linguistic descriptors (e.g., word
count), summary variables (e.g., analytical thinking), standard
linguistic dimensions (e.g., pronouns), and psychological con-
structs (e.g., cognition), resulting in a total of 93 LIWC de-
scriptors. We assessed the ability of each descriptor to differ-
entiate between the two types of responses in each task via a
t-test. A total of three descriptors (i.e., % words related to pol-
itics, tentativeness, politeness) were significantly different be-
tween Under-explained and Succinct responses (Task 1), while
12 descriptors (i.e., word count, prepositions, % words related
to numbers, achievement, causation, negations, tentativeness,
social processes, auditory processes, work, past) were signif-
icantly different between Comprehensive and Over-explained
responses. These descriptors were converted into simple self-
explanatory text and were added to the prompt (Fig. 1).

4.1.3. Integrating Context (IC)
We hypothesized that providing the LLM with additional con-
text about the flow of conversation in an interview might en-
hance its performance. Thus, we integrated context via adding
to the LLM prompt k question/response pairs from the inter-
view that were previously assessed by the LLM and the LLMs
output for each pair. Context was maintained separately for
each participant and grew incrementally as the interview pro-
gresses. For example, when evaluating thenth question-answer
pair of an interview, the LLMs were also provided with the
(n− 1)th, (n− 2)th,... , (n−k)th previous question/response
pairs of the interview and the LLM output for each pair.
4.2. Experimental settings
We conducted experiments with the Gemini-1.5-pro, GPT-3.5-
turbo, and GPT-4o in a n-shot learning framework ( n =
0, 1,..., 5). All prompts included an introductory sentence set-
ting up the context for the LLM (i.e., “ You are a text classifier.
Your task is to classify the following interview response into one
of two categories:”) and the definition of the classes included in
the corresponding task (i.e., Task 1 or Task 2; Table 1). De-
pending on the experimental setting, the prompt also included
text associated with the IE (Section 4.1.1), DK (Section 4.1.2),
and IC (Section 4.1.3). We also conducted experiments using
pairwise combinations of the above settings, and finally incor-
porating all these settings in the prompt. Each query input con-
sisted of a question-answer pair for a participant. We computed
the macro-averaged F1-score that weighs each class equally re-
gardless of its frequency. While both the tasks exhibit class im-
balance, we note that our experimental setup does not involve
model training and thus is not subject to learned biases in the
traditional sense.
4.3. Ablation studies
We conducted three types of ablation studies: (1) We analyzed
how incorporating various types of DK in the prompt influences
the GPT-3.5-turbo performance. For Task 1, we included in
the prompt text that corresponds to each of the three LIWC de-
scriptors (Section 4.1.2) separately and all their combinations.
For Task 2, we initially included the word count and % words
related to numbers, followed by adding one at a time each of
the remaining LIWC descriptors (Section 4.1.2); (2) We car-
ried out experiments with GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini-1.5-pro
using prompts with different context lengths ( k = 1 ,..., 5;
Section 4.1.3); and (3) We experimented with three types of or-
dering the examples in the prompt provided to Gemini-1.5-pro
to determine which would yield the best classification results:
(a) all examples from the majority label (i.e., Succinct, Compre-
hensive) mentioned first, followed by all examples from the mi-
nority label (i.e., Under-explained, Over-explained) (M1); (2)
all examples from the minority label mentioned first, followed
by all examples from the majority label (M2); (3) alternating
examples from each label within the prompt (M3).
5. Results
Results indicate that 1- and 2-shot learning yield good perfor-
mance for both Tasks 1 and 2, while 3- and 4-shot learning also
works well for Task 2 (Table 2). When examining each consid-
ered approach of LLM reasoning in isolation, IC yields better
performance in the majority of cases compared to IE and DK.

When applying IE+IC, Gemini-1.5-pro achieves the best results
for both Task 1 and 2, potentially underscoring the model’s abil-
ity to handle long prompts. GPT-4o yields on average better
results for Task 1 compared to GPT-3.5-turbo, while the oppo-
site holds for Task 2. A potential reason for this might be that
Task 1 is overall more challenging compared to Task 2, due to
its subtlety and the overall short length of responses involved in
this task, thus, it might require more sophisticated models such
as GPT-4o and the recently introduced reasoning models.
In the ablation studies, integrating DK via including all
three psycholinguistic descriptors for Task 1 (i.e., % words re-
lated to politics, tentativeness, and politeness) yields the best
result (Fig. 2a). For Task 2, the highest macro F1-scores were
achieved by combining the word count and % words related to
numbers with % words related to achievement, causation, social
processes, and work (Fig. 2b). This is likely because compre-
hensive and over-explained responses depict meaningful differ-
ences in these areas. While performance does not vary greatly
based on the context length (Table 3), adding some context in
the prompt (k> 2) generally improve the results, especially for
Gemini-1.5-pro, which tends to utilize context better compared
to GPT-3.5-turbo. Finally, the ordering of examples impacts the
macro F1-score in Task 1 with better results when the Under-
explained examples were provided before the Succinct ones in
the prompt (M2) (Table 5), suggesting the importance of pre-
senting examples from highly under-represented classes first.
6. Discussion
Findings from this study indicate that LLMs can be used to
conduct few-shot learning in context-rich SLU tasks, such as
assessing the degree of explanation in job interview responses.

The effective augmentation of LLM prompts via IC, IE, and DK
further underscores the adaptability of LLMs that can be poten-
tially adjusted to various domains. This could be particularly
useful in providing personalized feedback to job candidates dur-
ing interview training, where LLMs might be able to provide
personalized feedback based on a very small number of exam-
ples from a given user. This study, however, presents several
limitations. First, broader generalizability remains a challenge,
as the dataset used focuses on military veterans, which may not
fully capture the diversity of interview styles, job sectors, or
languages. Second, the study relies heavily on the construction
and refinement of LLM prompts, while it would be important
to explore LLM fine-tuning approaches as part of future work.
Finally, the study did not investigate the explanations provided
by the LLMs for their answers, which could further augment
our understanding of the inner workings behind those models.
The findings from this study suggest that LLMs could play
a significant role in designing speech technologies for inter-
view training, a relatable use case for investigating personal-
ized and adaptive feedback. Given their ability to perform few-
shot learning in context-rich SLU tasks, LLMs could assess key
aspects of interview responses, such as the degree of explana-
tion, and offer constructive suggestions and actionable feedback
for improvement. The adaptability of LLM prompts, enhanced
through the IC, IE, and DK techniques, indicates that these
models could be tailored to different industries and job roles,
making them a versatile tool for interview preparation. Addi-
tionally, the capability of LLMs to generate feedback based on
minimal user examples presents an opportunity for more effi-
cient and scalable interview coaching.
7. Conclusions
This study demonstrated the potential of few-shot learning with
LLMs for conducting SLU tasks, such as classifying the degree
of explanation in job interview responses. The results highlight
the effectiveness of incorporating IE, DK, and IC techniques
to LLMs. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset to
include a broader range of interview contexts and candidate pro-
files, exploring additional techniques for enhancing prompt de-
sign and LLM fine-tuning, and investigating the interpretabil-
ity of LLMs’ decision-making processes to gain deeper insights
into their reasoning.
