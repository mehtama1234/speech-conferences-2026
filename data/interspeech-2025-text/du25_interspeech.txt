Tonal Contrasts in the Malipo Variety of the Mienic Language 
Changhong Du1, Fang Hu2 
1University of Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, China 
2Institute of Linguistics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, China 
earendil1998@163.com, hufang@hotmail.com 
 
 
Abstract 
This paper describes tonal contrasts in the Malipo variety of the 
Mienic language and explores how they are manifested by 
acoustic cues. Results show that Linear Mixed-Effects Models 
based on Growth Curve Analysis successfully characterizes the 
5 level tones, 3 falling tones, 3 rising or concave tones, and 2 
convex tones in the Malipo variety. In addition to F0, duration 
also plays a role in a complex tonal system. 
Index Terms: tone, pitch, contour, duration, Mienic language 
1. Introduction 
The Hmong-Mien languages are known for their complexity in 
tones, with tones primarily serving to distinguish lexical 
meanings [1, pp. 11]. There are four major varieties or dialects 
in the Mienic language family, namely Mien, Kim Mun, Biao 
Min, and Zao Min. And the Kim Mun dialects have the richest 
inventories of tones, typically 12 to 15 tones, featuring an 
extremely complex tonal system with multiple layers of tonal 
split [2, pp. 158-162]. This paper describes tonal contrasts in 
the Malipo variety and explores how they are manifested by 
acoustic cues.

The Malipo variety belongs to the Kim Mun dialects, and is 
spoken by the Landian Yao people in Malipo County and its 
vicinity in the Wenshan Zhuang and Miao Autonomous 
Prefecture of Yunnan Province, China. The Malipo variety has 
13 tones. As shown in Table 1, the 9 tones in unchecked 
syllables are denoted as T1, T1’&T7’a (two merged tones), T2, 
T3, T3’, T4, T5, T5’ and T6, and the 4 tones in checked 
syllables with a stop ending are labeled as T7a, T7b, T7’b and 
T8. The tones are numbered according to the comparative tonal 
phonology in the Mienic languages so that one can examine 
tonal development in the Malipo variety. First, odd-numbered 
tones are associated with historical voiceless initials, and even-
numbered tones with historical voiced initials [3, 4, 5]. Second, 
an apostrophe denotes tonal split induced by the aspiration of 
initial consonant [6, pp. 26-28]. Third, the letters denote tonal 
split conditioned by (a) long vowel or (b) short vowel [6, pp. 
26-28] [7, 8]. Please be noted that these rules are historically 
conditioned, rather than synchronically correlated. For instance, 
the historical Tone 7 is not synchronically correlated with 
voiceless initials and has split into four tones in Malipo under 
the condition of vowel length and initial aspiration, among 
which T7’a is further merged with T1’. However, this paper is 
concerned with synchronic tonal phonology in the Malipo 
variety, with an aim to explore acoustic manifestations of tonal 
contrasts. 
2. Methods 
Eight speakers, four male and four female, from Tongta Village, 
Mengdong Yao Ethnic Township, Malipo County, Wenshan 
Zhuang and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, 
China, provided the speech data. They are all native Landian 
Yao and refer to themselves as /ki:m21mun21/. 64 monosyllabic 
words were used as test words and two repetitions were 
recorded, resulting in a total of 1,152 samples. The 16-bit audio 
speech was recorded into a Lenovo laptop through a Samson 
C01U Pro external condenser microphone at a sampling rate of 
44,100 Hertz.  
The rhyme was annotated as the tone-bearing unit in Praat 
6.4.01 [9]. 11 equidistant points of fundamental frequency (F0) 
data were extracted from the tone-bearing unit, and the duration 
of the tone-bearing unit was measured. The F0 data were 
normalized using Log-Z-score and duration data were 
normalized using Z-score. 
Growth Curve Analysis (GCA), a method within Linear 
Mixed-Effects Models (LMM), was employed to compare 
similarities and differences between F0 points along with their 
temporal changes [10]. This method has proven effective in the 
studies on complex Chinese tonal systems [11, 12]. The lme4 
package [13] in R [14] and R Studio [15] was used to build 
LMM models, where LZ values of the transformed pitch (F0) 
served as the dependent variable, and time (normalized), tone, 
gender, speaker, vowel type and coda type served as 
independent variables. After multiple fittings by adding or 
removing independent variables, the following model was 
chosen as the most suitable statistical model for the current 
study: 
Pitch (LZ value) ~ (Time + Time2) * Tone + Vowel type + (1 | Speaker) (1)

This model performed best in terms of Akaike Information 
Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), log-
likelihood ratio (logLik), and deviance, with chi-square test 
results supporting its significant superiority over other models 
(p<0.05). The model uses a quadratic orthogonal polynomial fit, 
with tonal and vowel types as fixed variables and speakers as 
random variables. 
After modeling these tones with quadratic orthogonal 
polynomials through GCA, three coefficients related to the 
pitch curve can be obtained: the intercept represents the overall 
pitch level of each tonal category, the linear coefficient of time 
in fixed effects represents the slope, and the quadratic 
coefficient of time represents the curvature. Therefore, to 
compare the similarities and differences in F0 points among 
these tones, we compared the differences in these three 
coefficients across different tone categories by obtaining 
Estimated Marginal Means (EMM) implemented in R through 
the emmeans package [16]. 
In addition, Linear Mixed-Effects Models were used to 
model the durational data of tones, with the final model being: 
Duration (Z value) ~ Tone + Codas type + (1 | Speaker)       （2） 
3. Results 
3.1. Pitch and Tonal Patterns 
As transcribed in Table 1, the 13 tones in the Malipo variety 
distinguish in 5 contour patterns: level, rising, falling, rise-fall, 
and fall-rise. Similar contours were compared below to examine 
how they are further differentiated from each other. 
3.1.1. Level Tones 
The Malipo variety has five level tones: T3’, T6, T7b, T7’b, and 
T8. Figure 1 shows the pitch curves of these level tones under 
two different y-axis scales. The left graph has a y-axis range 
defined by the maximum and minimum LZ values from all 
tones, representing the tonal range of the Malipo variety. And 
all the five level tones cluster in the mid region of the tonal 
space. The right graph magnifies the y-axis. And the 
distinctions between the five level tones are better manifested. 
 
 
Figure 1: The five level tones in the Malipo variety. 
The X-axis represents normalized equal time intervals, 
and the Y-axis represents LZ values. Each pitch curve 
is composed of 11 pitch points, each point 
representing the mean LZ values of the speakers with 
the shaded area representing one standard deviation

Figure 2 shows the EMM values of the five level tones in 
terms of intercept, slope, and quadratic following GCA in upper 
panel. And it also shows the results of Tukey’s post-hoc tests in 
lower panel. First, two groups of tones, T7’b-T7b and T7’b-T8, 
distinguish in intercept. That is, the tones with the lowest 
intercept values, T7b and T8, differ significantly from the tone 
with the highest intercept value, T7’b. Second, four groups of 
tones distinguish in slope, T3’-T7’b, T3’-T8, T6-T7’b and T6-
T8. That is, tones with lower slope values, T6 and T3’, differ 
significantly from those with higher slope values, T7’b and T8. 
Third, two groups of tones distinguish in quadratic, T3’-T7b 
and T6-T7b. That is, the tone with the lowest quadratic value, 
T7b, differs significantly from those with the highest values, T6 
and T3’.

3.1.3. Rising Tones and Fall-Rise Tones 
Figure 5 compares the rising tone T5, and the two fall-rise tones 
T1 and T7a together. The EMM results in Figure 6 show that 
T5 is significantly different from T1 and T7a in terms of 
intercept, suggesting that T5 has a higher pitch level, while T1 
and T7a do not differ in pitch level. Nevertheless, the three 
tones differ significantly in slope and quadratic, and the 
differences between T5 and the other two tones are more 
pronounced. It is thus reasonable to treat T5 as a distinctive high 
rising tone. As for T1 and T7a, the two low concave tones 
manifest significant difference in contour pattern, namely 213 
versus 212.

Figure 7 shows that the two rise-fall tones in the Malipo variety, 
T3 and T5’, are located in the mid-high and mid-low regions of 
the tonal space, respectively. The EMM results in Figure 8 
show that the two rise-fall tones have a significant difference in 
intercept, with T5’ being higher than T3. But they do not have 
significant differences in slope and quadratic, indicating that 
they share a similar contour pattern of convexity.

4. Conclusions 
The Malipo variety has an extremely complex tonal system with 
13 tones. The application of GCA enables us to examine tonal 
complexity from three perspectives: pitch, slope, and curvature. 
GCA successfully characterizes the 5 level tones, 3 falling tones, 
3 rising or concave tones, and 2 convex tones in the Malipo 
variety, even though the differences between some tones are 
subtle. The most intriguing cases concern with the differences 
between the two concave tones and among the five level tones. 
The two concave tones differ in concavity only, and the five 
level tones first differ in duration and then further differ in pitch 
and/or contour within unchecked and checked groups 
respectively. 
It should be admitted that the current study is solely based 
on F0 and durational data of tones. Further research is necessary 
to explore other aspects on tones. Complex tonal systems may 
rely on acoustic dimensions beyond F0 [17]. Therefore, it 
would be beneficial to examine whether phonation plays a role 
in differentiating tones, particularly those with subtle F0 
differences. In addition to acoustic studies, perception studies 
are also required to determine whether the tones with subtle 
acoustic differences show a tendency to merge.
